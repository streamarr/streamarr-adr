= ADR 0050: Outside Original, the Viewer's Limits Bind Copies as Well as Encodes, and Each Quality Tier Is a Picture Box in Either Orientation

== Status

Accepted

link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc[ADR 0049] decides whether a capability entry accepts an output.
This decision covers the other bound on an output: the viewer's playback choice and the viewer's limits.
It decides the questions that a review of https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/395[streamarr/streamarr-server#395] raised.
It also decides when the server lowers the audio under the viewer's maximum bitrate.
The server lowers it when the audio leaves no room for the smallest quality tier or for a video copy, and when lower multichannel audio lets a larger picture fit.

An amendment makes every audio encode name its output channel layout instead of a bare channel count (<<decision-channel-layout>>).
A track keeps 3 or 4 channels only in a layout that the target codec carries exactly, and otherwise encodes in stereo.
The rule covers the lower audio steps that keep a copied track's 3 or 4 channels and the 3- and 4-channel encodes that a capability entry's channel maximum produces.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/711[streamarr/streamarr-server#711] implements the copy side.
The layout comes from probe version 7 (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032]).

Another amendment replaces FFmpeg's default downmix with a named matrix and a limiter (<<decision-downmix>>).
Dialogue keeps ITU-R BS.775's −3 dB in every codec, or the level that an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track signals, and a `2.1` or `3.1` track keeps its low-frequency channel.
A limiter holds the channels that a downmix sums at its ceiling before encoding.

A later amendment follows an audiophile review of these rules.
An encode that only renames a surround pair is not a downmix, so it writes every sample unchanged.
A downmix that only drops channels has no limiter, and otherwise the limiter acts only on the channels that the matrix sums.
Every limited downmix uses a two-stage true-peak limiter at the source's own sample rate: −1 dBTP when FLAC or ALAC writes the downmix, and −3 dBTP for a lossy codec.
The stereo and mono matrix of an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track uses the mix levels that the track signals.
An AAC encode has at most six channels, and the rule names each encoder's own layouts of 5 to 8 channels (<<decision-channel-layout>>).

A further amendment settles a `7.0` track.
When an Opus or FLAC capability entry accepts 8 or more channels, the encode pads the track to `7.1` with a silent low-frequency channel, and every channel keeps its place.
Wherever the track cannot keep its seven channels, it encodes in 5.0 and invents no low-frequency channel (<<decision-channel-layout>>).

The same amendment takes the audio step from the largest audio rendition instead of the default track (<<decision-audio-steps>>).
It also raises the mono step to AAC at 96 kb/s and removes the stereo AAC step at 64 kb/s, so the lowest stereo step is 96 kb/s.
The stream session's audio bitrate already counts that rendition under link:0054-workers-measure-each-stream-bitrate-and-a-copy-advertises-its-measured-peak.adoc[ADR 0054], so no other track can take the stream over the viewer's maximum bitrate.

A later amendment keeps the −3 dBTP ceiling for stereo AAC and Opus downmixes after a wider search (<<decision-downmix>>).
A few synthetic full-scale pink-noise inputs still decode up to +1.43 dBTP between samples.
The worst of them come in the first AAC frame after an encoder starts.
No film soundtrack measured decoded above 0 dBTP.
A stereo track, such as stereo music, needs no downmix, so the limiter never touches it.

The same amendment decides two questions about Auto beside a copy.
When Auto copies the video, the playlist also lists encoded lower variants, and each lower variant encodes only when the player switches to it (<<decision-lower-variants>>).
The server does not tell a viewer at home from a viewer away by IP address, and the player's own variant switching adapts each viewer's quality (<<decision-no-network-classes>>).
A later amendment caps the Apple client's Auto only in Low Data Mode, not on every expensive path (<<decision-no-network-classes>>).

Each encoded lower variant beside a copy then forces its keyframes at the copy's own segment starts (<<decision-lower-variants>>).
The amendment ends this decision's deferral of that alignment.
The server lists encoded lower variants beside a copy only once each job attempt carries those exact starts (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/780[streamarr/streamarr-server#780]).

A ServerAdmin enters the server's internet upload speed, and counted stream sessions may use 80% of it together.
When the server serves a multivariant playlist, the budget leaves out each variant that would take the counted stream sessions over that share, and it always keeps the lowest variant.
It never refuses playback, never changes a direct play and never turns a copy into an encode.
When it leaves out the first-listed variant, the server starts the top variant that stays as it serves the playlist.
The client attests the network route of each playback session, and a stream session on the local network never counts (<<decision-network-route>>).
The server still never classifies a viewer by IP address.

== Context

The viewer picks a playback choice when a stream session starts.
Original is the playback choice that plays the source at its own quality (link:0045-clients-direct-play-covered-files-and-use-platform-players.adoc[ADR 0045]).
Auto is the playback choice in which the player adapts quality to bandwidth across the variants.
A fixed quality is a playback choice that names one quality, such as 720p, and keeps it (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036]).

A variant is one option of the quality ladder that the multivariant playlist lists (link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044]).
A copy is a stream copy, which passes the source video through unchanged.
An encode is a variant that the worker encodes.
Auto's encoded ladder offers one variant for each quality tier that applies, such as 720p or 1080p.

A client can send three limits when it starts a stream session: a maximum picture width, a maximum picture height and a maximum bitrate.
A client sends them, for example, to fit a small window or a slow connection.

Before this decision, the server applied these rules:

* The viewer's limits bounded encodes only.
When the client's capability entries accepted the source, the server copied the source even when the source was larger than the viewer's limits.
* The ADRs did not say whether the viewer's limits bound an encode in Original.
* The quality ladder keyed each tier on picture height.
A 1920×800 film therefore lost its 1080p tier, because the film is only 800 lines tall.
Its top variant was a 720p tier at 1728×720.
* A 1080×1920 portrait video received a 1080p tier at 608×1080.
* Auto offered no tier above 1080p, even for a 4K source on a 4K display.
* Each tier had one nominal bitrate for every codec family.
* The ladder labelled a tier's variant by its tier, even when a capability entry had shrunk the picture well below the tier's box.
* The server compared the maximum bitrate with a variant's video bitrate alone.
The audio that the player downloads beside the video was not counted.
* A fixed quality ignored its own resolution.
A fixed 480p on a 4K source produced a 4K encode, limited only by the client's capability entries.
* A fixed quality encoded at the source's bitrate, not at its tier's bitrate.

link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc[ADR 0043] makes audio one HLS rendition that every variant shares.
A rendition is an alternate HLS track, such as one audio language.
A stream session has one audio codec, and the default audio track decides it.
Each `EXT-X-STREAM-INF` tag's `BANDWIDTH` attribute counts the largest audio rendition.

The server encodes 5.1 audio at up to 768 kb/s, and a copied audio track can be larger.
The first version of this decision let the stream exceed the maximum bitrate when the audio left less than the 360p tier's video bitrate.
The stream then exceeded the maximum by the whole audio bitrate.
With 5.1 audio at 768 kb/s and the 360p tier's video bitrate of 800 kb/s, the 360p tier needs a maximum bitrate of at least 1,568 kb/s.
The first version also checked a video copy against the source's container bitrate.
That bitrate counts the source's own audio, not the audio that the session delivers.
A copy whose session audio is larger than the source's audio could therefore exceed the maximum bitrate.

Audio codecs have known working bitrates:

* Item 2.9 of Apple's HLS Authoring Specification recommends 384 kb/s for Dolby Digital (AC-3) 5.1, 320 kb/s for AAC 5.1, and 32 to 160 kb/s for stereo AAC.
* Item 2.9 also recommends 192 kb/s for Dolby Digital Plus (E-AC-3) 5.1.
That rate relies on E-AC-3's spectral extension and adaptive hybrid transform.
FFmpeg's E-AC-3 encoder turns both tools off, so FFmpeg's E-AC-3 needs about AC-3's bitrate for the same quality.
* AC-3's bitrate table in ATSC A/52 includes 384, 448 and 640 kb/s.
DVD-Video carries AC-3 5.1 at up to 448 kb/s, and Blu-ray at up to 640 kb/s.
* FFmpeg's own AAC encoder writes AAC-LC only.
Below about 64 kb/s, stereo audio needs HE-AAC, and FFmpeg's only HE-AAC encoder is non-free.
* The lowest row of Apple's H.264 ladder is 145 kb/s.

== Decision

[[decision-terms]]
=== The viewer's limits are the fixed quality's picture box and the limits the client sends

The viewer's limits are the limits that bound a stream session's output apart from the client capability declaration.
They are:

* the picture box of the fixed quality that the viewer picked, if any (<<decision-fixed-quality>>);
* the maximum picture width that the client sends;
* the maximum picture height that the client sends;
* the maximum bitrate that the client sends.

A picture box is a width and a height that bound a picture in either orientation.
A picture fits a picture box when its longer side is at or below the box's longer side and its shorter side is at or below the box's shorter side.
ADR 0049 bounds a capability entry's picture size the same way.

The maximum picture width and the maximum picture height apply as the client gives them.
The width limit bounds the picture's width, and the height limit bounds its height.
They are not turned for a portrait picture.

*Why:* A capability entry describes a decoder, and a decoder's work does not depend on orientation.
A client sends its width and height limits for a window or a screen, which has an orientation.
A client that wants one limit for both orientations sends the same value for both.

[[decision-copies]]
=== Outside Original, the server copies the video only when the source fits the viewer's limits

In Auto and in a fixed quality, the server chooses a video copy only when the source fits every one of the viewer's limits.
The source fits the fixed quality's picture box in either orientation.
The source's width is at or below the maximum width, and its height is at or below the maximum height.
The copy's cost is at or below the maximum bitrate.

A video copy costs the copied video's bitrate plus the stream session's audio bitrate.
That is the same cost as a variant's (<<decision-bitrate>>).
When the probe knows the copied video's bitrate but not a copied audio track's, the copy costs the larger of that sum and the container bitrate.

When the probe does not know the copied video's bitrate, the container bitrate stands in for the video.
The container bitrate is the bitrate that the probe reports for the whole source file.
When every audio rendition is a copy of a source track, the video copy costs the container bitrate alone.
The container already holds those tracks.
When any audio rendition is an encode, the video copy costs the container bitrate plus the bitrate of the largest encoded rendition.

When the copy's cost is over the maximum bitrate, the server lowers the audio first (<<decision-audio-steps>>).
It takes the first audio step at which the copy's cost is at or below the maximum bitrate.
The video copy then keeps the source's full quality.
The server encodes the video only when no audio step fits.
That encode then takes its audio step by the rules for an encode.
For example, a copied video at 5,000 kb/s with E-AC-3 5.1 at 768 kb/s, under a maximum bitrate of 5,500 kb/s, keeps the copy with E-AC-3 5.1 at 448 kb/s.

When the source exceeds the picture box, the maximum width or the maximum height, the server encodes the video instead.
The encode follows the viewer's limits and one capability entry, as every encode does.

In Original, a copy ignores the viewer's limits.
The server still copies a source that exceeds them.
The server does not lower the audio beside that copy.

When Original must encode, the encode follows the viewer's limits, as an encode in Auto does.
Original must encode when no capability entry accepts a copy of the source.
That encode plays in Auto, as link:0052-a-failed-hls-copy-falls-back-to-an-encode-and-original-changes-to-auto.adoc[ADR 0052] decides, so Auto's rules apply to the encode.
The maximum width and the maximum height bound the encode as the client gives them.
The maximum bitrate, less the audio bitrate, bounds the encode's video (<<decision-bitrate>>).
Original has no fixed quality, so no picture box bounds that encode.

A source that the server cannot encode still copies when a capability entry accepts the copy.
It copies even when it exceeds the viewer's limits.
A source cannot be encoded when it has no usable output frame rate (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/446[streamarr/streamarr-server#446]).
It also cannot be encoded when its encoded picture would have no width or no height, such as a picture 1 pixel tall.

A source also copies over the viewer's limits when a capability entry accepts the copy but the server has nothing to encode it to.
The server has nothing to encode to when no capability entry accepts any encode that the server makes of the source.
It also has nothing to encode to when workers are connected but none has a worker-verified encoder for a codec that a capability entry accepts an encode in.
A busy worker's encoders count.
Every worker includes an encoder for each codec that the server encodes, so a connected worker without one is faulty.
The server reports the fault to whoever runs it, so that the worker can be fixed.
When no worker is connected, or when every worker with a fitting encoder is busy, the stream session fails with an answer that the client can retry.
A busy worker or a missing connection never makes the server copy over the viewer's limits.

Beside either copy, the server still takes the first audio step at which the copy's cost fits.
When no step fits, the session's audio stays as decided.
A lower step would still leave the copy over the limits, and an audio encode needs a worker's capacity that a copied track does not.
These rules hold in Auto and in a fixed quality.

A source whose copied video's bitrate and container bitrate are both unknown passes the bitrate check.
Its picture size still has to fit the viewer's other limits.

*Why:* A viewer sets a limit to get a smaller or cheaper stream.
A copy that ignores the limit gives the viewer the stream they asked to avoid.
The player downloads the session's audio beside the copied video, not the source's own audio.
An encoded audio track can be larger than the source's audio, so the container bitrate alone can miss the overshoot.
A video copy keeps the source's full picture, so lower audio costs the viewer less than an encode of the video.
The container bitrate holds the video and every copied track, so it never counts less than they do.
Original promises the source's own quality, and the server never silently replaces a manual choice (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013]).
A viewer who wants their limits honored picks Auto or a fixed quality.
An encode that Original reaches already gives up the source's own quality.
Nothing that Original promises is then left to protect, so the encode honors the limits as Auto does.
A stream over the viewer's limits is better than no stream at all.
A client that accepts a copy but no encode of the source has nothing else to play, just as a source that cannot be encoded has nothing else.
A connected worker without an encoder for an accepted codec stays broken until it is fixed, so the viewer gets the copy rather than a refusal that blames their device.
Reporting the fault tells whoever runs the server what to fix.
Which workers are free or connected changes from moment to moment, so a busy or missing worker gets an answer that the client can retry and never trades the viewer's limits for a stream.
Refusing a copy for an unknown bitrate would encode many files that the client plays as they are.

[[decision-lower-variants]]
=== In Auto, encoded lower variants sit beside a copy

When the server copies the video in Auto, the multivariant playlist lists the copy and the encoded variants of Auto's ladder below the copy's picture.
The copy is the first-listed variant, so creating the stream session starts only the copy.
link:0008-server-generated-hls-playlists.adoc[ADR 0008]'s rule that lists the variant nearest 2000 kb/s first applies to a ladder of encodes.
Each lower variant encodes only when the player switches to it, as link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019] starts a variant on demand.
When the upload budget leaves the copy out of the playlist, the top lower variant that stays starts when the server serves the playlist instead (<<decision-upload-budget>>).
The headroom model of link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] never starts a lower variant beside a copy early, and never keeps one running as an extra variant.
When the player switches back to the copy, the lower variant that the player leaves stops once the copy delivers a media segment, as ADR 0019 stops a left variant.
While the player stays on the copy, no lower variant runs.
Each encoded variant forces its keyframes at the starts of the stream session's shared segment list, as link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] requires for every stream session with a copied variant.
Every variant therefore lists the same segments, and every segment of every variant starts with a keyframe, so the player can switch between the copy and any lower variant at a segment boundary.
Under ADR 0037, segment `N` of that list starts at the source keyframe that opens the copy's segment `N`.
A lower variant forces its keyframe on the first output frame at or after that start, so its segment `N` starts less than one output frame after the copy's segment `N`.
The server lists encoded lower variants beside a copy only once each job attempt carries those exact starts.
With whole slots, a lower variant's segment `N` would start up to one slot before the copy's segment `N`, and a switch would repeat or skip that media.

*Why:* A copy alone gives the player no lower variant when the viewer's bandwidth drops, so playback stalls.
The viewer would then have to pick a fixed quality by hand.
With lower variants listed, the player's own variant switching moves down and back up by itself.
A lower variant that the player never requests costs no worker capacity.
The copy starts without an encoder, so listing it first does not delay the start.
Variants without the shared segment list would make a switch fall inside a segment.

[[decision-no-network-classes]]
=== The server does not tell a viewer at home from a viewer away

The server never classifies a viewer as at home or away by the viewer's IP address.
The server has no separate quality limits for viewers at home and viewers away.
Only the optional upload budget treats them apart, by the network route that the client attests (<<decision-network-route>>).
The player's own variant switching adapts each viewer's quality to the bandwidth that the viewer's own connection gives (<<decision-lower-variants>>).
The Apple client caps Auto only in Low Data Mode, when the system reports the network path as constrained (`NWPath.isConstrained`).
The cap is the 720p quality tier's picture box and 4 Mb/s for video plus audio, sent as the viewer's limits when a stream session starts.
An expensive path alone, such as cellular or a hotspot (`NWPath.isExpensive`), leaves Auto uncapped: many viewers have unlimited data, and the player's own variant switching already adapts to the connection.
Original and fixed qualities are never capped.
A ServerAdmin may set a total upload budget for the server.
That budget is optional and off by default (<<decision-upload-budget>>).

*Why:* An IP address does not show how fast a viewer's path is.
A viewer at home can have weak Wi-Fi, and a viewer away can have a fast fibre connection.
A VPN, a reverse proxy or carrier-grade NAT also hides where a viewer is.
Variant switching measures each viewer's own path while the viewer plays.
Only the device knows that its connection is metered, so the Apple client caps its own quality there.

[[decision-upload-budget]]
=== An optional upload budget leaves variants out of a playlist, and never blocks playback

A ServerAdmin may enter the server's internet upload speed as a speed test reports it, in whole megabits per second from 1 to 10,000.
The upload budget is off until a ServerAdmin enters a speed, and clearing the speed turns it off again.
The stream sessions that count against the budget may use 80% of that speed together.
That 80% is the streams' share.

A stream session counts at the average bitrate of what it sends, at the variant that its player last requested:

* a copy at its measured average (link:0054-workers-measure-each-stream-bitrate-and-a-copy-advertises-its-measured-peak.adoc[ADR 0054]), or at its probed average while the file has no measurement;
* an encoded variant at its target average;
* a direct play at its measured average once the file's measurements (ADR 0054) cover every video stream of the file, and at its probed container bitrate otherwise.

Before its player requests a media segment, a stream session counts at its first-listed variant.
The headroom that a variant's BANDWIDTH attribute adds above its average does not count.
A suspended stream session counts nothing.
A stream session on the local network never counts (<<decision-network-route>>).

When the server serves a counted stream session's multivariant playlist, it leaves out each variant whose average bitrate would take the counted stream sessions over the streams' share.
The lowest variant always stays.
When no variant that stays runs or can start now, because no configured transcode slot or worker job slot is free for it, the playlist stays whole.
The check runs each time the server serves the playlist and reserves nothing, so the budget is a soft cap.

The budget never refuses playback.
It never changes or blocks a direct play, never turns a copy into an encode and never changes any other part of the stream decision.
It only leaves variants out of an HLS multivariant playlist.

When the budget leaves out the first-listed variant, creating the stream session starts no variant.
Each time the server serves a playlist that leaves out the first-listed variant, and no job attempt of a variant that stays runs, it starts the top variant that stays at the current segment before it returns the playlist.
That variant then holds its place under the transcode limit, so another stream session cannot take the last place before the player requests a variant that stays.
This also covers a playlist that the player fetches again after the budget has left out the copy that it plays.
When that variant cannot start, the playlist stays whole and lists the first-listed variant again.
The playlist also stays whole when the top variant that stays has a job attempt that does not run, such as a suspended one: that job attempt holds no place, and recovery rather than a start replaces it.
The player can choose another variant that stays.
Until the player requests a media segment of a variant other than the one it plays, a request of another variant, an initialization request included, stops the variant that the playlist started when the stop frees the place that the start lacks, and tries the start or the replacement once more.
It stops no other variant.
A media-segment request for the variant that the player plays keeps the variant that the playlist started running, because the player may have sent it before it read that playlist.
Delivering a media segment that the player requested before that playlist keeps it running too.
When the budget leaves out no variant, variants start as link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc#decision-requested-variant[ADR 0019] decides.

*Why:* Every stream over the internet shares the server's one upload.
When the streams together ask for more than it carries, every one of them stalls, and each player's variant switching sees only its own path.
Leaving variants out moves a new stream session to a variant that fits, and the decisions of this ADR and of link:0045-clients-direct-play-covered-files-and-use-platform-players.adoc[ADR 0045] stay as they are.
The lowest variant stays, so a player always has a variant to play.
A playlist with only variants that cannot start would leave the player nothing that plays, while the copy needs no transcode place.
Averages, not peaks, are counted, because the peaks of several streams rarely come at once, and the 20% that the share leaves out absorbs them.
A speed test reports the upload that a ServerAdmin can check, so the setting takes that value.

A player starts with the first variant that the playlist lists.
When the budget leaves out the first-listed variant, the top variant that stays is the variant that the player requests first.
On demand, that variant would start only at the player's first request.
Another stream session could take the last transcode place between the playlist and that request, and the request would then fail with `503 Service Unavailable`, although the copy would have played.
Starting the variant as the server serves the playlist closes that gap, as starting the first-listed variant at creation does under ADR 0019.
The budget decides the playlist when the server serves it, so the start waits until then.

[[decision-network-route]]
=== The client attests the network route, and the server never classifies by IP address

`createPlaybackSession` requires the network route: `LOCAL_NETWORK` or `INTERNET`.
The client sends `LOCAL_NETWORK` when it reached the server through one of these:

* a private IPv4 address (RFC 1918);
* an IPv4 or IPv6 link-local address;
* an IPv6 unique local address in `fd00::/8`;
* a `.local` name, or a `.home.arpa` name (RFC 8375);
* local discovery.

The client sends `INTERNET` for every other route.
That includes loopback, public host names, tunnels, carrier-grade NAT in `100.64.0.0/10` including Tailscale, Tailscale's IPv6 prefix `fd7a:115c:a1e0::/48`, hairpin NAT through a public name, and a single-label host name, one with no dot that is not an IP literal.
Every format attempt of the playback session keeps the route.

A stream session on the local network never counts against the upload budget, and the budget leaves its playlist whole.
A false `LOCAL_NETWORK` claim only takes that stream session out of the budget.
It grants no access: authorization, playback tokens and the transcode limit ignore the route.

*Why:* The client knows the address that it reached the server at.
The server sees only the peer address, which a reverse proxy, a tunnel or hairpin NAT hides.
Loopback counts as the internet because a tunnel can forward a loopback port.
RFC 8375 reserves `.home.arpa` for home networks, and the public DNS never answers for it.
A resolver expands a single-label name with the DNS search domains, so the name reaches whatever host the first search domain that answers points to.
Tailscale's MagicDNS adds the tailnet's search domain, so a bare machine name reaches the server over Tailscale, which this decision keeps on the internet.
A work or ISP network can also hand out a public search domain.
A browser does not tell the client which address a name resolved to.

[[decision-tiers]]
=== Each quality tier is a 16:9 picture box in either orientation

A quality tier is a named picture box that the quality ladder offers.
The quality tiers and their boxes are:

[cols="1,1"]
|===
|Quality tier |Picture box

|360p |640×360
|480p |854×480
|720p |1280×720
|1080p |1920×1080
|1440p |2560×1440
|2160p |3840×2160
|===

A tier's variant is the source scaled to fit the tier's box, keeping the source's aspect ratio.
The server never upscales a tier's variant.
The server offers a tier only when the source is larger than the next smaller tier's box.
A source is larger than a box when the source does not fit the box.
A 1920×800 film therefore gets a 1080p tier at 1920×800, because the film does not fit the 720p box.
The film gets no 1440p tier, because it fits the 1080p box.
A 1080×1920 portrait video gets a 1080p tier at 1080×1920.

A tier never crops, pads or stretches the source.
A scope film, about 2.4:1, takes its tier box's full width at a shorter height.
The widest tier box is 2160p's, at 3840 wide.
An encode of a source wider than 3840 is therefore 3840 wide.
A copy keeps the source's size.
A future client with an ultrawide screen would get a wider encode from a wider tier box.

Auto offers the 1440p and 2160p tiers when the source, the viewer's limits and one capability entry allow them.
ADR 0044 still decides when each offered variant starts.

A capability entry can shrink a tier's variant into a smaller tier's box (ADR 0049).
That tier then gives way to the smaller tier, and the ladder does not offer it.
When two variants would take the same label (<<decision-labels>>), the ladder keeps the variant that reaches its own tier's box.
It drops the higher tier's shrunken variant.

Each tier has a nominal video bitrate for each codec family.
This decision sets them for AV1 and H.264.
From 360p to 1080p, both codec families keep the tier's existing bitrate.
At 1440p and 2160p, the AV1 bitrates follow the HEVC ladder in item 1.25 of Apple's HLS Authoring Specification.
H.264 needs more bitrate than AV1 for the same picture quality.
Its 1440p and 2160p bitrates are therefore higher than AV1's.
This ADR names no bitrate values.
The server's tier definitions hold them.

*Why:* A tier keyed on height shrinks a widescreen film below the size its name promises.
Widescreen films are a large share of a film library.
A future desktop app for a home theater must show a scope film as it is.
Cropping, padding or stretching would change the picture that the film's makers chose.
A tier keyed on height also makes a portrait video's tier far narrower than its box.
A box bounds both sides, so a 4:3 source never exceeds the tier's height and a wide source keeps the tier's width.
A 4K display in Auto should receive a 4K encode when the source and the client allow it.
AV1 compresses at least as well as HEVC, so Apple's HEVC rows suit AV1.
Apple's H.264 ladder stops at 1080p and gives no value for the larger tiers.
The lower tiers keep their bitrates, so this decision changes no existing variant's bitrate.

[[decision-labels]]
=== A variant takes its tier's label only when its picture reaches the tier's box

A picture reaches a box when it is as large as fits.
It fits the box in either orientation, and the worker's next larger picture of the same source would not fit.
The worker gives every encoded picture an even width and an even height, so its next larger picture is two lines taller.
A picture that reaches its box can therefore fall a few pixels short of the box's side.
A variant whose picture reaches its tier's box takes the tier's label, such as 360p or 1080p.
Otherwise the server labels the variant by its shorter side followed by "p".

* A 1920×800 variant reaches the 1080p box, so its label is 1080p.
* A 1276×532 variant of a 2.4:1 film reaches the 720p box, so its label is 720p.
Its next larger picture is too wide for the box.
* A 360×640 portrait variant reaches the 360p box turned, so its label is 360p.
* A 1080p tier that a capability entry shrinks to 1600×900 does not reach the 1080p box, so its label is 900p.
* A 320×180 variant has the label 180p, and a 498×280 variant has the label 280p.

Labels name the variants of Auto's ladder only.
The rule applies to each of them, with or without a tier.
A variant without a tier takes the label of a tier whose box it reaches.
That includes Auto's single variant that the viewer's limits and a capability entry bound.
A source smaller than the 360p box therefore gets one variant in Auto, labelled by its shorter side.

A fixed quality's encode is a ladder of one variant.
That variant takes no label, and its media playlist keeps the path named "default".
An encode that Original reaches is Auto's ladder, so the labels of Auto's ladder apply to its variants (ADR 0052).

*Why:* Players show a variant's label to the viewer.
A label should tell the viewer the size that they get.
A widescreen film at 1920×800 has a 1080p film's full width, and viewers know it as 1080p.
Even sides keep a 2.4:1 film at 720p to 1276×532, but no larger picture of it fits the box.
A 1600×900 picture could grow and still fit the 1080p box, so calling it 1080p claims more than it holds.
A ladder of one variant offers the player no choice.
The viewer already picked the quality, so a label would tell them nothing.

[[decision-bitrate]]
=== The maximum bitrate bounds video plus audio

A variant costs its video bitrate plus the stream session's audio bitrate.
The audio bitrate is the bitrate of the largest audio rendition, as link:0054-workers-measure-each-stream-bitrate-and-a-copy-advertises-its-measured-peak.adoc[ADR 0054] counts it.
A copied audio track counts at the track's bitrate, and an encoded audio track at the encode's bitrate.
Every variant shares the same audio renditions (ADR 0043), so every variant adds the same audio bitrate.

The server offers a tier only when the tier's video bitrate for the chosen codec family plus the audio bitrate is at or below the maximum bitrate.

A FLAC or ALAC encode counts its measured bitrate, or 65 % of its PCM bitrate without a measurement (link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc[ADR 0043]).
`BANDWIDTH` counts the largest peak among the audio renditions (link:0054-workers-measure-each-stream-bitrate-and-a-copy-advertises-its-measured-peak.adoc[ADR 0054]).

A variant without a tier is the single variant that Auto offers when no tier fits.
A fixed quality also has a single variant.
For each of these variants, the video bitrate is at most the maximum bitrate minus the audio bitrate, apart from the 145 kb/s floor below.
When the probe knows the source's bitrate, the source's bitrate caps the video too.

Before the server sizes any variant, the server checks the audio against the 360p tier (<<decision-audio-steps>>).
When the audio leaves less than the 360p tier's video bitrate, the server lowers the audio.
When the remainder is still below the 360p tier's video bitrate, the server bounds the picture to the 360p box.
The video then gets that remainder, but never less than 145 kb/s.
A source whose known bitrate is lower than 145 kb/s still caps the video.

*Why:* The player downloads the audio rendition beside every variant.
A variant whose video alone fills the maximum bitrate exceeds the viewer's limit by the audio.
A copied surround track can take a large share of a low maximum bitrate.
An encoder treats a zero bitrate as no bitrate limit at all, so a zero or negative remainder would produce the largest stream instead of the smallest.
An encode never needs more bitrate than the source holds, so the server never asks for more.
145 kb/s is the lowest video bitrate in Apple's H.264 ladder.

[[decision-audio-steps]]
=== When the audio leaves no room for the 360p tier, the server lowers the audio instead of exceeding the maximum bitrate

An audio step is one audio codec, channel layout and bitrate that the server can deliver in place of the stream session's audio.
The server tries the audio steps from the first to the last:

. The stream session's audio, as the audio decision chooses it.
That audio is a copy at the track's bitrate or an encode at the codec's default bitrate, such as AC-3 5.1 at 640 kb/s, E-AC-3 5.1 at 768 kb/s or AAC 5.1 at 384 kb/s.
This step costs the largest audio rendition (<<decision-bitrate>>).
The track of that rendition is the step track.
When several renditions tie as the largest, the default track is the step track if its rendition is one of them, and otherwise the first of them in the file's stream order.
When the bitrate of a copied rendition is unknown, the server cannot rank it, so the default track is the step track, as before this amendment.
. The step that link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc[ADR 0043] adds when a FLAC or ALAC rendition runs above 48 kHz.
. The multichannel steps.
They keep the codec and the channel layout of the step track's encode: the encode that the session delivers for it, or, when the session copies it, the encode that the server would choose for it.
When that encode is FLAC, ALAC or Opus, which have no multichannel steps of their own, they keep the E-AC-3, AC-3 or AAC encode that the server would choose for the step track without it (ADR 0043).
When the session copies the step track in AAC, AC-3 or E-AC-3, which have multichannel steps of their own, the copy's codec adds its steps too.
Those steps keep the copy's channel count, up to the 5.1 that the server encodes in AAC, AC-3 and E-AC-3.
A copy of 5 channels steps in the encoder's 5.0 layout: `5.0` for AAC and `5.0(side)` for AC-3 and E-AC-3.
An AAC copy of 7 or 8 channels therefore steps in AAC 5.1, because an AAC encode has at most six channels, and an AAC `7.0` copy steps in AAC 5.0 (<<decision-channel-layout>>).
A copy of 3 or 4 channels keeps its channels only in a layout that its codec carries exactly (<<decision-channel-layout>>).
Otherwise the copy's codec adds no step for it.
The steps of both codecs then run from the highest bitrate to the lowest.
At an equal bitrate, the copy's codec comes first.
A copied codec without multichannel steps of its own, such as FLAC, Opus or ALAC, steps through the server's encode only.
A codec adds these steps only when its encode or copy has more than two channels:
** E-AC-3 5.1 at 640, 448 and 384 kb/s;
** AC-3 5.1 at 448 and 384 kb/s;
** for a copied AC-3 or E-AC-3 track of 3 to 5 channels that keeps a multichannel step, the same bitrates in the layout that the step names;
** multichannel AAC at 64 kb/s per full-band channel, which is 320 kb/s for 5.1 and 5.0, 256 kb/s for 4.0 and 192 kb/s for 3.0.
. The stereo steps: AAC stereo at 128 and 96 kb/s.
A mono track has one step instead: AAC mono at 96 kb/s.
So does any track when no AAC capability entry for HLS accepts more than one channel.

A step counts only when it is smaller than the steps before it.

A copied AAC 5.1 track at 384 kb/s therefore steps to AAC 5.1 at 320 kb/s before stereo, even when the server would encode E-AC-3.
E-AC-3's steps are not smaller than the copy.
A copied AC-3 5.1 track at 640 kb/s steps to AC-3 5.1 at 448 and 384 kb/s.
When the server would encode E-AC-3, none of E-AC-3's steps count.
Its 640 kb/s step is not smaller than the copy, and its 448 and 384 kb/s steps tie with AC-3's.
A copied AAC 7.1 track at 512 kb/s steps to AAC 5.1 at 320 kb/s, then stereo.
When the server would encode E-AC-3, E-AC-3 5.1 at 448 and 384 kb/s come before AAC 5.1.

When the maximum bitrate minus the session's audio bitrate is below the 360p tier's video bitrate, the server takes the first audio step at which the 360p tier's video bitrate fits.
That step's bitrate plus the 360p tier's video bitrate is at or below the maximum bitrate.
The rule holds in Auto and in a fixed quality.
An encode that Original reaches plays in Auto under ADR 0052, so the rule holds for that encode too.
When no step fits, the server takes the last step, and <<decision-bitrate>> lowers the video.
Beside a video copy, the copy's cost decides the step instead of the 360p tier (<<decision-copies>>).

With 5.1 E-AC-3 at 768 kb/s and the 360p tier's video bitrate of 800 kb/s:

* a maximum bitrate of 1,400 kb/s gives E-AC-3 5.1 at 448 kb/s;
* a maximum bitrate of 1,000 kb/s gives AAC stereo at 128 kb/s;
* a maximum bitrate of 600 kb/s gives AAC stereo at 96 kb/s and video at 504 kb/s in the 360p box;
* a maximum bitrate of 150 kb/s gives AAC stereo at 96 kb/s and video at 145 kb/s, which exceeds the maximum.

A multichannel step keeps the codec of an encode that the server would choose or of the copied track, and no more channels than that encode or track has, so a capability entry already accepts it.
A stereo or mono step needs AAC.
Every client capability declaration carries an AAC capability entry for HLS (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc[ADR 0049]), so a step always fits the declaration.
When the step changes the codec, the session's audio codec becomes the step's codec, and every audio rendition follows.

A copied audio track counts at its bitrate.
When the server takes a step below a copy, the copy becomes an encode at that step.
In Original, a video copy ignores the viewer's limits, so an audio copy beside it stays a copy.

The server takes a step only when the client sends a maximum bitrate.
The server takes one step for the whole stream session, from the step track.
A lower step costs its own bitrate, because it caps every rendition at that bitrate.
A step below the session's audio caps every audio rendition: every track is delivered in the step's codec, with no more than the step's channels and bitrate.
A track that the cap leaves at 3 or 4 channels follows <<decision-channel-layout>>.
A track that already fits the cap's codec, channels and bitrate keeps its own copy-or-encode decision.
A step never raises a track's channel count.
It raises a track's bitrate only when the track is below the lowest bitrate that the step's codec encodes for its channels, such as a 16 kb/s mono track under an AC-3 step, whose encoder starts at 32 kb/s.
That lowest bitrate is below every multichannel step's bitrate, so the cap still holds.
Switching audio tracks never changes the step.
Neither the server nor the player interrupts playback with a notice when the server lowers the audio.

*Why:* A viewer sets a maximum bitrate because the viewer's connection carries no more.
A stream over that limit stalls, so a lower audio bitrate serves the viewer better than the overshoot.
A short list of steps gives encoders bitrates that they handle well and keeps the rule simple to test.
AC-3's encoder rounds any other bitrate to its table anyway.
Surround at 384 kb/s with the 360p tier fits a maximum bitrate of 1,184 kb/s.
The steps therefore drop to stereo only when surround cannot fit beside the smallest tier or beside a video copy.
A copied AAC 5.1 track keeps surround one step longer, because AAC's 320 kb/s step is below the lowest E-AC-3 and AC-3 step of 384 kb/s.
A copied AAC 7.1 track steps through AAC 5.1, because an AAC encode has at most six channels, and AAC 5.1 needs no capability entry beyond the one that accepts AAC 7.1.
At an equal bitrate, the step keeps the session's codec.
384 kb/s is the lowest E-AC-3 step because FFmpeg's E-AC-3 encoder lacks the tools behind Apple's 192 kb/s.
Every AAC step is AAC-LC.
A Linux worker writes it with FFmpeg's own encoder.
A macOS worker may write it with `aac_at`, Apple's AAC encoder in AudioToolbox.
A variant keeps the AAC encoder of its first job attempt for every later job attempt.
Every running variant writes the same audio renditions (ADR 0043), so every job attempt of one stream session encodes AAC with the same encoder.
A worker uses `aac_at` only after a check shows that its audio initialization segments and its junctions between job attempts meet ADR 0043's rules, because `aac_at` starts its first frame at a different delay than FFmpeg's encoder.
No step uses HE-AAC, even on a macOS worker whose `aac_at` writes HE-AAC, so every worker can write every step.
Streamarr does not depend on `libfdk_aac`, which FFmpeg classes as non-free.
96 kb/s is the lowest stereo step and the mono step because FFmpeg's AAC encoder at 64 kb/s decoded downmixes limited to −3 dBTP above 0 dBTP (<<evidence-true-peak>>).
From that ceiling, stereo AAC at 64 kb/s decoded 3 of 32 pink-noise inputs above 0 dBTP, up to +0.37 dBTP.
Mono AAC at 64 kb/s decoded the loudest 60 s of Tears of Steel's mono downmix at +0.67 dBTP, with 14 samples at full scale, and at 96 kb/s at −1.67 dBTP.
Stereo AAC at 96 and 128 kb/s still decoded 2 and 1 of the 32 pink-noise inputs above 0 dBTP, and 4 and 5 of 128, as <<decision-downmix>> states.
`BANDWIDTH` counts the largest rendition, so only a cap on every rendition keeps every variant within the maximum.
The step comes from the largest rendition for the same reason: a step from the default track alone would leave a larger track over the maximum.
The session's own audio caps nothing, because it can be a lossless copy, such as FLAC, whose bitrate no encode can match.
ADR 0043 keeps one audio codec per stream session, so all tracks follow one step.
Audio renditions switch without restarting the video, and an unchanged step keeps that true.

[[decision-channel-layout]]
=== An audio encode names its output channel layout, and keeps 3 or 4 channels only in a layout that its codec carries exactly

Every audio encode names its output channel layout, not only a channel count.
The job carries the layout, and the worker encodes with FFmpeg's `-ch_layout` instead of `-ac`.
The server reads each source track's layout from probe version 7 (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032]).
A track whose stored outcome records no layout, or a layout that this section does not name, has an unknown layout.

An encode of 1 or 2 channels names the encoder's mono or stereo layout.
An encode of 5 to 8 channels names the encoder's own layout for that count:

[cols="2,1,1,1,1"]
|===
|Encoder |5 channels |6 channels |7 channels |8 channels

|AAC |`5.0` |`5.1` |– |–
|AC-3, E-AC-3 |`5.0(side)` |`5.1(side)` |– |–
|FLAC |`5.0(side)` |`5.1(side)` |`6.1` |`7.1`
|Opus |`5.0` |`5.1` |`6.1` |`7.1`
|ALAC |`5.0` |`5.1` |`6.1(back)` |`7.1(wide)`
|===

A side pair and a back pair count as one surround pair, so a `5.1(side)` track keeps its layout in AAC's `5.1` (<<decision-downmix>>).
An encode names a 7-channel layout only for a track whose layout that 7-channel layout carries, such as a `6.1` track in FLAC or Opus.
No encoder's 7-channel layout carries a `7.0` track, which has no low-frequency channel.
An Opus or FLAC encode of a `7.0` track pads the track to `7.1` with a silent low-frequency channel when that codec's capability entry has a channel maximum of 8 or more.
The pad keeps all seven channels in place.
Wherever a `7.0` track cannot keep its seven channels, it encodes in 5.0: in AAC, AC-3, E-AC-3 and ALAC, and in Opus or FLAC under a channel maximum below 8.
The 5.0 encode folds the track's four surrounds into one pair and adds no low-frequency channel (<<decision-downmix>>).
In Opus, any other 7-channel track encodes in 5.1, and an 8-channel track keeps Opus's `7.1` within the channel maximum.
An AAC encode has at most six channels.
Any other track of 7 or more channels therefore encodes as AAC 5.1, and a copied AAC 7.1 track stays a copy.
AC-3 and E-AC-3 encode at most 5.1.
A `6.1` track keeps its layout in FLAC, Opus and ALAC, and encodes as 5.1 in AAC, AC-3 and E-AC-3, with its back centre mixed into each surround at 0.7071 (<<decision-downmix>>).
ALAC's 8-channel layout, `7.1(wide)`, holds front-of-centre channels instead of a second surround pair, so ALAC does not carry 7.1.
link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc[ADR 0043] decides what a FLAC or ALAC encode does with a layout that its encoder does not write.

An encode keeps a track's own 3 or 4 channels only when the target codec carries the track's layout exactly:

* AAC carries `3.0` and `4.0`;
* AC-3 and E-AC-3 carry `2.1`, `3.0` and `4.0`, and carry `quad` and `quad(side)` as one 2/2 mode, which decoders report as `quad(side)`;
* Opus carries `3.0` and `quad`;
* FLAC carries `3.0` and `quad`, and ALAC carries `3.0` and `4.0`.

AC-3 and E-AC-3 do not carry `3.0(back)` or `3.1` until a check on hardware passes.
AVFoundation misplaced both: it played a `3.0(back)` track's right channel from the centre surround and lost its back centre, and it left a `3.1` track's centre and low-frequency channels silent.
Outside FLAC and ALAC, a `4.1` track has 5 channels, so it encodes in the encoder's 5.0 layout like any 5-channel track.
The encode names the track's own layout when the codec carries it.
A 3- or 4-channel track in any other layout, or in an unknown layout, encodes in stereo, apart from a FLAC or ALAC encode under ADR 0043.

The rule applies wherever an encode would have 3 or 4 channels:

* A lower audio step that keeps a copied 3- or 4-channel track's channels in the copy's codec (<<decision-audio-steps>>).
When a copied AAC `4.0` track at 384 kb/s leaves no room, it can step to AAC `4.0` at 256 kb/s.
A copied AAC `quad` track steps to stereo instead.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/711[streamarr/streamarr-server#711] implements this case.
* Another audio track that a step caps (<<decision-audio-steps>>).
When the step changes a 3- or 4-channel track's codec, the track keeps its layout only if the step's codec carries it, and encodes in stereo otherwise.
* An encode that a capability entry's channel maximum of 3 or 4 caps (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc[ADR 0049]).
A track with more channels than the maximum has no layout of its own at that count, so it encodes in stereo.
A 5.1 track under an AAC channel maximum of 4 therefore encodes AAC stereo, not AAC `4.0`.
* An encode that a channel maximum of 5 or 7 caps.
Under a maximum of 5, a track of more channels encodes in the encoder's 5.0 layout, which AAC carries as channel configuration 5 and AC-3 and E-AC-3 carry as mode 3/2.
Under a maximum of 7, a track of 8 channels encodes in 5.1, a `6.1` track keeps its layout in FLAC, Opus and ALAC, and a `7.0` track encodes in 5.0.
* An Opus or FLAC encode of a `7.0` track under that codec's channel maximum of 6 or 7 is 5.0, because that maximum leaves no room for the pad to `7.1`.

The server's own encode of a 3- or 4-channel track, outside these cases, stays stereo, as before this amendment.

*Why:* With `-ac`, FFmpeg ignores the source's layout and picks the encoder's default layout for the count.
For 1, 2, 5 and 6 channels, that default is the layout that this rule names anyway, and so is each encoder's layout for 7 and 8 channels in the table.
For 3 and 4 channels, the default can change what the viewer hears.
A `quad` source encoded to AAC with `-ac 4` became AAC `4.0`, which mixes the two rear channels into one back channel.
A rear tone in opposite phase fell from −27.1 dB to −96.8 dB, while the AAC stereo step kept it at −30.1 dB.
A `3.0` source encoded to AAC with `-ac 3` became AAC `2.1`.
FFmpeg writes AAC `2.1` and `quad` as channel configuration 0 with a program config element, and Chromium's AAC parser refuses channel configuration 0.
ISO/IEC 14496-3 gives AAC channel configurations for `3.0` (configuration 3) and `4.0` (configuration 4), and FFmpeg wrote those.
ATSC A/52's audio coding modes 2/0 with the low-frequency channel, 3/0 and 2/2 hold the 3- and 4-channel AC-3 and E-AC-3 layouts that the rule names.
A/52 has one 2/2 mode, so FFmpeg's AC-3 and E-AC-3 encoders wrote both `quad` and `quad(side)` as `quad(side)`, and AVFoundation played both from its side surrounds.
With the source's layout named, AAC `3.0` and AC-3 `quad` kept every channel at the source's level.
Stereo keeps every full-band channel of the source in the mix, so stereo is the safe output for a layout that the codec cannot carry.
FFmpeg's default downmix leaves out the low-frequency channel, so <<decision-downmix>> folds that channel into the stereo of a `2.1` track.
FFmpeg's 5-channel AAC layout, `5.0`, wrote channel configuration 5, but its 7-channel AAC layout wrote channel configuration 0.
Its 8-channel AAC layout, `7.1`, wrote channel configuration 7, which ISO/IEC 14496-3 defines as front-wide 7.1: centre, left and right of centre, left, right, the surround pair and the low-frequency channel.
AVFoundation played that file's side surrounds from the left and right speakers and its front pair from the front-of-centre positions.
FFmpeg's own decoder reads configuration 7 as `7.1`, so a check with FFmpeg alone misses the error.
Of the 7- and 8-channel layouts, FFmpeg's Opus and ALAC encoders accepted only those in the table.
`libopus` refused a `7.0` input outright, so an Opus encode of a `7.0` track must pad it or fold it.
No AAC, AC-3, E-AC-3 or ALAC layout holds a `7.0` track's seven channels either.
Padded to `7.1`, a synthetic `7.0` track kept each of its seven channels in place at the source's level, and it played that way in Chromium and Firefox (<<evidence-seven-zero>>).
The 5.0 fold invents no channel, where a 5.1 encode would add a low-frequency channel that the track lacks.
That holds in every codec, so every encode that cannot keep a `7.0` track's seven channels uses the same fold.
FFmpeg's FLAC encoder wrote every layout, but only the table's layouts follow FLAC's own channel order (ADR 0043).
A downmix of a 5.1 track to AAC's 4-channel default, `4.0`, merges its surround pair into one back channel in the same way.
ADR 0032's evidence holds the measurements (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#evidence-channel-layout[ADR 0032]).

[[decision-downmix]]
=== Every downmix uses a named matrix, and a limiter keeps the channels that it sums below their ceiling

A downmix is an audio encode whose output layout lacks a channel of the source track's layout, so that the encode mixes source channels together or leaves one out.
A side pair and a back pair are one surround pair.
An encode that only renames that pair, such as `5.1(side)` to `5.1` or `6.1` to `6.1(back)`, is not a downmix.
It writes each channel unchanged, with no matrix and no limiter.
An Opus encode that pads a `7.0` track to `7.1` is not a downmix either.
It loses no channel.
The pad keeps every source channel in place and adds a silent low-frequency channel, so it runs no downmix matrix and no limiter.
Like ADR 0043's FLAC and ALAC pads, it uses `pan` with a gain of 0 for the added channel.
An encode that writes every decoded sample unchanged runs no filter except sample-format packing.

Every downmix uses the matrix below instead of FFmpeg's default.
That includes the stereo and mono steps, the stereo encode of a 3- or 4-channel layout that the target codec cannot carry, and every encode that a channel maximum caps.
It also includes an encode to 5.1 of a 7.1 or `6.1` track, such as AC-3 or E-AC-3 5.1 or the AAC 5.1 step, an encode to 5.0 under a channel maximum of 5, an encode of a `7.0` track to 5.0, and an encode of the same channel count that changes the layout, such as `6.0` to 5.1.
The worker converts the decoded audio to floating point (`aformat=sample_fmts=fltp`), applies the matrix with FFmpeg's `pan` filter, and then limits the channels that the matrix sums, before the encoder.
A downmix of an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track mixes the samples of ADR 0043's decode, which applies no dynamic range compression and applies the track's dialogue normalization, as a hardware decoder does for the copy.

The stereo matrix follows ITU-R BS.775's 2/0 equations at ATSC A/52's default levels:

* Left = FL + 0.7071 × FC + 0.7071 × LS, and right = FR + 0.7071 × FC + 0.7071 × RS.
* LS and RS are the source's surrounds on each side.
A source with one surround pair, side or back, uses that pair.
A source with both pairs, such as 7.1, mixes each side's two surrounds at 0.7071 each.
A back centre mixes into LS and RS at 0.7071.
* The low-frequency channel mixes into left and right at 0.7071 only when the source has no surround channel, as in `2.1` and `3.1`.
A source with surrounds, such as 5.1 or 7.1, leaves it out.
* Mono is 0.7071 × (left + right), which is BS.775's mono equation for a 3/2 source.

For an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track, the levels that the track signals for a Lo/Ro downmix replace A/52's defaults in the stereo and mono matrix.
The signalled centre level replaces 0.7071 on the centre, and the signalled surround level replaces 0.7071 on each surround channel.
The levels are `lorocmixlev` and `lorosurmixlev` when the track carries them, and otherwise `cmixlev` and `surmixlev`.
FFmpeg's decoder exports them as downmix side data on every decoded frame, so they need no probe fact.
The worker reads them from the track's first frame, so every job attempt of a stream session uses the same matrix.
The stereo is always Lo/Ro, never Lt/Rt, even when the track names Lt/Rt as its preferred downmix.

A downmix to 5.1 or 5.0, such as 7.1 to 5.1, `7.0` to 5.0, 5.1 to 5.0 or `6.0` to 5.1, keeps the front channels and the centre, and its surround pair is LS and RS as defined above, whichever pair the output layout names.
A downmix to 5.1 keeps the low-frequency channel, and a downmix to 5.0 leaves it out.
A downmix to 7.1, such as `7.1(wide)` to 7.1, keeps each source channel that 7.1 has.

Every other channel that FFmpeg names folds at 0.7071 into the nearest channel of these rules on its own side.
A wide, front-of-centre or top-front channel folds into the front channel, and a top-side, top-back or surround-direct channel folds into the surround.
A channel on the centre line, such as top centre or top back centre, folds into that pair at 0.5 on each side.
A second low-frequency channel follows the rule for the low-frequency channel.
Every layout that FFmpeg names therefore counts as a layout that this ADR names for a downmix (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032]).
FFmpeg reads a stream with an unknown layout, such as PCM in Matroska, as its default layout for the channel count, which is how `-ac` treats it, and the matrix applies to that layout.

The matrix is not normalized.
The sum stays in floating point until the limiter, so a 16-bit or 24-bit source cannot clip inside `pan`.

A downmix that only leaves channels out, such as 5.1 to 5.0, has no limiter.
In every other downmix, the limiter acts only on the output channels that sum two or more source channels, and every other channel passes through unchanged.
It links the two channels of a left-right pair, so the pair keeps its image, and it limits a centre or mono output on its own.
A 7.1 to 5.1 or `7.0` to 5.0 downmix therefore limits only its surround pair, and a stereo downmix limits its pair.
The worker splits the summed channels out with FFmpeg's `channelsplit`, limits them, and joins them back with `join`.

The ceiling depends on the codec that writes the downmix.
Each ceiling is a true peak as the example filter of ITU-R BS.1770-5 Annex 2 reads it, here called the Annex 2 filter:

* FLAC or ALAC: −1 dBTP.
For a source whose decoded channels stay within full scale, the Annex 2 filter read the output no more than 0.01 dB above −1 dBTP on every test, adversarial searches included (<<evidence-true-peak>>).
That is a measured result, not a proven bound for every input.
The tests do not cover a source whose decoded channels exceed full scale, and the worker does not scale or clip the source before the matrix.
* A lossy codec, in any downmix: −3 dBTP.
This ceiling is an empirical policy.
It held on the codecs, bit rates, layouts and materials that <<evidence-true-peak>> lists, and it does not hold for every material.
After decoding, full-scale white noise read above 0 dBTP at most multichannel steps, and so did some pink-noise inputs in stereo AAC and Opus and some test tones in mono AAC.
On 128 synthetic full-scale pink-noise inputs, stereo AAC and Opus decoded 2 to 13 inputs per bit rate above 0 dBTP, up to +1.43 dBTP between samples.
The highest readings came in the first AAC frame after an encoder started.
A seek into a loud scene starts an encoder in the same way.
No film soundtrack measured decoded above 0 dBTP at any stereo step.
The ceiling for stereo AAC and Opus stays at −3 dBTP despite these exceptions.

The worker limits the summed channels in two stages in series.
Both stages run `alimiter` at the same internal limit: 0.891251 (−1 dBFS) for FLAC and ALAC, and 0.707946 (−3 dBFS) for a lossy codec.
`alimiter` detects sample peaks only, so each stage passes it the summed channels together with short FIR copies of them at their own sample rate.
Each copy estimates the waveform at one instant between two samples.
The first stage has eleven copies: seven 128-tap Kaiser-windowed sinc phases at eighth-sample offsets, and the four phases of the Annex 2 filter.
The second stage reads the first stage's output through the four Annex 2 phases alone.
`alimiter` turns the summed channels down when a sample or a copy would pass the internal limit.
Its output is the summed channels themselves, so no resampler touches the audio that the viewer hears.
The worker checks the eleven coefficient vectors in as a fixed resource and never computes them at run time.

Every `alimiter` uses attack and release settings of 5 ms and 50 ms, adds no make-up gain (`level=0`) and removes its own delay (`latency=1`).
The worker trims the copies' delay, so the audio keeps its timing and its sample count.
While no sample and no copy passes the internal limit, and no earlier peak's attack or release is still in progress, the summed channels come out as the matrix wrote them, sample for sample.
When one passes, `alimiter` lowers the gain before that instant and raises it again after the last one, so neighbouring samples change too, including samples below the ceiling.
A weaker peak during a release does not restart the release, and the last step of a release can leave one sample with a gain slightly above 1.
A worker that does not list probe version 7 downmixes with FFmpeg's default, as before.

*Why:* BS.775 and A/52 both mix the centre at −3 dB into each side.
Two speakers at −3 dB give the same power as the centre speaker at 0 dB, so dialogue keeps its level against the front channels.
FFmpeg's default uses the same levels, but its result depends on the sample format.
FFmpeg normalizes the matrix only when the output or internal sample format is an integer.
AAC, AC-3 and E-AC-3 take floating-point samples, so their downmix was not normalized and went over full scale in a loud scene.
FLAC and ALAC take integer samples, so their downmix was normalized, and the centre fell to −10.7 dB from 5.1 and −12.9 dB from 7.1.
That is A/52's worst-case scaling of 1/2.414, which protects a sum that only a loud scene reaches by turning every scene down.
Adding `volume=2` to `-ac 2` lifted the centre to +3 dB but put 33 % of a loud scene's samples over full scale.
An unnormalized matrix keeps dialogue at −3 dB in every codec, and the limiter acts only on the peaks that would go over.

FFmpeg's decoders name the surround pair of TrueHD, DTS-HD MA and AC-3 as a side pair, while the AAC and Opus encoders name a back pair.
Counting that rename as a downmix ran `pan` and the limiter on every such encode, which a rename does not need.
On 60 s of Tears of Steel 5.1, a 24-bit master with a back pair that peaks at 0 dBFS, FLAC's `5.1(side)` alone kept every sample, while the limited path changed 4.5 % of them, by up to −19.3 dBFS.

`alimiter` links every channel that it receives.
With one channel at 0 dBFS and another at −20 dBFS, the second came out at −23 dBFS.
On Tears of Steel, a 5.1 to 5.0 downmix that sums nothing still drew a linked limiter at −1 dBFS, which turned 239,307 centre samples between −30 and −6 dBFS down by more than 0.5 dB.
Dialogue then ducked under peaks in other channels.
Limiting only the summed channels leaves the centre alone in every downmix to more than two channels.

`alimiter` detects sample peaks only, so its −3 dBFS stereo output of Tears of Steel still reached −0.1 dBTP.
The copies show `alimiter` the instants between samples at the source's own rate, so a true-peak ceiling needs no resampler in the audio that the viewer hears.
One stage is not enough.
`alimiter` changes its gain gradually over the attack, and the Annex 2 filter spans twelve samples, so the filter reads the limited output through a gain that changes across its taps.
After one stage, a bounded 16-sample burst read 0.10 dB over the ceiling, and a burst that a targeted search found read 0.23 dB over at 22.05 kHz (<<evidence-true-peak>>).
The second stage starts from samples that are already within the limit and removes only the first stage's small excess, so its own gain changes much less.
After both stages, the Annex 2 filter read no test output more than 0.01 dB above the ceiling.
The second stage uses the Annex 2 phases alone, because they are the meter that the ceiling names.
On Tears of Steel's 734 s stereo downmix, the two stages turned the mix down by more than 1 dB for 35.0 s (4.8 %), and the Annex 2 filter read the output at −1.00 dBTP.
FLAC and ALAC add no coding overshoot, so a downmix that they write needs only a true-peak margin.
A lossy codec overshoots after decoding, so its ceiling leaves 3 dB for that overshoot.
At −3 dBTP, the summed channels decoded below full scale on the film, pink-noise and pink-burst inputs in stereo AC-3 and E-AC-3, in every multichannel step measured and in mono AAC at 96 kb/s.
In stereo AAC and Opus, 1 to 3 of 32 pink-noise inputs decoded above 0 dBTP at each bit rate measured, up to +0.66 dBTP.
On 128 pink-noise inputs, 2 to 13 did at each bit rate, up to +1.43 dBTP.
The −3 dBTP ceiling accepts these exceptions.
They come from synthetic full-scale pink noise, whose waveform these codecs do not reproduce closely.
At AAC 96 kb/s, the coding error over a 20 s pink-noise input was only 8 dB below the signal.
A lower ceiling therefore did not reliably lower the decoded peak.
Film soundtracks kept below 0 dBTP at −3 dBTP, and a lower ceiling limits every film's loud passages for longer.
A stereo track, such as stereo music, needs no downmix, so the limiter never touches it, and these readings do not apply to it.
One −3 dBTP ceiling replaces the earlier −3 dBFS for a lossy stereo or mono downmix and −1 dBFS for a lossy downmix to more than two channels.
A sample-peak ceiling misses the peaks between samples, and at −1 dBFS the folded surround pair of a 7.1 to 5.1 downmix decoded over full scale in AAC 5.1 at 320 kb/s.
The limiter does not touch the channels that the matrix does not sum, so they keep the source's peaks; on film audio that peaks at 0 dBFS, AAC, AC-3, E-AC-3 and Opus each decoded those channels over full scale on at least one window measured.
The release stays at 50 ms, so the mix recovers within 50 ms of a peak instead of about half a second; its cost on a loud bass line is in the consequences.

A/52 lets a mixer signal the centre and surround levels of a stereo downmix, and Dolby's hardware decoders apply them.
FATE's Monsters, Inc. AC-3 sample signals its surrounds at 0.5 (−6 dB), and The Great Wall's E-AC-3 sample signals its centre at 0.595 (−4.5 dB) and its surrounds at 0.5.
A/52's defaults would play those surrounds 3 dB and that centre 1.5 dB louder than the mixer chose.
Lo/Ro is the stereo that plays correctly on two speakers and folds safely to mono.
Lt/Rt encodes the surrounds in opposite phase for a matrix decoder such as Pro Logic, so their content cancels in mono.
A hardware decoder that receives the copy turns it down by 31 dB less the track's dialogue level, so a downmix of the decoded track would otherwise play louder than the copy, and the level would jump when a step turns the copy into an encode.
With a side pair and a back pair, FFmpeg's default keeps one pair at 0 dB and mixes the other in at −3 dB, so a sound that pans between them changes level.
Mixing both at 0.7071 keeps the power of the four surrounds.
BS.775 says that a two-channel downmix often leaves out the low-frequency channel and that the main channels must carry every essential part of the programme.
Film codes the low-frequency channel for +10 dB of playback gain (BS.775).
Folding it in at its coded level would be 10 dB too quiet, and at its playback level it would drive the limiter on every explosion.
A 5.1 or 7.1 track is most often a film mix, so it follows BS.775 and leaves the channel out.
Music codes the low-frequency channel for no playback gain (BS.775), and a track without surrounds is more often music or television than a film mix.
At 0.7071 into each side, that channel keeps its power, and a film's `2.1` track keeps its bass 10 dB below its playback level instead of losing it.
The measurements are in <<evidence-downmix>>.

[[decision-audio-for-picture]]
=== The server lowers multichannel audio when a lower bitrate lets a larger picture fit

After <<decision-audio-steps>>, the server can take a lower multichannel step than the step that rule took.
The server takes it only when the step lets a larger picture fit:

* In Auto, a step lets a larger picture fit when the ladder with that step offers a higher top tier.
* In a fixed quality, a step lets a larger picture fit when the single variant falls short of the full bitrate that it would have without a maximum bitrate, and the step gives it that full bitrate.

An encode that Original reaches plays in Auto under ADR 0052, so the rule for Auto applies to that encode.

Among the multichannel steps, the server takes the first step that reaches the largest picture that any multichannel step reaches.
The server never takes a stereo step under this rule.
When no multichannel step gives a larger picture, the server lowers nothing further.
The rule applies to encodes only.
A video copy keeps the source's picture, so no step gives it a larger one.

With 5.1 E-AC-3 at 768 kb/s, a maximum bitrate of 2,000 kb/s gives the 480p tier with E-AC-3 at 448 kb/s instead of the 360p tier with 768 kb/s.
A maximum bitrate of 5,500 kb/s gives the 1080p tier with 448 kb/s instead of the 720p tier with 768 kb/s.

*Why:* 5.1 at 448 kb/s is DVD's usual AC-3 rate, and viewers hear little loss from 768 kb/s.
A larger picture tier is plainly visible.
Surround stays, so a home theater keeps surround.
A step that gains no tier would lower the audio for nothing, so the server does not take it.

[[decision-fixed-quality]]
=== Each fixed quality has the picture box of its tier

Each fixed quality names one quality tier, and that tier's box is the fixed quality's picture box:

* the 360p fixed quality has the 360p box;
* the 480p fixed quality has the 480p box;
* the 720p fixed quality has the 720p box;
* the 1080p fixed quality has the 1080p box;
* the 4K fixed quality has the 2160p box.

A fixed quality's single variant is bounded by its picture box, the viewer's other limits and one capability entry.
The server never upscales a fixed quality's variant.
A fixed 720p on a 480p source therefore plays the source at 480p.

A fixed quality's bitrate follows the picture that the worker encodes.
The variant encodes at the nominal bitrate, for the chosen codec family, of the smallest tier whose box holds the encoded picture in either orientation.
A fixed 4K on a 1080p source therefore encodes at the 1080p tier's bitrate.
Two values cap that bitrate: the source's bitrate, when the probe knows it, and the maximum bitrate less the audio bitrate (<<decision-bitrate>>).
When the probe does not know the source's bitrate, the variant takes that tier's nominal bitrate within the maximum bitrate less the audio bitrate.

*Why:* A viewer picks a fixed quality to get that quality.
A fixed quality that ignores its resolution gives the viewer a 4K encode when they asked for 480p.
The same boxes as the tiers make a fixed quality's variant match the variant that Auto offers for that tier.
A tier's bitrate suits a picture of that tier's size.
A fixed quality above the source's size, or one that a capability entry shrinks, encodes a smaller picture than its name says.
The bitrate of the tier that holds that picture suits it.
The source's bitrate suits the source's picture, which can be far larger.

[[explicitly-deferred]]
=== Explicitly deferred

* This ADR does not add a quality cap for each viewer or each Profile.
* This ADR does not add a ServerAdmin list of custom domain names that count as the local network.
A later decision may add one for a household that names its server under its own domain.
* This ADR does not decide whether a client's playback details show that the server lowered the audio.
The multivariant playlist already names the delivered audio codec and channel count.
* This ADR does not add a night mode, a dialogue boost or other dynamic range compression for the viewer to turn on.
The worker's decode of an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track applies none of the track's own compression (ADR 0043).
Such a mode is the viewer's choice, not a property of a correct downmix.
The viewer's platform already offers such a mode, for example tvOS's Reduce Loud Sounds.
The server's audio work ends at a bit-perfect copy or a correct downmix, and a night mode would change the mix after that.
link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc#explicitly-deferred[ADR 0043] records the deferral.
A later decision that adds such a setting, or a loudness normalisation beside it, would read each track's integrated loudness, loudness range and true peak from a background scan like ADR 0054's bitrate scan (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032]).

== Consequences

* A source that exceeds the viewer's limits is encoded in Auto and in a fixed quality, even when the client could copy it.
That encode costs worker capacity and some picture quality.
* A viewer in Original receives a copy of the source even when the source exceeds their limits.
The stream can then buffer on a slow connection.
* In Original, the viewer's limits never bind a copy.
When Original must encode, the playback choice changes to Auto, and the viewer's limits bind the encode (ADR 0052).
* A source that cannot be encoded can play over the viewer's limits in Auto and in a fixed quality.
So can a source whose copy the client accepts when the client accepts no encode that the server makes of it.
When the player refuses the copy of such a source, no encode can replace the copy, so playback ends with the typed terminal error unless another eligible format remains (ADR 0052).
* When workers are connected but none has a worker-verified encoder for a codec that the client accepts an encode in, the source plays over the viewer's limits until the worker is fixed.
* When the client accepts an encode, a busy worker or no connected worker leaves the viewer with an answer that the client can retry rather than a copy over the viewer's limits.
* A copy of a source whose copied video's bitrate and container bitrate are both unknown can exceed the viewer's maximum bitrate.
* In Auto and in a fixed quality, a video copy can play beside lower audio than the session would otherwise get.
The viewer keeps the source's full picture in exchange.
* When the probe does not know the copied video's bitrate, the copy's cost over-counts.
The container bitrate also holds the source's tracks that the session does not deliver, such as other audio languages, commentary and subtitles.
When the session encodes its audio, the container bitrate also holds the source track that the encode replaces.
The server can then lower the audio or encode the video for a source that would fit.
The over-count lasts until the probe measures each stream's own bitrate.
* When the probe does not know the copied video's bitrate and every audio rendition is a copy, no audio step lowers the copy's cost.
Every encoded step adds its bitrate to the container bitrate.
In Auto and in a fixed quality, a copy whose container bitrate is over the maximum bitrate therefore encodes its video, unless the source cannot be encoded or the server has nothing to encode it to.
* The server learns of a turned window or screen at the next stream session.
ADR 0049 decides the same for a display change.
* Widescreen films and portrait videos keep the full width or height of their tier.
Their variants carry more pixels than before at the same tier bitrate.
* Auto's 1440p and 2160p tiers cost more encoding load than a 1080p tier.
ADR 0044's headroom decides when they start.
* Each tier carries a bitrate for each codec family.
A codec family that the server starts to encode needs its own bitrate for every tier.
* Players show labels outside the tier names, such as 900p or 280p, for pictures smaller than their tier's box.
* A picture a few pixels short of its tier's box still takes the tier's label.
* When a capability entry shrinks a higher tier to a lower tier's label, Auto offers one fewer variant.
* When the audio leaves no room for the 360p tier, the viewer hears lower-bitrate audio, and surround can become stereo.
A copied audio track can become an encode, which costs worker time.
* Below a maximum bitrate of 1,184 kb/s, a video encode whose 5.1 steps are E-AC-3 or AC-3 plays in stereo.
Below 1,120 kb/s, one whose steps include 5.1 AAC does.
An AAC 7.1 copy has that step too.
A copied AAC 5.1 or 7.1 track has that step even when the server would encode E-AC-3.
A copied 5.1 or 7.1 track whose bitrate is below those steps keeps surround down to its own bitrate plus the 360p tier's 800 kb/s.
* Under a maximum bitrate, an encode can play surround at a lower multichannel step, such as 5.1 at 448 kb/s, in exchange for a higher top tier in Auto or a single variant's full bitrate.
In Auto, every variant of the ladder, the lowest included, carries that lower audio.
* Below a maximum bitrate of 896 kb/s, the video gets less than the 360p tier's bitrate.
Below 241 kb/s, the stream exceeds the maximum bitrate rather than not playing at all.
* The lowest audio step is 96 kb/s, so a stream under a low maximum bitrate spends 32 kb/s more on audio than a 64 kb/s step would, and its video gets that much less.
* The server can change the session's audio codec to AAC to meet the maximum bitrate, and every audio rendition then follows.
* A copied 3- or 4-channel track keeps surround in a lower step only in a layout that its codec carries exactly.
A copied AAC `quad` or `2.1` track, and any 3- or 4-channel track whose layout the probe does not record, steps to stereo.
* No AAC output carries channel configuration 0, so Chromium plays every AAC rendition that the server encodes.
* A track with more channels than a capability entry's channel maximum of 3 or 4 plays in stereo, not in a 3- or 4-channel downmix.
* A downmix keeps dialogue at −3 dB on each side in every codec, or at the level that an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track signals, and a `2.1` or `3.1` track keeps its low-frequency channel in stereo.
A 5.1 or 7.1 track still leaves its low-frequency channel out of stereo.
* An encode that only renames a surround pair keeps every sample, so a TrueHD, DTS-HD MA or AC-3 5.1 track reaches AAC's or Opus's `5.1` without a filter.
* A downmix that only leaves channels out, such as 5.1 to 5.0, is never limited.
In a 7.1 to 5.1 downmix, the limiter can briefly turn the surround pair down, which shifts the rear image while the front channels and the centre stay unchanged.
* When a loud scene's stereo sum passes the ceiling, the limiter lowers both channels, dialogue included.
In the synthetic loud scene of <<evidence-downmix>>, the centre fell from −3.0 dB to −7.3 dB while the limiter held the peaks.
* The summed channels of a lossy downmix peak 3 dB below full scale, so they play up to 3 dB quieter than an unlimited mix in their loudest moments.
* A downmix that FLAC or ALAC writes has a ceiling 2 dB higher than a lossy downmix of the same track, so it plays louder in loud passages.
An audio step from a FLAC downmix to a lossy codec therefore lowers the level of those passages.
With the earlier limiters, the gap was about 1 dB on Tears of Steel (−14.5 against −15.4 LUFS); the two-stage limiter's gap is not measured.
* The true-peak measurements cover FFmpeg's AAC encoder only.
How far `aac_at` overshoots after the limiter is not measured.
* The two-stage limiter costs CPU and memory.
A whole stereo audio job of Tears of Steel to FLAC took 1.97 % of one core per real-time stream, against 1.49 % with one stage and 0.31 % without a limiter, and up to 139 MB of memory, against 49 MB without a limiter.
These figures come from one FFmpeg process's CPU time; the capacity for concurrent streams is not measured.
* Each stage holds back its 5 ms lookahead and its copies' delay before it passes audio on.
The output's timestamps do not move, but the delay before the first output through HLS is not yet measured.
* The ceiling names the Annex 2 filter, and other true-peak meters can read the limited channels higher.
On full-band noise, a −1 dBTP downmix read up to −0.70 dBTP on libebur128 and up to −0.12 dBTP on a 1025-tap 32-phase reconstruction.
On film audio, both read it within 0.1 dB of the ceiling.
* The Annex 2 filter reads tones up to 0.22 dB high near a quarter of the sample rate, so the limiter can turn such a tone down by that much while a wider reconstruction reads it under the ceiling.
* While the limiter acts on a loud bass line, its 50 ms release modulates the mix at the bass frequency.
A 40 Hz line at −1 dBFS in a stereo downmix put sidebands at −39.6 dBc around 1 kHz dialogue; a 500 ms release would lower them to −58.4 dBc.
* A lossy encode of the summed channels can still go over full scale after decoding.
In stereo AAC and Opus, 2 to 13 of 128 synthetic full-scale pink-noise inputs read above 0 dBTP at each bit rate measured, up to +1.43 dBTP.
Full-scale white noise did so at most multichannel steps, up to +2.43 dBTP (<<evidence-true-peak>>).
The highest AAC readings came in the first AAC frame after an encoder started.
A seek into a loud scene starts an encoder, so a stereo AAC downmix is most likely to read above full scale just after such a seek.
* Auto beside a copy lists variants that the server encodes only after a switch.
The first segments after a switch down wait for that variant's encoder to start, as every on-demand start does under ADR 0019.
* A viewer away from home gets the same rules as a viewer at home.
A viewer on a slow connection relies on the player's variant switching, or on the Apple client's own cap on a metered connection.
* Under the upload budget, a stream session can play a lower variant than its viewer's own connection carries, because other stream sessions use the upload.
* The budget reserves nothing, so stream sessions that start at the same moment can together take the counted total over the streams' share.
* Before its player requests a media segment, a stream session counts at its first-listed variant, even when the budget left that variant out, so the counted total runs high for a moment.
* When the budget leaves the copy out, a lower variant encodes from the start of playback and takes a transcode place and a worker job slot, where the copy needed neither.
* When a player keeps requesting the copy after a playlist fetched again left it out, the variant that the playlist started keeps running and holds its transcode place until the player requests another variant.
* A false `LOCAL_NETWORK` claim takes that stream session out of the budget, so the streams can use more than the share.
* A viewer who reaches the server on the local network by a single-label name counts against the budget.
* A direct play of a file whose measurements do not cover every video stream, such as a second video track or cover art stored as a video stream, counts at the probed container bitrate.
* The setting takes whole megabits per second, so a speed test of 23.7 Mb/s is entered as 23 or 24.
* An AAC encode of a track of 7 or more channels plays 5.1, or 5.0 for a `7.0` track, so the track's second surround pair folds into the first.
* An Opus or FLAC encode of a `7.0` track plays as 7.1 with a silent low-frequency channel when that codec's capability entry accepts 8 or more channels, and the player shows 7.1.
In Opus, it costs the 7.1 rate of 640 kb/s, where a 5.0 encode costs 448 kb/s (ADR 0043).
Everywhere else, the track plays 5.0, and each side's two surrounds share one channel.
* A copied AC-3 or E-AC-3 `3.0(back)` or `3.1` track steps to stereo, and an encode of such a track is stereo, until a check on hardware passes.
* Every job that encodes audio names a layout, and a job that names a layout of 3, 4, 7 or 8 channels needs a worker that lists probe version 7 (ADR 0032).
Until the server stores a file's probe version 7 outcome, that file's 3- and 4-channel tracks step to stereo.
* A track larger than the default track decides the audio step for the whole stream session.
A copied AAC stereo default track at 128 kb/s beside a copied AAC 5.1 track at 384 kb/s costs 384 kb/s.
Under a maximum bitrate of 1,150 kb/s, the 5.1 track encodes at AAC 5.1 at 320 kb/s and the default track keeps its copy.
Under 1,000 kb/s, both play AAC stereo, the default track still as its copy.
Before this amendment, that session cost 128 kb/s and took no step, so a variant sized to the maximum went 256 kb/s over it.
* A step that changes the codec for a larger track encodes the default track too, even when the default track alone would have kept its copy.
* A fixed quality above the source's size plays the source's size, not the size that its name suggests.
It encodes at the bitrate of the tier that holds that size.
* A source wider than 3840 is encoded at 3840 wide, even on a screen that could show more.
* Players show no label for a fixed quality's encode.
* A fixed quality on a source whose bitrate is below the tier's bitrate plays at the source's bitrate.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Offering a copy alone in Auto stalls playback when the viewer's bandwidth drops below the copy's bitrate.
* Encoding the lower variants beside a copy from the start spends worker capacity on variants that the player may never request.
* Listing the variant nearest 2000 kb/s first beside a copy, as ADR 0008 does for encodes, starts an encode when the stream session starts, for a player that may never need it.
* Giving the lower variants their own segment list makes a switch from the copy fall inside a segment.
* Listing lower variants beside a copy while job attempts carry whole slots lets a switch between the copy and a lower variant repeat or skip up to one slot of media.
* Classifying viewers as at home or away by IP address, with separate limits for each, misjudges viewers on weak Wi-Fi at home and on fast connections away.
A VPN, a reverse proxy or carrier-grade NAT hides the viewer's location from the server.
* A server-side cap for cellular viewers cannot see that a connection is metered, because only the device knows.
* Refusing playback, or encoding instead of copying, when the upload budget is full would turn a soft limit into a playback failure.
* Counting each variant at its BANDWIDTH peak would leave out variants that fit, because the peaks of several streams rarely come at once.
* Leaving the top variant that stays to start on the player's first request lets another stream session take the last transcode place before that request.
* Holding a transcode-limit place for the variants that stay until the player's first request would hold a place that runs no job attempt and needs its own timeout.
* Counting a direct play at its measured average when its measurements do not cover every video stream counts it below what it sends.
* Counting a single-label host name as the local network would attest a Tailscale route through MagicDNS's search domain as local.
* Classifying the network route on the server by the peer address fails behind a reverse proxy, a tunnel or hairpin NAT.

* Applying the viewer's limits to encodes only would let a copy exceed every limit the viewer set.
* Applying the viewer's limits to a copy in Original would break Original's promise of the source's own quality.
* Letting an encode that Original reaches ignore the viewer's limits would keep no promise of Original, because the encode already changes the source.
It would only give the viewer a larger stream than they asked for.
* Refusing to copy a source that cannot be encoded when it exceeds the viewer's limits would leave the viewer with nothing to play.
Refusing a copy over the limits when the client capability declaration accepts no encode of the source would do the same.
* Refusing a copy over the limits when no connected worker has an encoder for an accepted codec would blame the viewer's device for a broken worker and tell no one to fix it.
* Copying over the viewer's limits whenever no worker can encode at that moment would let a busy or missing worker override a limit that the viewer chose.
* Refusing a copy when the source's bitrate is unknown would encode files that the client plays as they are.

* Full-scale white noise decoded above 0 dBTP at most multichannel steps, by up to +2.43 dBTP in 5.1 Opus at 256 kb/s, and in mono AAC at 64 and 96 kb/s, AC-3 at 96 kb/s and Opus at 64 kb/s.
* The channels that the limiter does not touch keep the source's peaks.
On a Tears of Steel window whose front channels peak at 0 dBFS, AAC 4.0 at 256 kb/s decoded those channels at +2.06 dBTP, with 177 samples beyond full scale in 60 s.

[[evidence-seven-zero]]
== Evidence: a `7.0` track in Opus

These measurements ran on 2026-11-03 against FFmpeg 8.1.3 in the pinned worker image.
The source was a synthetic 48 kHz `7.0` file that the same FFmpeg built.
Each of its seven channels carried its own tone, between 300 and 1,900 Hz, at −20 dBFS peak.
Each level below is relative to the source channel's own level.

* `libopus` refused the `7.0` layout, with and without `-mapping_family 1`.
* Padded to `7.1` with `pan`, which gave the added low-frequency channel a gain of 0, the Opus encode decoded with each of the seven tones at 0.0 dB in its own channel.
The low-frequency channel decoded as silence.
* Folded to 5.0, with each side's back and side surrounds at 0.7071, the Opus encode kept the front channels and the centre at 0.0 dB.
Each surround tone came out at −3.0 to −3.1 dB in its own side's surround channel.
* hls.js 1.7.3 in Chromium 156 and Firefox 157 played the padded `7.1` Opus encode as fragmented MP4 with `-mapping_family 1`, through HLS and through plain MSE.
Every channel came out in its RFC 7845 position, within 0.6 dB of the source's level, and the low-frequency channel stayed silent.
* In the same players, the 5.0 encode put every channel in its RFC 7845 position, and each surround tone came out within 0.6 dB of the fold's −3 dB.
