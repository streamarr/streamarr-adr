= ADR 0044: Encoder Headroom, Measured in Pixel Rate, Decides When Extra Variants Start, and One Preset Scale Serves Every Software Encoder

== Status

Accepted

link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018] left its capacity policy for later, until a working playback path existed.
This decision fills in that capacity policy.
This decision builds on link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019].
ADR 0019 starts and restarts the jobs that produce each variant.
ADR 0019 starts each variant on demand unless this ADR's policy allows more.
This decision relies on a rule in link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
Under that rule, the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.

== Context

In this ADR, a variant is one HLS variant in the sense of RFC 8216's `EXT-X-STREAM-INF`.
A stream session's variants form its quality ladder.
The quality ladder holds the same media at several sizes and bitrates.
The player switches between the variants of the quality ladder.
A variant is never an HLS rendition.
An HLS rendition is an `EXT-X-MEDIA` audio or subtitle track.
Production speed is the number of media seconds that a job attempt produces per second of wall-clock time.
A variant produced at 2× real time delivers each media segment in half the segment's media duration.

Before this decision, the server started every variant of a session together whenever the worker had free job slots.
A worker with four slots and no hardware encoder therefore ran four software encodes at once.
The worker passed no `-preset` option to FFmpeg.
libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1 therefore each ran at their library's default preset.
Those defaults suit offline encoding.
Those defaults are slow for live transcoding.

On a 10-core arm64 host with libx264 at its default preset, running four variants at once made every variant about three times slower.
The 1080p variant was then produced at about real time.
That speed is too slow for a player to keep fetching the 1080p variant instead of switching down.
Free job slots say nothing about how fast each job will run.
The server needs a measure of the encoding load that a host can carry.
The server also needs a rule that decides which variants may run within that encoding load.

Streamarr can serve several users from one worker at once.
A stream session's extra variants are the variants that ADR 0019's on-demand rules would not run.
Extra variants use up encoder headroom.
Encoder headroom is the encoding load that a worker can still take on.
Another user's stream session may need that encoder headroom.
A seek also needs its first media segment quickly.
On a busy worker, producers that already run ahead of their viewers compete with that seek for CPU.

An encoder preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
The preset also decides how much encoding load each variant costs.
libx264 and libx265 name their presets with words from `ultrafast` to `veryslow`.
libsvtav1 numbers its presets instead.
A ServerAdmin is an account that holds the global superuser authority of link:0024-identity-authority-by-relationship.adoc[ADR 0024].
A ServerAdmin needs one name that means the same trade-off for every software encoder.

Some workers have a hardware encoder backend, such as NVENC on an NVIDIA GPU or VideoToolbox on macOS.
Such a worker can also run the software encoder for the same codec.
The server needs a rule that decides which encoder backend each new variant uses.

== Decision

[[decision-pixel-rate]]
=== Pixel rate is the unit of encoding load

Pixel rate is width × height × frames per second.
Streamarr expresses encoding load in pixel rate wherever it models, compares or reports that load.
The output pixel rate uses a variant's output size and output frame rate.
The source pixel rate uses the decoded size and frame rate of the source video stream.
Streamarr never expresses encoding load as a resolution or as a preset name.

*Why:* At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film encodes about 2.5 times faster relative to real time than a 60 fps source of the same size.
A resolution cannot tell those two sources apart.
A faster preset saved less at small variants.
`superfast` cost 0.28× `medium`'s CPU at 1080p but 0.53× at 360p.
The saving shrank because the cost of decoding the 1080p source does not shrink with the output.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-tonemap>>.

[[decision-headroom-model]]
=== A headroom model predicts each variant's production speed

The headroom model predicts the encoding load that each job attempt puts on a worker.
The model states that load as the job attempt's cost in CPU seconds per media second.
The cost of one job attempt is the sum of three terms.

* The encode term is an encoder-and-preset constant `a` × the output pixel rate.
The encoder and its preset select `a`.
* The decode term is a decoder constant `d` × the source pixel rate.
The source's codec and whether the job attempt decodes in hardware together select `d`.
* The tone-mapping term is the cost of tone mapping.
The tone-mapping term applies only when the job attempt tone-maps.

Every variant's job decodes the source in its own FFmpeg process.
Each job attempt's cost therefore includes its own decode term.
The model predicts the production speed of every running variant, not only the worker's total CPU.
The model derives each running variant's production speed from that variant's share of the worker's cores.
The model sizes a worker's encoder headroom for several users' encodes at once, not for a single session.

A seek attempt is a job attempt that starts after media time zero.
Under link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037], a seek attempt on a VideoToolbox or NVDEC worker decodes in hardware.
The decoder constant of such a seek attempt is the hardware decoder's constant, not the software decoder's constant.

*Why:* With constants fitted per source and preset, the model predicted the CPU of each measured libx264 variant within about 7 %.
One four-variant ladder at `medium` needed 6.1 CPU seconds per media second.
On paper, 10 cores therefore held 1.6 such ladders.
Yet in a single ladder, the 1080p variant ran at only 0.93× real time.
The 360p variant of the same ladder ran at 3.6× or faster.
In that ladder, each FFmpeg process received an equal share of the cores.
That equal share was too small for the 1080p variant to keep up with real time.
The 360p variant needed less than its equal share.
Hardware decoding changes a job attempt's CPU cost.
On an RTX 5090 in the pinned worker image, one 1080p libx264 replacement attempt at `faster` used 16.47 CPU seconds with NVDEC decode, against 19.26 s with software decode.
That attempt covered 421 frames, and each figure is the median of 5 runs.
A decoder constant that ignores the hardware decoder would therefore overstate the cost of seek attempts on such a worker.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-hardware-decode>>.

[[decision-speed-floor]]
=== Every running variant keeps the speed floor

A player switches down to a lower variant when the player's current variant arrives too slowly.
The speed floor is the production speed that every running variant must keep.
The speed floor is the larger of two speeds.
The first speed is 1.35× real time.
Below 1.35× real time, hls.js switches down.
The second speed is the floor of the neediest Apple player at the stream session's segment length.
An Apple player's floor is the lowest production speed at which the first media segment after a seek still arrives within that player's limit.
AVPlayer abandons a segment request for which the server has sent no bytes after one segment length.
Safari's native player switches down when a held segment request waits longer than the player allows.
The neediest Apple player is the Apple player with the higher floor.
At 3 s segments, the neediest Apple player is Safari's native player.
At 3 s segments, the speed floor is therefore about 2.10× real time.
The 2.10× floor also covers AVPlayer's lower floor of about 1.70×.

A stream session can have merged-slot segments under link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
In such a stream session, a slot is one 3 s interval of the zero-based media timeline.
A merged-slot segment spans several slots.
The segments of such a stream session therefore differ in length.
In a stream session with merged-slot segments, the server computes the speed floor at the session's slot length, 3 s.

Every rule in this ADR that protects a running variant's speed protects the speed floor.
No rule in this ADR protects 1.35× real time alone.

*Why:* hls.js 1.7.0 keeps a variant while 0.95 × the hls.js throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate.
The server advertises `BANDWIDTH` as 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`.
Those three facts give 1.33× to 1.37× across the ladder.
The Apple floors are derived, not measured.
On a 10-core arm64 host, two or three users each started a seek at the same time.
The runs used 3 s and 4 s segments, with 3 runs for each combination.
Each seek started a 1080p libx264 job attempt at `faster`.
A fit of the measured waits against each stream's measured production speed gives each Apple player's floor.
At 3 s segments, the fit gives 1.64× for AVPlayer and 2.01× for Safari's native player.
Over 20 seeks of a 1080p variant at 3 s segments, a real server and worker added about 0.1 s on top of the worker's own production time.
Other work shared the host, so 0.1 s is an upper bound.
That overhead raises the floors to about 1.70× and 2.10×.
1.35× alone protects hls.js but not the Apple players.
Evidence: <<evidence-floor>>, <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-calibration]]
=== The server measures each worker's constants continuously and keeps them

The server derives each job attempt's production speed from the arrival times of the attempt's media segments.
The server fits each worker's constants to those speeds continuously while sessions play.
The server keeps each worker's last calibration, together with the hardware and FFmpeg build that the calibration describes.
A worker that registers again with the same hardware and the same FFmpeg build takes jobs at once.
The server starts that worker from the kept calibration and keeps calibrating the worker continuously.
Only a worker with new hardware or a new FFmpeg build runs a short benchmark when the worker registers.
A worker that registers for the first time counts as a worker with new hardware.
Such a worker runs the benchmark before the worker accepts any job.
The server takes that worker's first constants from the benchmark.

A measurement validates the constants before any early start of an extra variant or any preset choice depends on the constants.
Until that measurement passes, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply unconditionally.
Until that measurement passes, Automatic also keeps the fixed meaning that <<decision-preset-setting>> gives Automatic.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/499[streamarr/streamarr-server#499] builds the headroom model and runs that measurement.

*Why:* The same preset cost different amounts on different content.
At every variant, a grain-heavy clip cost 1.3× to 1.9× as much per media second as a 24 fps film.
A single set of constants fitted across four sources missed individual ladders by up to about a quarter.
With one set of three variants running, 1080p measured 1.19× real time in the first run and 1.39× in a repeated run.
A benchmark at every registration would delay every restart of a worker whose hardware and FFmpeg build have not changed.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-eager-start]]
=== Extra variants start only while every running variant keeps the speed floor

The requested variant is the variant whose media segment the player has just requested, as ADR 0019 defines it.
ADR 0019's on-demand rules decide which variants run when the headroom model allows no extra variant.
Under those rules, creating a stream session starts the variant that the multivariant playlist lists first.
Once the stream session exists, a variant starts only when the player requests one of that variant's segments.
ADR 0019's only-requested stop is the rule that a media-segment request for one video variant stops the producers of the stream session's other video variants.
An extra variant is a variant that runs only because the headroom model allows it.
ADR 0019's on-demand rules would not run an extra variant.

The server starts extra variants only while the headroom model predicts that every running variant on the worker, in every stream session, stays at or above the speed floor with the extra variants running.
One stream session's extra variants therefore never push another stream session's requested variant below the speed floor.
The server starts the extra variants nearest the requested variant first.
At each distance from the requested variant, the server starts the variant below the requested variant before the variant above.
The server continues in that order up to the whole ladder.

An extra variant that the headroom model started is exempt from ADR 0019's only-requested stop.
An extra variant runs while headroom allows.
The server stops an extra variant when headroom runs out.
When the player switches to another variant, the variant that the player leaves keeps running as an extra variant while headroom allows.
When the model predicts no headroom for an extra variant, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply.
Until a measurement validates the model's constants, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply unconditionally.

*Why:* With libx264 at `medium` and 6 s segments, four variants running together pushed 1080p down to 0.94× to 1.06× real time.
That speed is below the speed floor.
An extra variant is worth starting only when every running variant keeps the speed floor with the extra variant running.
Without the exemption, the player's next media-segment request would stop every extra variant.
A quality switch would then find no segments of the new variant ready.
ADR 0019 gives the player measurements behind starting variants on demand.
Evidence: <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-capacity-shortfall]]
=== A capacity shortfall never refuses playback

A capacity shortfall exists when no preset that the preset setting allows keeps a variant at the speed floor.
A 60 fps source or many concurrent users can cause a capacity shortfall.
During a capacity shortfall, the server never refuses playback.
The requested variant always runs.
The server stops the extra variants on that worker.
A capacity metric shows the shortfall to the ServerAdmin.

No separate rule removes variants from the advertised quality ladder when capacity is short.
ADR 0019's on-demand start and the headroom model decide which variants run when capacity is short.

*Why:* Stopping extra variants frees encoder headroom for every requested variant and keeps every viewer playing.
The capacity metric tells the ServerAdmin that the worker needs more capacity.

[[decision-preset-setting]]
=== The preset is Automatic unless a ServerAdmin fixes one

A preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
The preset setting is one server-wide setting.
The web client shows the preset setting on its ServerAdmin settings page.
The preset setting's default is Automatic.
A ServerAdmin can replace Automatic with one fixed preset from the preset scale in <<decision-preset-scale>>.
One fixed preset covers every software encoder: libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1.
A variant's resolved preset is the encoder's own preset that the server derives from the preset setting for that variant.
Every job carries its variant's resolved preset to the worker.
The worker has no preset setting.
The worker passes the job's preset to the encoder.

Automatic means `faster` for libx264.
Automatic means `veryfast` for libx265.
Automatic means preset `10` for libsvtav1.
On the preset scale, libsvtav1 preset `10` is `faster`.
Until a measurement validates the headroom model's constants, Automatic keeps exactly those presets.

Once a measurement validates the headroom model's constants, headroom may move an encoder's preset under load.
The server picks a variant's preset when that variant's first job attempt starts.
The server picks the slowest preset in the encoder's range that keeps every running variant at or above the speed floor.
The server keeps that preset for that variant, as <<decision-fixed-preset>> requires.
Each encoder has its own range under Automatic.

* libx264 runs at `faster`.
libx264 moves to `veryfast` only when `faster` would push a running variant below the speed floor.
The server never picks a libx264 preset slower than `faster`.
* libx265 stays at `veryfast` until libx265's seek waits are measured.
* libsvtav1 runs at preset `10`.
libsvtav1 moves to preset `11` only when preset `10` would push a running variant below the speed floor.
Until libsvtav1's seek waits are measured, the server never picks a libsvtav1 preset slower than `10`.

*Why:* The server picks each headroom-driven preset.
The headroom model needs to know which preset each job runs.
The server therefore owns the preset setting.
The job therefore always carries the preset.
On a 10-core arm64 host, with libx264 over four sources and the four-variant ladder, `faster` used 0.59× `medium`'s CPU per media second at 1080p.
VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
At equal VMAF, `faster` needed about 10 % more bitrate than `medium`.
At equal VMAF, `veryfast` needed 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium`.
At 3 s segments, the first 1080p media segment after a seek took 1.28 s with `faster` and 2.09 s with `medium`.
Those two times are medians of 12 runs.
Automatic therefore starts libx264 at `faster` and moves libx264 only toward `veryfast` under load.
A slower preset lengthens the wait for the first media segment after a seek.
The seek waits of libx265 and libsvtav1 have not been measured, so neither encoder runs slower than its Automatic preset.
On a 14-core arm64 host with a synthetic 1080p30 source, libx265 at `veryfast` already spent about three times the CPU of libx264.
SVT-AV1's documentation uses preset 10 as its example for fast and real-time encoding.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-svt-av1>>.

[[decision-preset-scale]]
=== One preset scale serves every software encoder

The preset scale maps each preset name to one preset of every software encoder.
libx264 and libx265 use the name itself.
libsvtav1 uses the number in its column.

[cols="2,2,2,1,4",options="header"]
|===
|Preset name |libx264 |libx265 |libsvtav1 |Role under Automatic

|`ultrafast` |`ultrafast` |`ultrafast` |`13` |Not used
|`superfast` |`superfast` |`superfast` |`12` |Not used
|`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`11` |Automatic for libx265, and the choice under load for libx264 and libsvtav1
|`faster` |`faster` |`faster` |`10` |Automatic for libx264 and libsvtav1
|`fast` |`fast` |`fast` |`9` |Not used
|`medium` |`medium` |`medium` |`8` |Not used
|`slow` |`slow` |`slow` |`7` |Not used
|`slower` |`slower` |`slower` |`6` |Not used
|`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`5` |Not used
|===

"Not used" means that Automatic never picks the preset.
A ServerAdmin can still choose that preset as the fixed preset.
The scale has no name for a libsvtav1 preset below `5`.
A ServerAdmin therefore cannot fix libsvtav1 at a preset below `5`.
A hardware encoder backend gets rows in the preset scale only once that backend is verified.

*Why:* SVT-AV1's documentation reserves presets 1 to 3 for extremely high efficiency when encoding time does not matter.
The documentation reserves presets 4 to 6 for a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
The documentation describes presets 7 to 13 as the range for fast and real-time encoding.
The documentation names 12 as the fastest preset intended for human use.
SVT-AV1's default preset is 8.
The scale places 8 beside `medium`.
`medium` is the default preset of libx264 and libx265.
Evidence: <<evidence-svt-av1>>.

[[decision-fixed-preset]]
=== A variant keeps its preset for its whole life

The preset stays fixed for a variant.
Every replacement attempt of a variant uses the preset of the variant's first job attempt.
Neither Automatic nor the headroom model changes a running variant's preset.
A preset change, whether by a ServerAdmin or by Automatic, reaches only variants that start afterwards.

*Why:* The preset changes the initialization segment.
Under ADR 0037, the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.
Within one preset, variant and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts.
The initialization segment stayed identical with 3 s, 4 s and 6 s segments and after every seek.
The initialization segment differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` for all 16 combinations of variant and source.
Evidence: <<evidence-init>>.

[[decision-seek-priority]]
=== Producers well ahead of their viewers give up CPU to a seek

A producer is the worker module that owns one job attempt's FFmpeg process.
A producer runs ahead of its viewer when the producer has delivered media segments that the viewer's player has not requested yet.
A producer whose encode is faster than real time keeps adding to that lead.
A seek's first segment is the first media segment that a player needs after a seek.
While a seek's first segment is pending on a busy worker, producers that run well ahead of their own viewers give up CPU.
Two mechanisms can make those producers give up CPU.
The first mechanism lowers those producers' CPU priority.
The second mechanism pauses those producers until the seek's first segment is complete.
One proof compares both mechanisms, with real encodes on one worker and three viewers.
The proof then picks one mechanism.
The change that implements the picked mechanism writes the rule into this ADR.
Streamarr holds back a producer that runs far ahead of its viewer with the same mechanism that the proof picks.

*Why:* AVPlayer abandons a segment request for which the server has sent no bytes after one segment length.
A producer that runs well ahead of its viewer does not need the CPU while a seek's first segment is pending.

[[decision-hardware-first]]
=== A hardware encoder backend takes new variants first

An encoder backend is the encoder that a worker actually runs for a codec family, together with whether that encoder is hardware or software.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398] reports which encoder backends each worker has verified.
On a worker that has verified both a hardware encoder backend and the software encoder for the same codec, each new variant encodes with the hardware encoder backend first.
The headroom model predicts each hardware encoder backend's headroom as well as the worker's CPU.
When the headroom model predicts that the hardware encoder backend has no headroom left, each further new variant encodes with the software encoder for the same codec.
libx264 takes those H.264 variants.
libx265 takes those HEVC variants.
libsvtav1 takes those AV1 variants.
A variant keeps its first job attempt's encoder backend for every replacement attempt, as link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] and https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398] require.

VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
Streamarr measures each hardware encoder backend's VMAF against libx264 at `faster`, at the ladder's bitrates.
A hardware encoder backend's bitrates rise only where that measurement shows the backend falling short of libx264.
Streamarr fixes no bitrate margin for a hardware encoder backend in advance.

*Why:* A hardware encoder backend has its own encoding limit.
The worker's CPU does not show that limit.
Automatic gives software H.264 encodes libx264 at `faster`.
The VMAF comparison therefore shows whether a hardware encoder backend matches that software encode at the ladder's bitrates.

== Consequences

* Consider one software worker on a host like the measured one.
On that host, two concurrent `veryfast` ladders slowed the 1080p variants to about real time.
The model therefore leaves little room for extra variants once several users play at once.
Whenever the model predicts that an extra variant would push a running variant below the speed floor, variants start only under ADR 0019's on-demand rules.
* The speed floor limits how many encodes a worker carries.
On a 10-core arm64 host, concurrent 1080p encodes at `faster` each ran at 2.80× real time for two streams and 1.90× for three.
A 2.10× floor therefore admits two such encodes on that host, not three.
AVPlayer's 1.70× floor would have admitted about 24 % more load per worker.
That lower floor would leave Safari's native player below Safari's own floor.
* The server now owns the production speed of each attempt and the constants of each worker.
The server needs segment arrival times to measure each worker's constants.
A worker that returns with the same hardware and FFmpeg build starts from the server's kept calibration.
Only new hardware or a new FFmpeg build delays a worker's first job by a short benchmark.
Free job slots still bound how many jobs a worker accepts.
Headroom decides which extra variants run.
Headroom also decides which preset Automatic picks within an encoder's range.
Headroom also decides when new variants move from a hardware encoder backend to the software encoder for the same codec.
* On a worker with a verified hardware encoder backend, one codec's variants can come from two encoder backends at once.
Each of those variants keeps one encoder backend for every replacement attempt.
* A hardware encoder backend that scores below libx264 at `faster` gets higher bitrates, so the backend's variants cost more bandwidth.
* During a capacity shortfall, a ServerAdmin sees a capacity metric instead of refused playback.
Viewers keep playing, but a quality switch then waits for an on-demand start under ADR 0019.
* Encoders, presets and sources become comparable in one unit, pixel rate.
Metrics can therefore show load against capacity directly.
* A faster preset costs compression efficiency at a given bitrate.
In libx264 at equal VMAF, Automatic's `faster` needs about 10 % more bitrate than `medium`.
Under load, `veryfast` needs 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium`.
Automatic accepts those costs to keep every running variant at or above the speed floor.
* A preset change, whether by a ServerAdmin or by Automatic, reaches only variants that start afterwards.
* Every job carries a preset, even while Automatic keeps its fixed presets.
A ServerAdmin changes the preset on the web ServerAdmin settings page, never in a worker's configuration.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Resolution as the unit of load ignores frame rate and decode cost.
At the same cost per frame, a 1080p60 source costs about 2.5 times a 1080p24 source.
* Preset names as the unit of load mean different costs in different encoders.
At the encoders' own default preset, `medium`, libx265 spends about twice the CPU of libx264 on the same encode.
At `veryfast`, which Automatic uses for libx265, libx265 spends about three times the CPU of libx264.
libsvtav1 numbers its presets instead of naming them.
* A fixed number of variants, or free job slots, cannot track the host, the preset, the content or other sessions.
On the same host, the number of concurrent 1080p encodes that each stayed at 1.2× real time or faster ranged from two at `medium` to six at `veryfast`.
A grain-heavy clip fits proportionally fewer encodes.
* Changing a variant's preset mid-stream changes the variant's initialization segment.
The server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.
A mid-stream preset change would therefore amount to a new variant.
* Keeping 1.35× real time as the whole speed floor protects hls.js but not the Apple players.
At 3 s segments, the derived floors are about 1.70× for AVPlayer and 2.10× for Safari's native player.
* Pacing producers protects no speed by itself.
The server still needs a speed floor to know how fast each running variant must be produced.
* A floor for each client kind would need the server to know which player fetches each variant.
One floor protects hls.js and both Apple players without that knowledge.
* AVPlayer's floor of about 1.70× would admit about 24 % more load per worker.
That lower floor would leave Safari's native player below Safari's own floor of about 2.10×.
* Letting ADR 0019's only-requested stop also stop the variants that the headroom model started would stop every extra variant at the player's next media-segment request.
A quality switch would then never find an extra variant's segments ready.
* Starting extra variants nearest first, with no preference between the variant below and the variant above, leaves the order of two equally near variants undefined.
* Starting every lower variant first makes the variant just above the requested variant wait behind variants further from the requested variant.
* Rewriting https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/402[streamarr/streamarr-server#402] for the headroom model would add a separate rule that removes variants from the advertised ladder when capacity is short.
On-demand start and the headroom model already decide which variants run.
* Refusing new stream sessions during a capacity shortfall takes playback away from new viewers.
Stopping extra variants keeps every viewer playing.
* Starting new stream sessions at a lower quality during a capacity shortfall lowers a new viewer's quality before the server stops any extra variant.
* Benchmarking every worker at every registration, before the worker takes jobs, delays every restart of a worker whose hardware and FFmpeg build have not changed.
* Taking jobs at once with cautious default constants, and benchmarking when the worker is idle, runs a new worker on constants that no measurement supports.
A busy worker may also never be idle.
* A fixed `veryfast` default with a ServerAdmin override ignores the headroom that a worker actually has.
At equal VMAF, `veryfast` also needs 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium`.
* Keeping each encoder's default preset, `medium`, makes the first 1080p media segment after a seek take 2.09 s instead of 1.28 s at 3 s segments.
On the measured host, `medium` also kept only two concurrent 1080p encodes at 1.2× real time or faster.
* Starting libx264 at `faster` with a headroom range from `veryfast` to `medium` would let a worker with spare headroom pick `medium`.
`medium` lengthens the wait for the first media segment after a seek.
* Keeping `veryfast` as libx264's Automatic preset needs 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium` at equal VMAF, against about 10 % for `faster`.
* Using `faster` for 1080p and 720p and `medium` for 480p and 360p adds a preset rule for each resolution.
After a seek at 360p, `medium` still cost about 1.4 times the CPU of `faster`, averaged over four sources.
* Using `fast` for libx264 costs 0.80× `medium`'s CPU at 1080p, against 0.59× for `faster`.
At 6 s segments, `fast` also took 2.58 s to the first 1080p media segment after a seek, against 1.95 s for `faster`.
* Mapping Automatic to libsvtav1 preset `11`, so that `veryfast` names Automatic for every encoder, moves libsvtav1 off preset `10`.
SVT-AV1's documentation gives preset `10` as its example for fast and real-time encoding.
* Keeping libsvtav1's headroom range from `10` down to `8` would let libsvtav1 run slower than `10` before libsvtav1's seek waits are measured.
* A preset setting on the worker would hide each job's preset from the headroom model, which needs that preset.
* Applying seek priority only when a worker accepts a new job leaves running producers untouched.
Those running producers hold the CPU that a seek's first segment needs.
* The same ladder bitrates for every encoder, hardware or software, would give a lower picture quality wherever a hardware encoder backend scores below libx264 at `faster`.
* Sending only some jobs to a hardware encoder backend leaves the hardware encoder backend's headroom unused while software encodes take the worker's CPU.
* A ServerAdmin setting on each worker that picks hardware or software asks the ServerAdmin to make a choice that the headroom model can predict.

== Evidence

Each group below holds the measurements behind one part of the Decision.
Unless a group says otherwise, the measurements ran on a 10-core arm64 host, in the pinned worker image with FFmpeg 8.1.2 and libx264.
The ladder was 1080p at 5 Mb/s, 720p at 3 Mb/s, 480p at 1.5 Mb/s and 360p at 800 kb/s, with 6 s segments.
The sources were a 24 fps live-action film, a 30 fps animation, a grain-heavy clip at 25 fps and a dark clip at 24 fps.
Unless a measurement names another preset, libx264 ran at its default preset, `medium`.
These measurements do not describe 3 s segments or the presets that Automatic picks.
A measurement that ran at 3 s segments says so.

[[evidence-contention]]
=== Contention across variants

* 170 timed runs compared each variant alone with all four variants together, both after a seek and at first play.
* With all four together, every variant took 2.78 to 3.22 times as long to complete its first segment.
After a seek, the first 1080p segment took 9.0 s instead of 2.9 s.
* One variant alone already used 6.9 to 8.7 of the 10 cores.
* While all four ran, 1080p was produced at 0.94× to 1.06× real time, against 2.96× to 2.99× alone.
720p was produced at 1.64× to 1.87×, against 5.41× to 5.45× alone.
* An independent re-run reproduced the slowdown ratios at 2.95 to 3.22.
The re-run measured 1080p at 1.06× to 1.08× real time with all four variants running.
* 36 simulated sessions compared restarting all four variants with restarting only the requested variant.
The sessions ran in hls.js 1.7.0 in Chromium and Firefox, in WebKit's native player, and in AVPlayer on macOS 26.6.2.
ADR 0019 gives the playback results of those sessions.
* When all four variants restarted, hls.js made 12 to 16 emergency aborts per session, about 6 s apart.
hls.js also reversed its choice of variant 30 to 34 times per session.
* In a control run, every variant ran at its single-variant speed.
The control run produced no cycle of emergency aborts.
The abort cycle therefore comes from the production speed.

[[evidence-floor]]
=== The speed floor

* The 1.35× part of the speed floor comes from the source code of hls.js 1.7.0.
An independent reading of that source code confirmed the 1.35× figure.
* hls.js keeps a variant while 0.95 × the hls.js throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
hls.js switches up at 0.7 × that estimate.
* When a segment load receives no byte for half a segment duration or more, and hls.js predicts that the load will not finish before the player's buffer runs out, hls.js abandons that load.
hls.js then resets its throughput estimate to the `BANDWIDTH` of the lower variant that hls.js switches to.
* A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate.
With the hls.js rule for keeping a variant and with `BANDWIDTH` at 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`, that download speed gives 1.33× to 1.37× for this ladder.
* The runs measured 15 sets of running variants on the animation.
1080p fell below 1.35× in only two sets.
1080p ran at 1.06× with all four variants, and at 1.19× with 720p and 480p.
The set with 720p and 480p measured 1.39× in the independent re-run.
* The Apple floors are derived, not measured.
On the 10-core arm64 host, two users and then three users each started a seek at the same time.
Each seek started a 1080p libx264 job attempt at `faster`.
The runs used 3 s and 4 s segments, with 3 runs for each combination.
* AVPlayer abandons a segment request for which the server has sent no bytes after one segment length.
Safari's native player switches down when a held segment request waits longer than the player allows.
* For each Apple player, a fit of the measured waits against each stream's measured production speed gives one speed.
At that speed, the slowest stream's first media segment after a seek arrives just at that player's limit.
The derived floors are adjusted to 250 ms fragments.
At 3 s segments, the fit gives 1.64× for AVPlayer and 2.01× for Safari's native player.
* A real server and worker started a 1080p variant at a new position 20 times, at 3 s segments.
The player saw about 0.1 s on top of the worker's own production time.
Other work shared the host, so 0.1 s is an upper bound.
Adding 0.1 s raises the floors to about 1.70× for AVPlayer and 2.10× for Safari's native player.
At 4 s segments, the floors would be about 1.49× and 1.85×.
* 1.35× decides the speed floor only when the neediest Apple floor is lower than 1.35×.
No derived Apple floor at 3 s or 4 s segments is lower than 1.35×.
* On the same host, concurrent 1080p encodes at `faster` each ran at 2.80× real time for two streams, 1.90× for three and 1.42× for four.
Those speeds are measured, with 3 runs for each load.
A 2.10× floor therefore admits two concurrent 1080p seeks on that host.
AVPlayer's 1.70× floor would have admitted three.
Both counts are derived.
* From the ratio of the two floors, AVPlayer's lower floor would admit about 24 % more load per worker.
That figure is derived.
The 2.10× floor protects both Apple players.

[[evidence-presets]]
=== Preset cost and capacity

* After a seek at 1080p, CPU per media second relative to `medium`, averaged over four sources, was 0.80 for `fast`, 0.59 for `faster`, 0.38 for `veryfast` and 0.28 for `superfast`.
At 360p the same presets cost 0.86, 0.72, 0.59 and 0.53.
* At 1080p, `medium` needed 2.8 to 4.3 CPU seconds per media second.
`veryfast` needed 1.0 to 1.6.
The grain-heavy clip was the costliest source in every cell, at 1.3 to 1.9 times the 24 fps film.
* Over 12 runs, the median time to the first 1080p media segment after a seek was 3.18 s for `medium`, 2.58 s for `fast`, 1.95 s for `faster`, 1.35 s for `veryfast` and 0.99 s for `superfast`.
* At 3 s segments, over 12 runs, the median time to the first 1080p media segment after a seek was 2.09 s for `medium` and 1.28 s for `faster`.
* The most concurrent 1080p encodes of the 24 fps film that all stayed at 1.2× or faster were 2 at `medium`, 3 at `fast`, 4 at `faster`, 6 at `veryfast` and 8 at `superfast`.
* No full four-variant ladder kept 1080p at real time at `medium`.
One ladder did at `fast`.
At `veryfast`, two ladders did with no margin, and one ladder did with margin.
* On the 24 fps film, one ladder used 6.11 CPU seconds per media second at `medium` and 2.61 at `veryfast`.
* On a 14-core arm64 host with a synthetic 1080p30 source, libx265 ran at 53 fps at its default preset and 82 fps at `veryfast`.
libx264 ran at 110 fps at its default preset and 251 fps at `veryfast`.
libsvtav1 ran at 98 fps at its default preset and 222 fps at `10`.
* 364 encodes of 30 s excerpts from the four sources measured each preset's quality against `medium`.
VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
At equal VMAF, each preset needed more bitrate than `medium`: about 8 % more for `fast`, 10 % for `faster`, 34 % to 39 % for `veryfast` and 65 % to 68 % for `superfast`.
At the ladder's bitrates, `veryfast` scored about 3 VMAF points below `medium`.
On the grain-heavy clip, `veryfast` scored up to 7 VMAF points below `medium`.
The pinned worker image has no libvmaf.
A separate FFmpeg 8.0.1 build with libvmaf 3.0.0 therefore scored the encodes.

[[evidence-model]]
=== Model fit

* With constants fitted per source and preset on the steady-state runs, the model predicted the CPU per media second of each libx264 variant within about 7 %.
* One set of constants fitted across all four sources missed individual ladders by up to about 25 %.
The continuous measurement of each worker's constants absorbs that error.
* Fewer ladders ran with 1080p at real time than the CPU budget from the fitted costs allows.
At `veryfast`, 10 cores held 3.8 ladders of the 24 fps film on paper.
Only two of those ladders ran with 1080p at real time.
* In every ladder run, each FFmpeg process received an equal share of the cores.
The 1080p variant was always the slowest variant.
In the single-ladder runs, the 360p variant ran at 3.6× to 4.2× real time.

[[evidence-tonemap]]
=== Tone-mapping cost

* These runs used 60 fps HDR sources, libx264 and libsvtav1, and an 8-bit tone-mapping chain with the BT.2390 curve.
The cost of the curve that Streamarr chooses is measured again when tone mapping is implemented.
* Tone mapping added no measurable CPU on PQ and HLG sources at 1080p.
Tone mapping added 0.4 to 0.9 CPU seconds per media second on 2160p PQ and HLG sources.
* The Dolby Vision profile 5 reshape added about 1.1 to 1.2 CPU seconds per media second at 1080p60.
The reshape added 1.9 to 2.0 CPU seconds per media second at 2160p60.
* With tone mapping, one 1080p variant of a 60 fps 2160p source ran at 0.90× to 1.01× real time with libx264 and at 0.81× to 0.94× with libsvtav1.
Each encoder ran at its default preset.
Without tone mapping, the same variant ran at 1.05× to 1.14× with libx264 and at 0.87× to 1.00× with libsvtav1.
At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film would encode about 2.5 times faster relative to real time.

[[evidence-hardware-decode]]
=== Hardware decoding

* These runs used an RTX 5090 in the pinned worker image.
* One 1080p libx264 replacement attempt at `faster` covered 421 frames.
Each figure is the median of 5 runs.
* The attempt used 16.47 CPU seconds with NVDEC decode and 19.26 s with software decode.
NVDEC decode therefore cut the attempt's CPU by 14 %.
* With NVDEC, decoding alone fell from 2.57 CPU seconds to 0.14 s.

[[evidence-init]]
=== Initialization segments and presets

* Within one preset, variant and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts.
The initialization segment stayed identical with 3 s, 4 s and 6 s segments and after every seek.
* Across presets, the initialization segment differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` for all 16 combinations of variant and source.
* In the pinned libx264, `veryfast` and `superfast` produced identical initialization segments.
That match is a coincidence of libx264's current preset tables.
This decision does not rely on that match.
* In 592 of 592 attempts, every preset placed keyframes exactly at the forced keyframe times.
* Every preset passed the junction check at 20 of 20 junctions.
The junction check compares a replacement attempt's first segments with the first attempt's segments at the same positions.
The segments must open at the same times, hold the same frame times and show the same source frames.

[[evidence-svt-av1]]
=== SVT-AV1's preset guidance

* SVT-AV1 accepts presets −1 to 13.
SVT-AV1 reserves negative presets for debugging.
SVT-AV1's default preset is 8.
* SVT-AV1's documentation calls presets 1 to 3 extremely high efficiency.
The documentation calls presets 4 to 6 a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
The documentation calls presets 7 to 13 fast and real-time encoding.
* For fast or real-time encoding, the documentation says the preset must be high enough that the CPU encodes without stuttering.
The documentation gives preset 10 as its example.
* SVT-AV1 names 13 as its highest preset.
SVT-AV1 names 12 as the highest preset intended for human use.
