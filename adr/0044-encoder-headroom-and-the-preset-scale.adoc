= ADR 0044: Encoder Headroom, Measured in Pixel Rate, Decides When Extra Variants Start, and One Preset Scale Serves Every Software Encoder

== Status

Proposed

This decision fills in the capacity policy that link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018] left to follow a working path.
It builds on link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019], which starts and restarts the jobs that produce each variant and starts only the requested variant unless this ADR's policy allows more.
It relies on the rule in link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] that the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's.

== Context

In this ADR, a variant is one HLS variant of a stream session's quality ladder, in the sense of RFC 8216's `EXT-X-STREAM-INF`.
A variant is never an HLS rendition, which is an `EXT-X-MEDIA` audio or subtitle track.
Production speed is the media seconds that a job attempt produces per second of wall-clock time.
A variant produced at 2× real time delivers a 6 s media segment every 3 s.

Before this decision, the server started every variant of a session together whenever the worker had free job slots.
A worker with four slots and no hardware encoder therefore ran four software encodes at once.
The worker passed no `-preset`, so libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1 each ran at their library's default preset.
Those defaults suit offline encoding and are slow for live transcoding.

Measured on a 10-core arm64 host with libx264 at its default preset, four variants at once made every variant about three times slower.
The 1080p variant then came out at about real time, which is too slow for a player to keep.
Free job slots say nothing about how fast each job will run.
The server needs a measure of the encoding load that a host can carry, and a rule for spending it.

Streamarr can serve several users from one worker at once.
Headroom that one session's extra variants spend is headroom that another user's requested variant may need.

The preset also decides how much load each variant costs.
libx264 and libx265 name their presets with words from `ultrafast` to `veryslow`, while libsvtav1 numbers its presets.
An administrator needs one name that means the same trade-off for every software encoder.

== Decision

[[decision-pixel-rate]]
=== Pixel rate is the unit of encoding load

Pixel rate is width × height × frames per second.
Streamarr expresses encoding load in pixel rate wherever it models, compares or reports that load.
The output pixel rate uses a variant's output size and output frame rate.
The source pixel rate uses the source video stream's decoded size and frame rate.
Encoding load is never expressed as a resolution or as a preset name.

*Why:* At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film encodes about 2.5 times faster relative to real time than a 60 fps source of the same size.
A resolution cannot tell those two apart.
A faster preset saved less at small variants: `superfast` cost 0.28× `medium`'s CPU at 1080p but 0.53× at 360p, because decoding the 1080p source does not shrink with the output.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-tonemap>>.

[[decision-cost-model]]
=== A cost model predicts the load, and capacity bounds it

The cost of one job attempt, in core-seconds per media second, is:

* a(encoder, preset) × output pixel rate,
* plus d(source codec) × source pixel rate,
* plus the cost of tone mapping when the attempt tone-maps.

The encode coefficient `a` depends on the encoder and its preset.
The decode coefficient `d` depends on the source's codec.
The decode term counts once per FFmpeg process, because every variant's job decodes the source on its own.
A worker's capacity is its cores × an efficiency factor, minus the load the host carries besides that worker's encodes.
The efficiency factor is below one because the operating system shares cores equally between FFmpeg processes, so a large variant falls behind while small ones run ahead.

*Why:* With constants fitted per source and preset, the model predicted each measured libx264 variant's CPU within about 7 %.
One four-variant ladder at `medium` needed 6.1 core-seconds per media second, so 10 cores held 1.6 ladders on paper.
Yet a single ladder's 1080p variant ran at only 0.93× real time, while its 360p variant ran at 3.6× or faster.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-threshold]]
=== A variant holds in hls.js at about 1.35× real time

A variant holds in a player when the player keeps fetching it instead of switching down.
A variant holds in hls.js when it is produced at about 1.35× real time or faster.
The eager-start rule and Automatic both protect this speed for every running variant.

*Why:* hls.js 1.7.0 keeps a variant while 0.95 × its throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate, and the server advertises `BANDWIDTH` as 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`.
Those two rules give a threshold of 1.33× to 1.37× across the measured ladder.
With four variants running, 1080p came out at 0.94× to 1.06×.
hls.js then made 12 to 16 emergency aborts per session and showed mostly 360p on a 25 Mb/s link.
Evidence: <<evidence-threshold>>, <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-calibration]]
=== The server calibrates the model online

The server derives each job attempt's production speed from the arrival times of its media segments.
It fits the model's constants to those speeds while sessions play.
A benchmark that the worker runs may seed the constants, so that a new worker does not start from nothing.
Seeding is optional.

*Why:* The same preset cost different amounts on different content.
A grain-heavy clip cost 1.3× to 1.9× as much per media second as a 24 fps film at every variant.
A single set of constants fitted across four sources missed individual ladders by up to about a quarter.
A repeated run of one set of three variants measured 1.39× where the first run measured 1.19×.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-eager-start]]
=== Extra variants start only while every session keeps its headroom

A requested variant is the variant whose segment a player asks for.
An extra variant is any other variant of the same session.
The server starts extra variants eagerly only when the model predicts that every running variant stays at about 1.35× real time or faster with them running.
That prediction must leave room for several users' concurrent encodes.
One session's extra variants never slow another session's requested variant below 1.35×.
Otherwise ADR 0019's rule applies: only the requested variant starts or restarts.
Until the model is validated, ADR 0019's rule applies unconditionally.

*Why:* Four variants running together pushed 1080p down to 0.94× to 1.06× real time, below the hold threshold.
An extra variant is worth starting only when it leaves every running variant above that threshold.
ADR 0019 gives the player measurements behind starting only the requested variant.
Evidence: <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-preset-setting]]
=== The preset is Automatic unless an administrator fixes one

A preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
Streamarr has one preset setting whose default is Automatic.
An administrator can override Automatic with one fixed preset.
Today Automatic means `veryfast` for libx264 and libx265, and `10` for libsvtav1.

Once the headroom model is validated, Automatic becomes headroom-driven.
The server then picks each variant's preset at session start, and the job carries it to the worker.
Automatic picks the slowest preset from `veryfast` to `medium` that keeps every running variant at about 1.35× real time or faster.
For libsvtav1 that range runs from `10` to `8`.

*Why:* After a seek, `veryfast` used 0.38× `medium`'s CPU per media second at 1080p.
The first 1080p media segment of a 6 s period completed in 1.35 s with `veryfast` and 3.18 s with `medium`.
Six concurrent 1080p encodes of a 24 fps film each stayed at 1.2× real time or faster with `veryfast`, but only two did with `medium`.
SVT-AV1's documentation uses preset 10 as its example for fast and real-time encoding.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-svt-av1>>.

[[decision-preset-scale]]
=== One preset scale serves every software encoder

The preset scale maps each preset name to one preset of every software encoder.
libx264 and libx265 use the name itself.
libsvtav1 uses the number in its column.

[cols="2,2,2,1,4",options="header"]
|===
|Preset name |libx264 |libx265 |libsvtav1 |Role under Automatic

|`ultrafast` |`ultrafast` |`ultrafast` |`13` |Not used
|`superfast` |`superfast` |`superfast` |`12` |Not used
|`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`11` |Automatic today for libx264 and libx265, and the fastest headroom-driven choice for them
|`faster` |`faster` |`faster` |`10` |Automatic today for libsvtav1, and its fastest headroom-driven choice
|`fast` |`fast` |`fast` |`9` |Inside the headroom-driven range for all three encoders
|`medium` |`medium` |`medium` |`8` |The slowest headroom-driven choice for all three encoders
|`slow` |`slow` |`slow` |`7` |Not used
|`slower` |`slower` |`slower` |`6` |Not used
|`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`5` |Not used
|===

"Not used" means that Automatic never picks the preset; an administrator can still fix it.
libsvtav1 presets below `5` are excluded from the scale because they are too slow for live encoding.
Hardware encoders get rows only when streamarr/streamarr-server#398 verifies them.

*Why:* SVT-AV1's documentation reserves presets 1 to 3 for extremely high efficiency when encoding time does not matter, and presets 4 to 6 for a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
It describes presets 7 to 13 as the range for fast and real-time encoding, with 12 as the fastest intended for human use.
Its default preset is 8, which the scale places beside `medium`, the default of libx264 and libx265.
Evidence: <<evidence-svt-av1>>.

[[decision-fixed-preset]]
=== A variant keeps its preset for its whole life

The preset stays fixed for a variant.
Every replacement attempt of a variant uses the preset of the variant's first job attempt.
Neither Automatic nor the headroom model changes a running variant's preset.

*Why:* The preset changes the initialization segment, and the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs (ADR 0037).
Within one preset, level and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts, three segment periods and every seek.
It differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` in 16 of 16 pairs of the same level and source.
Evidence: <<evidence-init>>.

=== Implementation

Worker issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/23[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#23] passes an explicit preset to every software encoder and adds the Automatic setting with its administrator override.
The encoder-headroom issue under epic https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/399[streamarr/streamarr-server#399] adds pixel rate, the cost model, online calibration and the eager-start rule.
The headroom-driven preset is the last step of that issue.

[[explicitly-deferred]]
=== Explicitly deferred

* Rows for hardware encoders in the preset scale, and their constants and session limits in the model (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398]).
* The validated model and its constants.
This decision fixes the model's form, not the values of `a`, `d`, the tone-mapping cost or the efficiency factor.
The measurements cover libx264 only; libx265 and libsvtav1 presets have not been measured on a ladder.
* The rate-control choices, including any CRF values.
The quality that each preset gives up at a fixed bitrate is still being measured.
* Which variants a ladder includes when capacity is short (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/402[streamarr/streamarr-server#402]).

== Consequences

* With one software worker on a host like the one measured and today's Automatic, the model will not let every variant of a ladder run at 1.35×.
Only the requested variant starts or restarts in that case.
* The server now owns production speed per attempt and a per-worker model, and it needs segment arrival times to calibrate it.
Free job slots still bound how many jobs a worker accepts; headroom decides only whether extra variants start.
* Encoders, presets and sources become comparable in one unit, so metrics can show load against capacity directly.
* A faster preset costs compression efficiency at a given bitrate.
Automatic accepts that cost today to keep variants above the hold threshold.
* A preset change, whether by the administrator or by Automatic, reaches only variants that start afterwards.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Resolution as the unit of load ignores frame rate and decode cost.
A 1080p60 source costs about 2.5 times a 1080p24 source at the same cost per frame.
* Preset names as the unit of load mean different costs in different encoders.
libx265 ran at about half libx264's speed at their default presets, and libsvtav1 numbers its presets.
* A fixed number of variants, or free job slots, cannot track the host, the preset, the content or other sessions.
Concurrent 1080p encodes at 1.2× or faster ranged from two at `medium` to six at `veryfast` on the same host.
A grain-heavy clip fits proportionally fewer.
* Changing a variant's preset mid-stream changes its initialization segment, which the server refuses for a replacement attempt.
It would amount to a new variant.

== Evidence

Each group below holds the measurements behind one part of the Decision.
Unless a group says otherwise, the measurements ran on a 10-core arm64 host, in the pinned worker image with FFmpeg 8.1.2 and libx264.
The ladder was 1080p at 5 Mb/s, 720p at 3 Mb/s, 480p at 1.5 Mb/s and 360p at 800 kb/s, with 6 s segments.
The sources were a 24 fps live-action film, a 30 fps animation, a grain-heavy clip at 25 fps and a dark clip at 24 fps.

[[evidence-contention]]
=== Contention across variants

* 170 timed runs compared each variant alone with all four together after a seek and at first play.
* With all four together, every variant took 2.78 to 3.22 times as long to complete its first segment.
A 1080p seek took 9.0 s instead of 2.9 s.
* One variant alone already used 6.9 to 8.7 of the 10 cores.
* While all four ran, 1080p came out at 0.94× to 1.06× real time, against 2.96× to 2.99× alone.
720p came out at 1.64× to 1.87×, against 5.41× to 5.45× alone.
* An independent re-run reproduced the ratios at 2.95 to 3.22 and the all-four 1080p speed at 1.06× to 1.08×.
* 36 simulated sessions in hls.js 1.7.0 in Chromium and Firefox, WebKit's native player and AVPlayer on macOS 26.6.2 compared restarting all four variants with restarting only the requested one; ADR 0019 gives their playback results.
* With all four, hls.js made 12 to 16 emergency aborts about 6 s apart, and 30 to 34 reversals of the fetched variant per session.
* A control with every variant at its single-variant speed produced no abort cycle, so the cycle comes from the production speed.

[[evidence-threshold]]
=== The hold threshold

* The threshold is derived from hls.js 1.7.0's source and checked by an independent reading.
* hls.js keeps a variant while 0.95 × its estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`, and switches up at 0.7 × its estimate.
* After half a segment or more without a byte, hls.js abandons the load and resets its estimate to the lower variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
* A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate, which gives a threshold of 1.33× to 1.37× for this ladder.
* Of 15 measured sets of running variants on the animation, only two fell below the threshold: 1080p with all four (1.06×) and 1080p with 720p and 480p (1.19×).
That second set measured 1.39× in the independent re-run.

[[evidence-presets]]
=== Preset cost and capacity

* CPU per media second after a seek, relative to `medium` and averaged over four sources: `fast` 0.80, `faster` 0.59, `veryfast` 0.38 and `superfast` 0.28 at 1080p.
At 360p the same presets cost 0.86, 0.72, 0.59 and 0.53.
* At 1080p, `medium` needed 2.8 to 4.3 CPU seconds per media second and `veryfast` needed 1.0 to 1.6.
The grain-heavy clip was the costliest source in every cell, at 1.3 to 1.9 times the 24 fps film.
* Time to the first 1080p media segment after a seek, median of 12: `medium` 3.18 s, `fast` 2.58 s, `faster` 1.95 s, `veryfast` 1.35 s, `superfast` 0.99 s.
* Concurrent 1080p encodes of the 24 fps film with every stream at 1.2× or faster: `medium` 2, `fast` 3, `faster` 4, `veryfast` 6, `superfast` 8.
* Full four-variant ladders with 1080p at real time: none at `medium`, one at `fast`, two at `veryfast` with no margin, and one with margin.
* Ladder CPU per media second on the 24 fps film: `medium` 6.11, `veryfast` 2.61.
* On a 14-core arm64 host with a synthetic 1080p30 source, libx265 ran at 53 fps at its default preset and 82 fps at `veryfast`.
libx264 ran at 110 and 251 fps, and libsvtav1 at 98 fps at its default preset and 222 fps at `10`.
* Pending: the bitrate each preset needs for the same VMAF as `medium` is still being measured.

[[evidence-model]]
=== Model fit

* Fitted per source and preset on the steady-state runs, the model predicted each libx264 variant's CPU per media second within about 7 %.
* One set of constants fitted across all four sources missed individual ladders by up to about 25 %, which the per-worker online calibration absorbs.
* Measured ladders stayed below the CPU budget that the fitted costs give.
At `veryfast`, 10 cores held 3.8 ladders of the 24 fps film on paper, but only two ran with 1080p at real time.

[[evidence-tonemap]]
=== Tone-mapping cost

* These runs used 60 fps HDR sources, libx264 and libsvtav1, with an 8-bit tone-mapping chain using the BT.2390 curve.
The cost of the chosen curve is re-measured when tone mapping lands.
* Tone mapping added no measurable CPU on PQ and HLG sources at 1080p.
It added 0.4 to 0.9 CPU seconds per media second on 2160p PQ and HLG sources.
* The Dolby Vision profile 5 reshape added about 1.1 to 1.2 CPU seconds per media second at 1080p60 and 1.9 to 2.0 at 2160p60.
* One 1080p variant of a 60 fps 2160p source ran at 0.81× to 1.01× real time, at or below real time even without tone mapping.
A 24 fps film would encode about 2.5 times faster relative to real time at the same cost per frame.

[[evidence-init]]
=== Initialization segments and presets

* Within one preset, level and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts, across 3 s, 4 s and 6 s periods, and after every seek.
* Across presets it differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` in all 16 pairs of level and source.
* `veryfast` and `superfast` produced identical initialization segments in the pinned libx264, a coincidence of its current preset tables that this decision does not rely on.
* Every preset kept keyframes exactly on the forced list in 592 of 592 attempts and passed the junction check at 20 of 20 junctions.

[[evidence-svt-av1]]
=== SVT-AV1's preset guidance

* SVT-AV1 accepts presets −1 to 13, reserves negative presets for debugging, and defaults to 8.
* Its documentation calls presets 1 to 3 extremely high efficiency, presets 4 to 6 a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time, and presets 7 to 13 fast and real-time encoding.
* For fast or real-time encoding it says the preset must be high enough that the CPU encodes without stuttering, and gives preset 10 as its example.
* It names 13 as the highest preset and 12 as the highest intended for human use.
