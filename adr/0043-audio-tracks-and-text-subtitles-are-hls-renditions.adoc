= ADR 0043: Audio Tracks and Text Subtitles Are HLS Renditions, so Switching Them Never Restarts the Video

== Status

Accepted

This decision amends link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
A variant's media segments carry video alone instead of multiplexed video and audio.
Multiplexed variants remain in use until the server delivers audio renditions.
Each audio rendition gets its own initialization segment.
Every running variant's job attempts write the audio renditions.
The server keeps the first delivery of each audio segment.
Audio before media time zero is dropped in audio renditions (for AAC, the priming frame).
Multiplexed variant segments keep that audio.
Audio renditions and WebVTT segments follow the stream session's segment list from ADR 0037.
This decision extends link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013].
The stream decision selects every audio track and chooses the subtitle tracks.
Each audio track keeps its own copy-or-encode decision inside one audio codec per stream session.
An image subtitle track that the viewer selects is burned into the video for a client without a subtitle layer.

Another amendment settles languages and how clients draw subtitles.
Each Profile has a preferred audio language and a preferred subtitle language, and the Profile's overrides for an episode or movie, a season or a series come first (<<decision-languages>>).
A language that the viewer switches to during playback becomes the Profile's override for that series.
Clients draw subtitles in a layer above the platform's player, from the original subtitle data that the server sends (<<decision-subtitle-layer>>).
Burn-in and WebVTT renditions stay the fallback for a client without that layer.
A subtitle file beside the source file is a subtitle track of that media file.
Streamarr does not download subtitles.
A night mode and a dialogue boost stay deferred, because the viewer's platform already offers them (<<explicitly-deferred>>).

An amendment after an audiophile review sets how the worker treats samples.
Only an encode that writes every decoded sample unchanged is called lossless.
Every audio encode joins its job attempts on its audio frame grid, and the producer never moves a copied track (<<decision-audio-grid>>).
The worker decodes AC-3 and E-AC-3 without their dynamic range compression and with their dialogue normalization, dithers every conversion to 16 bits, and resamples with tuned settings (<<decision-sample-processing>>).
A FLAC or ALAC encode pads a layout that its codec does not carry with silent channels, a FLAC or ALAC rendition above 48 kHz has a 48 kHz step, and Opus encodes have measured rates (<<decision-audio-codec>>).
A later amendment changes what the default track becomes (<<decision-audio-codec>>).
A TrueHD or DTS default track keeps its copy wherever the client declares the codec, and a track that cannot reach that codec is offered by replacing the stream session.
A default track that cannot be copied, lossy or lossless, is encoded to FLAC wherever the client declares FLAC.
Among the tracks of the default track's language, a lossless copy comes first, then a FLAC encode of a lossless track, then a copy of a lossy track that keeps as many channels as any lossy encode, then a lossy encode.
A FLAC copy needs a block size that the client accepts, and a copied E-AC-3 track with Atmos is marked `CHANNELS="16/JOC"` (<<decision-audio-renditions>>).
A title marks a commentary or an audio description track when no flag does (<<decision-names>>).
ADR 0054's bitrate scan measures the FLAC encodes that a stream session can deliver.
Mac browser checks gate the tickets, and the TV checks run once at the end of the epic (<<decision-device-checks>>).

== Context

This section describes the system before this decision.

The server delivers one audio track.
Every variant's media segments multiplex that track with the video.
The stream decision (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013]) decides how each source stream reaches the client.
The stream decision reads only the source's first audio stream.
The worker maps that stream beside the first video stream.
To map a stream is to tell FFmpeg to include the stream in its output.
The multivariant playlist lists one audio `EXT-X-MEDIA` tag with no URI.
The subtitle decision always excludes subtitles, so the worker drops every subtitle stream.
No code burns a subtitle into the video.
Clients can already send an audio language and a subtitle language when they start a stream session.
The server ignores both languages.
The persisted probe (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]) records each stream's index, codec, language, channel count, and default and forced flags.
The probe records no stream title.
Besides the default and forced flags, the probe records no disposition flag, such as commentary or hearing impaired.
The probe records neither each stream's end time nor whether the video carries captions.
This decision depends on those missing facts.
Probe version 4 adds the titles, the handler names, the hearing-impaired flags and the end times, and a later ticket adds caption detection (<<decision-probe-facts>>).

Every variant multiplexes the one audio track.
So a switch to another language would need new job attempts for every variant.
The viewer would see the video restart.
link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018] and link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036] use the term variant for an option in the quality ladder.
Those ADRs reserve the term rendition for an alternate HLS track, such as an audio language.
Under RFC 8216, the multivariant playlist lists each rendition in an `EXT-X-MEDIA` tag.
A player switches renditions without leaving its variant.

link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] has the worker read fragmented MP4 (fMP4) from FFmpeg's standard output.
The worker rebuilds every media segment on a fixed grid.
Media time is presentation time measured from the source container's start time.
On the grid, segment `N` covers media time `[N × period, (N + 1) × period)`, where period is the segment duration.
An amendment to ADR 0037 replaces the fixed grid with one segment list per stream session.
The segment list fixes the media-time interval of each media segment.
Every variant of the stream session shares that list.
In a stream session that copies the video, one media segment can span several of the list's 3 s slots.
Under link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037], the server serves each variant as multiplexed fMP4.
Each variant has one initialization segment, or init.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/60[streamarr/streamarr-server#60] asks for text subtitles as WebVTT, in the server and in the worker.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/60[streamarr/streamarr-server#60] left open whether a variant's job or a separate job produces the WebVTT subtitles.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-apple/issues/4[streamarr/streamarr-apple#4] asks for audio and subtitle menus in the Apple client.

== Decision

[[decision-audio-renditions]]
=== Every audio track is its own audio rendition

The stream decision selects every audio track in the media file, except a track whose `dependent` disposition is set.
Each selected audio track becomes its own HLS audio rendition.
A rendition is an alternate track that the multivariant playlist lists in an `EXT-X-MEDIA` tag.
Each rendition has its own media playlist.
A rendition is never a variant, and a variant is never a rendition.
The multivariant playlist lists one `EXT-X-MEDIA:TYPE=AUDIO` tag per audio rendition.
All these tags belong to one group.
Every variant references that group.
Every variant's media segments carry video alone, whether the worker copies the video stream or encodes it.
A viewer switches audio language by selecting another rendition in the player.
The video keeps playing.
The switch starts no job attempt.

The stream decision (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013]) also chooses which rendition is the default.
The default track is never a commentary, an audio description or a music-and-effects track while another track remains, whether a disposition or the title marks it (<<decision-names>>).
Among the remaining tracks, it is the first in stream order whose `default` disposition is set, or the first in stream order when none has it.
The ranking of <<decision-audio-codec>> can then let another track of the same language play as the default.
A dependent track, such as narration alone that a receiver mixes into the main track, stays out of the renditions for now, because played alone it carries only the narration.
An audio rendition's `NAME` follows the naming rules of <<decision-names>>, applied among the file's audio tracks.
Each `EXT-X-STREAM-INF` tag's `CODECS` attribute lists the stream session's one audio codec (<<decision-audio-codec>>).
Each `EXT-X-STREAM-INF` tag's `BANDWIDTH` attribute counts the largest audio rendition.
RFC 8216 requires both rules.
Each audio rendition's `CHANNELS` attribute gives the channel count that the rendition delivers.
A copied E-AC-3 track whose profile name contains "Dolby Atmos" gives `CHANNELS="16/JOC"` instead, and its `CODECS` value stays `ec-3`.
An encode of such a track gives its channel count, because the encode loses the objects.

*Why:* In all five players tested, a switch between audio renditions never restarted or rewound the video.
The players were Chromium, Firefox and WebKit with hls.js 1.7.0, WebKit's native HLS, and AVPlayer.
Chromium played the new language after about 0.3 s.
Firefox played the new language after about 1.0 s.
Both delays came from the browsers' audio output queues.
A rendition for every audio track lets the viewer switch to any language without a new stream session.
Apple's HLS authoring specification marks E-AC-3 with Atmos by `JOC` in the rendition's `CHANNELS` attribute, for example `CHANNELS="16/JOC"`, and keeps `ec-3` in `CODECS`.
It asks for the number to match the `complexity_index_type_a` field of the track's `dec3` box, but FFmpeg 8.1.3 wrote no such field for FATE's Atmos sample, so the server writes 16, the specification's example.
Evidence: <<evidence-switching>>.

[[decision-audio-codec]]
=== A stream session has one audio codec

A stream session has one audio codec, and every audio rendition of the stream session uses that codec.
The session's audio codec is the codec of the default track as delivered.
The client capability declaration is the record of the combinations that a player and its delivery path can accept.
A track can be copied only when one audio capability entry for HLS accepts the track's codec and channel count (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc[ADR 0049]).

The server encodes audio to AAC, AC-3, E-AC-3, FLAC, Opus and ALAC.
The default track decides the session's audio codec:

* When the default track can be copied, the default track is copied, and the session's audio codec is the default track's codec.
This holds for a TrueHD or DTS default track too, although the server has no encoder for either codec (below).
* When the default track cannot be copied, the default track is encoded, and the session's audio codec is the codec of that encode.
The default track, lossless or lossy, is encoded to FLAC when a FLAC capability entry for HLS accepts the channel count of the FLAC layout that the layout rules below give the track.
A lossless track is TrueHD, DTS-HD MA, PCM, FLAC or ALAC.
Otherwise the encode follows link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc[ADR 0050]'s encode for the default track.
It takes the first of E-AC-3, AC-3 and AAC whose capability entry for HLS keeps more than two of the track's channels.
When none does, it takes AAC with at most two channels.
When the viewer's maximum bitrate leaves no room for the FLAC encode, ADR 0050's audio steps take that E-AC-3, AC-3 or AAC encode instead.

[[decision-sibling-ranking]]
Before these rules apply, the stream decision chooses which track of the default track's language plays as the default.
The candidates are the default track and every other track in its language that is neither a commentary, an audio description, a music-and-effects track nor a dependent track.
Each candidate ranks by what the rules above would deliver for it:

. a copy of a lossless track;
. a FLAC encode of a lossless track;
. a copy of a lossy track that keeps at least as many channels as every lossy encode among the candidates;
. a lossy encode;
. any other copy of a lossy track.

The best-ranked candidate plays as the default, and decides the session's audio codec.
Among candidates of equal rank, the default track comes first, then the candidate with more channels, then the first in stream order, so the order is total.
For example, with a flagged stereo AAC default, a TrueHD track that only an E-AC-3 5.1 encode reaches, and an AC-3 5.1 track, all copyable except the TrueHD track, the AC-3 copy plays as the default, then the TrueHD encode would, then the stereo copy.
One exception keeps Atmos objects: when the best candidate is a FLAC encode of a TrueHD track with Dolby Atmos, and the language holds an E-AC-3 track with Atmos (JOC) that can be copied, that E-AC-3 track plays as the default and is copied.
The copy keeps the objects, which a FLAC encode of the TrueHD track loses.

[[decision-restart-tracks]]
The server has no TrueHD or DTS encoder, so in a session whose audio codec is TrueHD or DTS, only a track already in that codec that a capability entry accepts gets a rendition, as a copy.
Every other track cannot reach the session's codec.
The stream decision still lists such a track, and the client offers it beside the renditions.
Choosing it replaces the stream session inside the playback session at the viewer's position, and the chosen track plays as the default of the new stream session, without the ranking, and decides its audio codec.
In that stream session, a TrueHD or DTS track whose copy a capability entry accepts is listed for a replacement, which returns the viewer to the copy.
It also gets a rendition encoded to the session's codec when the server encodes that codec, and none when the session's codec is TrueHD or DTS too.
Such a replacement uses none of the format-attempt budget, as for an image subtitle (<<decision-image-subtitles>>).
Every switch between renditions still starts no job attempt.

A FLAC encode of a lossless default track keeps every channel of the track, up to eight, except where the layout rules below downmix.
A FLAC encode of a lossy default track is not lossless, but it adds no second lossy generation to the track.
FFmpeg decodes the channel mix of a TrueHD track with Dolby Atmos and of a DTS-HD MA track with DTS:X, and ignores their objects, so the encode loses the objects.
Only a player that decodes such a track itself can keep them.
A server encode cannot, because neither FLAC nor the server's other encodes carry objects.
The FLAC encode has no audio steps of its own, so under ADR 0050 it steps like a copied FLAC track, through the E-AC-3, AC-3 or AAC encode that the server would choose without it.

A FLAC or ALAC rendition above 48 kHz, copied or encoded, adds one audio step before ADR 0050's multichannel steps.
That step keeps the codec, the layout and the written depth, at 48 kHz, or at 44.1 kHz when the source's rate is a multiple of 44.1 kHz.
Like every step, it counts only when it is smaller than the session's audio, and under it every rendition above that rate is resampled to it.
So a maximum bitrate reaches a lossy step only when the 48 kHz or 44.1 kHz FLAC or ALAC step does not fit either.
That step resamples, so it is not lossless.
A client can also bound the rate in its FLAC or ALAC capability entry with ADR 0049's optional maximum sample rate (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/717[streamarr/streamarr-server#717]).

Each other track keeps its own copy-or-encode decision.
Another track is copied only when that track already has the session's audio codec and a capability entry accepts the track.
Otherwise that track is encoded to the session's audio codec, with no more channels than that codec's capability entry accepts.
So the worker can copy one track and encode another.

A FLAC capability entry can give a minimum block size.
Under such an entry, a FLAC track can be copied only when its STREAMINFO minimum block size, which link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032's probe version 7] reports, is at least that size.
An unknown block size fits no entry that gives one.
A FLAC track that fails this check is encoded to FLAC like any other track, and that encode keeps every sample, because its frames hold the sample rate ÷ 20 samples, at least 400 at 8 kHz.
The Apple client gives 192, and so does the web client in WebKit, whose media stack is Apple's CoreMedia.
A lower audio step under the viewer's maximum bitrate caps every track, as link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc[ADR 0050] decides.

Every audio encode names its output channel layout under the channel layout rule of https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/395[streamarr/streamarr-server#395].
link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc#decision-channel-layout[ADR 0050] lists the layouts that each encoder carries, and its downmix applies whenever the output lacks a channel of the track's layout.
FLAC writes `3.0`, `quad`, `5.0(side)`, `5.1(side)`, `6.1` and `7.1`; Opus writes `3.0`, `quad`, `5.0`, `5.1`, `6.1` and `7.1`; ALAC writes `3.0`, `4.0`, `5.0`, `5.1`, `6.1(back)` and `7.1(wide)`.
A side pair and a back pair count as one surround pair.
Every FLAC or ALAC encode, of a lossless or a lossy track, writes the smallest layout of its codec that holds every channel of the track:

* FLAC carries mono, stereo, `3.0`, `quad`, 5.0, 5.1, `6.1` and 7.1, so a `6.1(back)` track keeps every channel as FLAC's `6.1`.
FLAC pads `2.1` and `3.1` to `5.1(side)`; `3.0(back)`, `4.0`, `4.1` and `6.0` to `6.1`; and `7.0` to `7.1`, with silent channels.
`7.1(wide)` and `7.1(wide-side)` have no FLAC layout that holds them, because their extra pair sits between the front centre and the front left and right, so they take ADR 0050's downmix.
* ALAC carries mono, stereo, `3.0`, `4.0`, 5.0, 5.1, `6.1(back)` and `7.1(wide)`, so a `6.1` track keeps every channel as `6.1(back)`, and a `7.1(wide-side)` track as `7.1(wide)`.
ALAC pads `3.0(back)` to `4.0`; `quad` to 5.0; `2.1` and `3.1` to 5.1; and `4.1` and `6.0` to `6.1(back)`, with silent channels.
ALAC's `7.1(wide)` holds front-of-centre channels instead of a second surround pair, so ALAC does not carry 7.1, and `7.0` and 7.1 take ADR 0050's downmix to 5.0 and 5.1.

When that layout has more channels than the capability entry accepts, the encode follows ADR 0050's rules for a channel maximum.
An encode that only renames a surround pair maps the channels with FFmpeg's `channelmap` filter, and an encode that pads uses `pan` with a gain of 0 for each added channel.
A FLAC or ALAC encode of a lossless track keeps every sample when it keeps or pads the track's layout, and is not lossless after a downmix.
Every Opus encode of more than two channels uses `-mapping_family 1`.

An Opus encode uses constrained VBR (`-vbr constrained`) at 96 kb/s for mono, 160 kb/s for stereo, 448 kb/s for 5.1, 576 kb/s for 6.1 and 640 kb/s for 7.1.
A `3.0`, `quad` or 5.0 encode takes the 5.1 rate until a measurement gives it its own.
These rates are provisional until an ABX listening check against the master, through a receiver, confirms them before production.
An Opus encode counts its rate plus 2 %, because constrained VBR ran up to 1.5 % over it.
Opus has no audio steps of its own, so under a maximum bitrate the session steps through ADR 0050's E-AC-3, AC-3 or AAC steps, and only the steps smaller than the Opus rate count.

A FLAC or ALAC encode has no target bitrate.
Its output's PCM bitrate is sample rate × bit depth × channels.
ADR 0054's bitrate scan measures what a FLAC encode of each audio track of a file with a lossless track writes (link:0054-workers-measure-each-stream-bitrate-and-a-copy-advertises-its-measured-peak.adoc#lossless-audio-measurement[ADR 0054]).
With a measurement of the same output, a FLAC encode counts its measured average and peak.
Without one, and for every ALAC encode, such an encoded rendition's bitrate is 65 % of that PCM bitrate, and its cost adds its framing.
Its segments then hold the audio of their intervals, widened by one encoded frame, at the full PCM bitrate plus their framing, and its peak follows ADR 0054's rule for an encoded audio rendition.
So an unmeasured stereo FLAC encode at 16 bits counts about 1,000 kb/s in the cost and 1,536 kb/s in `BANDWIDTH`, and a 7.1 encode at 24 bits counts 5,990 and 9,216 kb/s.

An encoded variant (link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044]) and a copy without a measured peak advertise `BANDWIDTH` as 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`.
When any audio rendition of the session is a FLAC or ALAC encode, such a variant's `BANDWIDTH` is instead 1.2 × its `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH` without the audio, plus the largest peak among the audio renditions.
For that rule, a FLAC or ALAC encode's peak is its measured peak, or without a measurement its full PCM bitrate, widened by one frame, plus its framing.
A copied rendition's peak is its measured peak (ADR 0054) when the server holds one.
Every other rendition's peak is 1.2 × its counted bitrate.
The largest peak can belong to another rendition than the largest cost, and 1.2 × 65 % would cover only 78 % of the PCM bitrate.

FFmpeg's ALAC encoder, and its FLAC encoder outside an experimental 32-bit mode, write 16 or 24 bits.
link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032's probe version 7] reports each audio stream's sample format and bit depth.
A FLAC or ALAC encode of an integer track of up to 24 bits keeps every sample: a track of up to 16 bits writes 16 bits, and a track of 17 to 24 bits writes 24 bits, with zero low bits added.
Float or 32-bit PCM is cut to 24 bits and is not lossless: FFmpeg's FLAC encoder drops the low 8 bits, and float samples above full scale clip.
When the probe gives no depth for a lossless track, the written depth follows the source codec, as before: PCM whose codec name states 16 bits, such as `pcm_s16le`, writes 16 bits, and any other lossless track 24 bits.
Decoded lossy audio writes 16 bits with TPDF dither (`aresample=osf=s16:dither_method=triangular`, <<decision-sample-processing>>).
A downmix of a 24-bit lossless track writes 24 bits, and a downmix of a 16-bit lossless track writes 16 bits with the same dither.
Only an encode that writes every decoded sample unchanged is called lossless.
The PCM bitrate counts the bit depth that the encode writes.

The 65 % lies above every film soundtrack measured: FLAC of decoded AAC, AC-3 and lossless film audio averaged 26 to 53 % of the PCM bitrate, and ALAC wrote 2 to 5 % more than FLAC.
The PCM bitrate bounds every frame, because FLAC and ALAC store a frame that does not compress as plain samples.
A segment can hold one more frame than its interval: with FFmpeg's default FLAC frame of 4,608 samples, 96 ms at 48 kHz, a 3 s window of white-noise PCM held 2 % more than its PCM bitrate, and the 50 ms frames of <<decision-audio-grid>> hold less.
A cap below it would understate some segments: the largest 3 s window of a decoded film track reached 85 % of the PCM bitrate, and of decoded white noise 94 %.
Evidence: <<evidence-lossless-size>>.

DTS and TrueHD follow the same declared-codec rule as every other codec.
A DTS or TrueHD track is copied only when the client declares that codec in fMP4, and is otherwise encoded.
FFmpeg's MP4 muxer writes TrueHD only under `-strict experimental`, so a job that copies a TrueHD track passes `-strict experimental`, and every other job keeps `-strict unofficial`.
A TrueHD copy writes an `mlpa` sample entry with a `dmlp` box, and its `CODECS` value is `mlpa`.
No tested browser or Apple player decoded DTS or TrueHD from fMP4, in 0 of 36 plays across six players.
AVFoundation reads a TrueHD copy's sample entry but has no TrueHD decoder, so a client that plays through AVPlayer never declares TrueHD.
An Apple client that decodes TrueHD itself can declare it.
Media3, the Android media library, can play DTS on the SHIELD TV Pro by passthrough to an HDMI receiver, or with Media3's FFmpeg decoder module.
A reading of Media3 1.11.1's source found both Media3 paths, and no measurement covers them.
For TrueHD, Media3 1.11.1 reads the `mlpa` sample entry, but maps no `mlpa` codec string and regroups TrueHD for passthrough only in its MP4 and Matroska extractors, not in the fragmented MP4 extractor that its HLS player uses.
The Android client declares DTS or TrueHD only after a check on the SHIELD TV Pro through an HDMI receiver (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-android/issues/1[streamarr/streamarr-android#1]).

*Why:* In the audio measurements, hls.js 1.7.0 in Chromium, Chrome and Firefox played audio renditions of AAC, Opus and FLAC, and the page switched between those renditions.
After a switch that changed the codec, the audio played 90 to 140 ms late until the next seek.
Switches between AAC renditions stayed in sync.
With one audio codec per stream session, no switch changes the codec, so no client gets that offset.
When a variant's `CODECS` attribute listed `ec-3` or `ac-3`, hls.js in Chromium, Chrome and Firefox refused the whole stream, even with an AAC default rendition.
So one rendition that a player cannot decode fails the whole stream, and a copy needs the client's declaration.
Stereo Opus played in all six tested players, but 5.1 Opus failed in every Apple player.
So a safe Opus copy depends on the channel count as well as the codec.
Before the FLAC, Opus and ALAC encodes, a copied default track in one of those codecs left every other track needing a codec that the server did not encode.
FFmpeg 8.1.3 in the pinned worker image lists its native `flac` and `alac` encoders and the `libopus` encoder as ready for use.
So the server can keep a lossless or passthrough default track as a copy and still encode the other tracks to its codec.
The same FFmpeg marks its `dca` (DTS) and `truehd` encoders as experimental, so the server encodes neither codec.
Under `-strict experimental`, the same FFmpeg copied FATE's 7.1 TrueHD Atmos and 5.1 TrueHD samples into fragmented MP4 with every packet byte-identical, its major-sync flags kept, and the same decoded samples.
Seeked copies wrote the same `dmlp` box, because FFmpeg's copy starts at a major sync, so a TrueHD copy keeps one init across job attempts.
FFmpeg's experimental flag here guards the muxing, which the readback shows to be sound, not an encoder.
Encoding a TrueHD default track because a commentary track needs its codec would lose the Atmos objects that a client decoding TrueHD keeps, so the server copies the default track and offers the commentary by a replacement.
An encode of the default track keeps every track in one codec, so a switch stays in sync.
ADR 0050's encode takes E-AC-3 before AC-3 and AAC, so the encoded default track keeps surround wherever the client declares E-AC-3 or AC-3 with more than two channels.
A FLAC encode of a lossless track keeps every sample of up to eight channels, where the lossy E-AC-3 and AC-3 encodes stop at 5.1.
A FLAC encode of a lossy track keeps the decoded audio, where an E-AC-3, AC-3 or AAC encode adds a second lossy generation.
A disc's own lossy track was encoded once from the master, so its copy beats the server's lossy encode of the lossless track.
On macOS 26.6.2, AVPlayer played a stream whose audio rendition was 7.1 FLAC at 24 bits in fMP4, and reported eight channels in WAVE 7.1 order.
In the same FFmpeg, an Opus encode refused 2.1, 3.0(back), 4.0 and quad(side).
An ALAC encode wrote 2.1 as stereo, and quad, quad(side) and 3.0(back) as five channels.
A FLAC encode wrote 2.1, 3.0(back) and 4.0 under another layout, with only a warning.
Such an encode moves channels: a `4.0` track's centre played from the back left, a `2.1` track's low-frequency channel from the centre, and a `6.0` or `7.0` track's back channel from the low-frequency channel.
Padded with silent channels to the smallest FLAC layout that holds them, `2.1`, `3.1`, `3.0(back)`, `4.0`, `4.1`, `6.0` and `7.0` tracks kept every sample in place, for at most 0.06 % more bytes.
Padded to the smallest ALAC layout, `2.1`, `3.1`, `quad`, `3.0(back)`, `4.1` and `6.0` tracks did too.
A player then shows the padded layout, such as 6.1 for a `4.0` track.
FFmpeg's `pan` filter copies channels by position when every gain is 1, so a rename with it put a `6.1` track's back centre in the back left, while `channelmap` kept every channel in place.
Each multichannel Opus rate is the lowest tested rate at which every real channel of Tears of Steel and Sintel scored within about 1.5 points of disturbed frames and 1 dB of stereo at 160 kb/s, on a per-channel noise-to-mask measure.
Xiph's guidance gives 96 to 128 kb/s for stereo music, 128 to 256 kb/s for 5.1 and 256 to 450 kb/s for 7.1 as starting points to adjust by listening, and 5.1 at 384 kb/s already left 11.5 to 21.4 % of the worst channel's frames disturbed.
Unconstrained VBR at 448 kb/s spent 406 kb/s on Tears of Steel and left 26.5 % of the centre channel's frames disturbed, against 1.4 % with constrained VBR.
Without `-mapping_family 1`, FFmpeg's `libopus` encoder played a 5.0 track's centre from the rear right and a 6.1 track's side left from the centre.
With it, every channel of 5.0, 5.1, 6.1 and 7.1 landed in its RFC 7845 position.
Evidence: <<evidence-audio-cases>>, <<evidence-encoders>>, <<evidence-lossless-surround>>.

[[decision-sample-processing]]
=== The worker decodes AC-3 and E-AC-3 without compression, dithers every conversion to 16 bits, and resamples with tuned settings

The worker decodes every AC-3 and E-AC-3 track that it encodes with `-drc_scale 0 -target_level -31`.
The decode applies none of the track's dynamic range compression, and applies the track's dialogue normalization as a hardware decoder does for the copy, so an encode plays at the copy's level through an AV receiver and a step from a copy to an encode keeps the level.
ADR 0050's downmix mixes these decoded samples.

Every conversion from floating point to 16 bits applies TPDF dither (`aresample=osf=s16:dither_method=triangular`).
That covers every 16-bit FLAC or ALAC encode of decoded lossy audio and every downmix that writes 16 bits.

A capability entry's maximum sample rate (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc[ADR 0049]) bounds every encode, lossy or lossless, as ADR 0049 decides.
Within that bound, a lossy encode keeps a source rate of 44.1 or 48 kHz and writes 48 kHz from any other rate, and an Opus encode writes 48 kHz.
A FLAC or ALAC encode keeps the source's rate, except under that bound and in its 48 kHz step (<<decision-audio-codec>>).
Every rate change uses `aresample=filter_size=128:phase_shift=14:cutoff=0.95:kaiser_beta=14`.

*Why:* FFmpeg's AC-3 and E-AC-3 decoders apply a track's dynamic range compression unless `drc_scale` is 0.
On FATE's Miller's Crossing AC-3 sample, the compression lowered the peak from −6.41 to −14.03 dBFS, and on The Great Wall's E-AC-3 sample it changed the gain by −1.6 to +5.9 dB from one 10 ms window to the next.
A FLAC encode that carries that compression is not the track's audio.
A Dolby decoder applies the track's dialogue normalization to a copy, so the copy's dialogue plays at −31 dBFS.
With `-drc_scale 0`, `-target_level -31` lowered Miller's Crossing by 4.0 dB and CSI Miami's E-AC-3 by 5.0 dB, and left The Great Wall unchanged, so an encode matches the copy through a receiver.
Without dither, FFmpeg's conversion of a −90 dBFS 1 kHz float tone to 16 bits put harmonics at −112 and −106 dBFS and read the tone 0.85 dB too loud.
With TPDF dither, every harmonic fell to −136 dBFS or below, under the dither's own noise.
On decoded AAC, the conversion also floored instead of rounding, which left a −0.5 LSB offset.
The pinned image has no soxr resampler.
With FFmpeg's default resampler settings, 44.1 to 48 kHz lowered a 21 kHz tone by 2.09 dB, and 96 to 48 kHz folded a 24.5 kHz tone back to 23.5 kHz at −14.4 dB.
With the tuned settings, the 21 kHz tone lost 0.03 dB and the folded tone fell to −138.3 dB, and a 7.1 resample from 96 to 48 kHz ran at about 45 times real time.
No HLS player was tested with AAC above 48 kHz, so a lossy encode writes at most 48 kHz.
Evidence: <<evidence-sample-processing>>.

[[decision-video-job]]
=== Every running variant's job attempt produces the audio renditions

Each job attempt runs one FFmpeg process that writes its output.
On the path of link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013] that moves a profile 5 stream's RPUs, a second FFmpeg process feeds that process a fragmented MP4 copy of the source.
The copy already holds the attempt's audio, copied or encoded from the source, and the process that writes the output copies that audio unchanged.
The FFmpeg process that writes the output writes the video and every selected audio track to the standard output that link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037]'s producer reads.
The video and each audio track stay separate tracks in that output.
The worker splits that output into the variant's video segments and each audio rendition's segments.
The worker runs no job attempt that carries audio alone.
Every audio rendition follows the stream session's segment list from link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
So an audio rendition's segment `N` covers the same media time as segment `N` of every variant.
Where the segment list merges several 3 s slots of the grid into one media segment, the audio rendition's segment spans the same slots.
Each audio sample joins the segment whose interval holds the sample's start time.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] places audio the same way.
Media time zero stays the source container's start time, whatever tracks a job attempt maps.
To keep that origin, every FFmpeg that reads the source cancels FFmpeg's start time correction, as link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-media-time-zero[ADR 0037] decides.
That FFmpeg passes `-discard none` and `-start_at_zero`, and `-itsoffset` with the start time correction that the probe reports.
The start time correction does not depend on which tracks a job attempt maps, so every job attempt and every rendition share one origin.
On the path that moves RPUs, the FFmpeg that reads the source passes those flags, and the FFmpeg that reads its copy keeps the copy's timestamps without `-start_at_zero`, as link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] states.
Every audio encode joins its job attempts on its audio frame grid, and the producer never moves a copied track (<<decision-audio-grid>>).

Every running job attempt of every variant writes every selected audio track.
The server keeps the first delivery of each audio segment that it receives.
That first delivery can come from any running variant.
A later delivery of the same audio segment replaces nothing.
The server designates no variant for audio.
Under link:0033-transcode-worker-is-a-separate-service.adoc[ADR 0033], the server dispatches a job with audio renditions only to a worker that advertises support for audio renditions.
A worker without that support would ignore the job's new fields and write multiplexed output.

*Why:* In all five players, audio that the variant's job attempt carried played exactly like audio from one separate job per language.
Carrying the audio in the variant's job attempt read only 0 to 0.5 % more source bytes.
Three separate audio jobs on a Matroska or MPEG-TS source would read 3.0 times the video attempt's bytes.
Every language exists from the start of the stream session, so no first switch waits for a job to start.
Variants whose job attempts started at the same segment wrote byte-identical audio in 2,208 of 2,208 segment pairs.
So the first delivery of an audio segment matches a later delivery from a variant that started at the same segment.
When the viewer's variant changes, the newly requested variant's job attempt writes every audio track from its first segment.
So the audio renditions never wait for the server to hand audio production from one variant to another.
On an MPEG-TS source, FFmpeg's `-start_at_zero` option measured media time zero from the earliest mapped stream.
Mapping a different set of languages moved media time by 170.7 ms.
With `-discard none` on the input, media time zero stayed at the container's earliest stream.
A subtitle or data stream that starts 1 s or more before the other streams still moves media time zero under `-discard none`, so ADR 0037 also cancels the start time correction.
Evidence: <<evidence-video-job>>, <<evidence-audio-cases>>.

[[decision-audio-grid]]
=== Every audio encode joins its job attempts on its audio frame grid, and the producer never moves a copied track

Each encoded audio rendition has its own audio frame grid: the start times of its encoder's frames, at the encode's frame length and sample rate, counted from the default track's first sample.
When the session copies the default track, that first sample is the copy's own.
When the session encodes the default track, it is the track's probed start.
The frame length is the encoder's: 1,024 samples for AAC, 1,536 for AC-3 and E-AC-3, 960 for Opus, the sample rate ÷ 20 rounded down to a whole sample for FLAC, such as 2,400 at 48 kHz and 1,102 at 22.05 kHz, set with `-frame_size`, and 4,096 for ALAC, whose FFmpeg encoder writes no other length.
A copied track keeps its own frames, whatever their length.

Every audio encode puts its output frames on its grid.
FFmpeg's encoders start their first frame a fixed delay before their input: 256 samples for AC-3 and E-AC-3, 312 for Opus, one whole frame for AAC, and none for FLAC and ALAC.
Each job attempt decodes each encoded track from one segment period before the start of its first segment.
It trims the decoded audio to the first point at or after that start that lies the encoder's delay after a grid point, so the encoder's first frame starts on that grid point.
An attempt that starts at the track's first sample instead adds silence before that sample, so that its first frame starts on the last grid point that lies at least the encoder's delay before that sample.
The silence is the distance from that grid point to the first sample, less the encoder's delay, so it also absorbs a track whose first sample lies off the default track's grid.
For a track that starts on a grid point, it is 1,280 samples for AC-3 and E-AC-3 and 648 for Opus.
So consecutive attempts meet without a gap or an overlap, and a FLAC or ALAC encode of a lossless track decodes to the same samples on both sides of a junction.
When the source container rounds timestamps, as Matroska rounds them to 1 ms, a seeked attempt takes its decoded audio's start from the source track's own frame grid, counted from the track's probed start, and not from the rounded packet time.

The producer never moves a copied track.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-audio[ADR 0037] rebuilds each copied sample's duration from its sample count, and its decode time from the running sum.
A session with two copied tracks whose frames start at different times keeps both, and its encodes count their grids from the default copy's first sample.
A switch between those two copies accepts a gap or an overlap of up to half a frame, the frame's samples ÷ (2 × the sample rate): 10.7 ms for AAC and 16 ms for AC-3 at 48 kHz.
A viewer's audio delay moves every audio rendition by the same amount (link:0055-a-viewers-audio-delay-is-a-playback-session-parameter-and-the-server-shifts-the-audio-renditions.adoc[ADR 0055], proposed in https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-adr/pull/87[streamarr/streamarr-adr#87]).

The merge gate of https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/503[streamarr/streamarr-server#503] decodes across a junction for every pair of source codec and encoder, TrueHD, DTS-HD MA and AC-3 included, at 44.1, 48 and 96 kHz.
It requires 0 samples of gap or overlap, and identical decoded samples for a FLAC or ALAC encode of a lossless track.

*Why:* An encoder's frames start a fixed delay before its input.
After a plain seek to a segment boundary, consecutive attempts met with gaps or overlaps of −59.3 to +26.2 ms, depending on the encoder's frame length and the boundary.
DTS-HD MA decoded from a plain seek differed from a continuous decode in 17,801 samples after the junction, so even its FLAC encode was not lossless there.
Decoding from 3 s early and trimming to the grid gave 0 samples of gap or overlap at all 60 measured junctions, and the FLAC and ALAC encodes of FLAC, TrueHD and DTS-HD MA tracks decoded to the source's samples on both sides.
FLAC's default frame of 4,608 samples does not divide most boundaries, and the sample rate ÷ 20 gives 50 ms frames for 0.4 to 0.8 % more bytes.
FFmpeg's ALAC encoder ignored `-frame_size 2400` and wrote 4,096-sample frames.
Copied AAC from an FFmpeg-made Matroska file sat 16 samples off the AAC encoder's frame grid.
Without alignment, switches between AAC renditions moved the audio's offset to the picture by −46 to +60 ms, and with the copy and the encodes on one grid they stayed within −12 to +9 ms, about the measurement's own ±10 ms jitter.
Counting the encodes' grids from the copy's first sample aligns the renditions without moving the copy, which keeps the copy's authored sync.
An encode of AAC copied in Matroska started 8 samples early after a seek, because Matroska had rounded the seek packet's timestamp.
Evidence: <<evidence-audio-junctions>>, <<evidence-audio-cases>>.

[[decision-init]]
=== Each audio rendition has one initialization segment

Each audio rendition has its own init, which describes the rendition's one track.
That init equals the init that a single-track FFmpeg job writes for the same track.
The init depends on neither the job attempt, nor the variant, nor the other tracks that the job maps, nor the job's video codec.
Each audio rendition's init gives the track's language as `und`, the code for an undetermined language.
The rendition's `LANGUAGE` attribute in the multivariant playlist carries the track's language as a BCP 47 tag.
So the playlist alone names the track's language.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] rejects a replacement attempt whose init differs from the stored init.
That rule applies to each audio rendition's init as it applies to each variant's init.
The init of copied audio declares the audio's bitrate unknown.
The worker already declares that bitrate unknown for the audio it copies today.
FFmpeg writes one combined `moov` box that describes every track of the job.
The worker's implementation and tests define how the worker derives each init from that box.

*Why:* FFmpeg's combined `moov` box set each audio track's track number, enabled flag and alternate group from the set of tracks in the job.
For copied AAC from an MPEG-TS source, the combined `moov` box gave 5 different inits in 12 attempts.
The inits differed because the demuxer's bitrate estimate depends on where the demuxer starts.
The derived inits gave one init per rendition on Matroska, MP4 and MPEG-TS sources.
That result held across every attempt, every variant and both tested ways of seeking a replacement attempt.
In 4 of 4 renditions from H.264 jobs, the derived init was byte-identical to a single-track FFmpeg job's init.
In SVT-AV1 jobs, FFmpeg wrote the video's brand `av01` into the `ftyp` box of the combined output.
H.264 jobs carried no video brand in that box.
So an init cut from the combined output differs between an AV1 job attempt and an H.264 job attempt.
A stream session can run an AV1 variant beside an H.264 variant, so ADR 0037's identity check would refuse one of the two inits.
A single-track audio job writes the same `ftyp` box whatever the video codec.
An init that declares copied AAC's bitrate unknown played in all five players exactly like an init that declares the bitrate.
Evidence: <<evidence-init>>, <<evidence-audio-cases>>.

[[decision-priming]]
=== Audio renditions start without audio before media time zero

The AAC priming frame is a frame of encoder delay that an AAC track can carry before media time zero.
When FFmpeg encodes AAC, the priming frame's decode time is −1024 samples, about 21 ms at 48 kHz.
Copied AAC can carry a priming frame too.
Other codecs can also carry audio that starts before media time zero.
Two rules cover that audio, one for each kind of media segment that carries audio.
Audio before media time zero is dropped in audio renditions (for AAC, the priming frame).
Multiplexed variant segments keep that audio.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] states both rules in the same words.

An audio rendition's media segment carries one audio track alone.
Without that audio, no audio rendition's segment starts at a negative decode time.
For AC-3 and E-AC-3, the drop removes about 27 ms of real audio at the start of the stream.
For Opus, the drop removes about 14 ms of real audio at the start of the stream.
A multiplexed variant's media segment carries video and audio together under link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
Multiplexed variants remain in use until the server delivers audio renditions.
A video-only variant's media segment carries no audio, so neither rule applies to that segment.
Before the renditions ticket (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/503[streamarr/streamarr-server#503]) merges, the ticket's player check shows again that every tested player plays audio renditions without the priming frame (<<decision-device-checks>>).
The drop stays until the Apple TV check of the end of the epic.
If AVPlayer trims a kept priming frame at the start, by an edit list whose media time is 1,024 samples, every client gets the kept frame with that edit list instead.
If AVPlayer plays the kept frame late, as Firefox does, the drop stays.

*Why:* When segment 0 kept the priming frame at a negative decode time, hls.js playback stopped for good, at first after a language switch in the first 6 s.
The stall happened in 10 of 10 runs in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit.
The stall also happened in 3 of 3 runs of an independent re-check.
The frame's decode time is the 64-bit two's complement of −1024.
hls.js 1.7.0 reads that value as an invalid signed value.
hls.js keeps only the upper 32 bits as the segment's start.
At 48 kHz, that start is 89,478 s.
When the worker dropped the frame, every run played.
A later re-run with other fixtures kept the priming frame, switched at 2 s, and played to the end in 15 of 15 hls.js runs.
So the stall depends on a condition that the later re-run lacked.
A third run stalled with no switch at all: WebKit with hls.js stopped for good at 6.02 s in 2 of 55 plays of a negative decode time, so the negative decode time alone can cause it.
The drop costs the first frame's quality: the first 21.3 ms of an AAC tone decoded at 2.8 dB signal-to-noise ratio, against 37.2 dB with the frame kept.
A kept frame with an edit list, or with a composition offset of −1,024 samples, stalled in none of 45 plays per player, and Chromium played it in sync.
Firefox played it one AAC frame, 21.3 ms, late from a start at 0 until the first seek.
hls.js places a multiplexed fragment by the fragment's video track.
So the priming frame's decode time does not decide where hls.js places a multiplexed segment.
An audio rendition's fragment has no video track, so hls.js places that fragment by its audio decode time.
With the drop, AC-3, E-AC-3 and Opus renditions played in every player that plays the codec, and no timestamp moved.
Evidence: <<evidence-priming>>, <<evidence-audio-cases>>.

[[decision-audio-end]]
=== An audio track that ends early is padded with silence

An audio track that ends at least one media segment before the video is encoded and padded with silence.
A job attempt that starts after the track's end encodes silence for that track from a generated source.
So every job attempt carries the same tracks, and no playlist uses `EXT-X-GAP`.
`EXT-X-GAP` is the HLS tag that marks a media segment as missing.
Audio in a multiplexed variant follows the same rule, even when the variant is a stream copy.
A stream copy passes the source video through unchanged.
The probe gives each track's end time where the container header states that end time.
Otherwise the server learns the end time from the first job attempt that runs past the end.

FFmpeg's interleave limit is how far FFmpeg lets one stream run ahead while FFmpeg waits for packets of another stream.
The worker sets that interleave limit explicitly to 10 s.
Once an audio track stops arriving, FFmpeg holds back the video for up to the interleave limit.
After FFmpeg writes that video, the worker closes a media segment when the segment's video has run 1 s past the segment's end.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] owns this closing rule.
In this ADR, the closing rule is only a safety net behind the padding rule.
The safety net covers a track whose end time is wrong or missing, and audio that stalls past the interleave limit.

*Why:* FFmpeg holds its first fragment until every mapped track has a sample.
In a test, a 72 s file's audio track ended at 40 s, and the input ran at twice real time.
A job attempt that started at 48 s and mapped the ended track wrote its first byte after 12.0 s, when the input ended.
The same job attempt without the ended track wrote its first byte after 0.8 s.
For a film, that wait lasts the rest of the transcode.
On a Mac, AVPlayer stopped the whole stream where a rendition's playlist ended early.
On an iPhone, hls.js stopped for good inside the `EXT-X-GAP` segments when the viewer switched to the ended track.
Silence padding played to the end on the iPhone and in every headless engine.
AVPlayer stopped at the end of multiplexed audio in 12 of 12 runs, so multiplexed audio needs the padding too.
A non-fragmented MP4 file states each track's duration in its track header (ISO/IEC 14496-12), and mkvmerge writes a `DURATION` statistics tag for each Matroska track.
A Matroska file without statistics tags and an MPEG-TS file state no track duration, so the server also learns the end time from a job attempt.
In normal output, audio trails video by at most 0.16 s, so the 1 s margin never closes a segment while the segment's audio is still on the way.
Evidence: <<evidence-audio-cases>>.

[[decision-languages]]
=== The Profile's language preferences choose the default audio language and the subtitles that start on

Each Profile has a preferred audio language and a preferred subtitle language.
link:0024-identity-authority-by-relationship.adoc[ADR 0024] keeps a Profile's preferences with the Profile.
A Profile can also override either language for one episode or movie, for one season or for one series.
Each override belongs to one Profile.
An override names a language, never one track of a file.
An override is user-authored data, so it attaches to the title's catalog entry (link:0039-catalog-entries-hold-metadata-edits-and-user-data.adoc[ADR 0039]).

When a stream session starts, the stream decision takes the audio language from the first of these that applies:

. the Profile's override for the episode or movie;
. the Profile's override for the season;
. the Profile's override for the series;
. the Profile's preferred audio language;
. the language of the default track that <<decision-audio-renditions>> chooses from the file's own flags.

A language applies only when the file holds a track in that language that can be the default.
The ranking of <<decision-sibling-ranking>> then chooses the default track among the tracks of that language.
The subtitle language follows the same order, with the Profile's subtitle overrides and preferred subtitle language.
The last step for subtitles is the subtitle track that the file marks as default, or no subtitle when the file marks none.
The client turns on the subtitle track that the stream decision names.
The rules of <<decision-subtitle-selection>> for forced tracks still apply while subtitles are off.

When the viewer switches the audio language or the subtitle language during playback, the server stores the new language as the Profile's override for the series.
For a movie, the switch becomes the Profile's override for that movie.

*Why:* A viewer who prefers one language wants it in every file without choosing it each time.
A viewer can watch one series in its original language and another series dubbed, so overrides exist at each level.
The most specific override describes the viewer's narrowest choice, so it comes first.
Viewers in one Household can prefer different languages, so each override belongs to one Profile.
Each episode's file can list its tracks in a different order, so an override names a language rather than a track.
A viewer who switches language in one episode usually wants that language in the next episode, so the switch applies to the series.

[[decision-subtitle-layer]]
=== Clients draw subtitles in a layer above the platform's player, and burn-in is the fallback

A client draws subtitles in its own layer above the platform's player.
The layer draws text subtitles, ASS and SSA subtitles with their styling and positions through libass, and PGS image subtitles.
The server sends the layer each selected subtitle track's original data, without converting it.
A client declares that it has the layer and the subtitle formats that the layer draws.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/506[streamarr/streamarr-server#506] decides how the declaration spells them.

The layer counts only while the client plays on its own screen.
When the client hands playback to another device, such as an AirPlay receiver, that device gets the rules for a client without the layer.
When those rules need burn-in, the client replaces the stream session inside the playback session at the viewer's position.

For a client with the layer, the server never burns in a subtitle that the layer draws.
Such a subtitle never makes the stream session encode its video.
While subtitles are off, a forced track whose language matches the selected audio still shows, whatever its format.
A client with the layer selects that forced track itself, draws it in the layer when the layer draws its format, and selects again when the audio language changes.
link:0045-clients-direct-play-covered-files-and-use-platform-players.adoc[ADR 0045] counts a subtitle that the layer draws as covered for direct play.
For a client without the layer, or for a format that the client's layer does not draw, the earlier rules stay.
Text subtitle tracks become WebVTT renditions (<<decision-subtitles>>), and a selected image subtitle is burned into the video (<<decision-image-subtitles>>).

A subtitle file that sits beside the source file, such as a `.srt` file with the same base name, is a subtitle track of that media file.
The server treats such a track like a text subtitle track inside the file.
Streamarr does not download subtitles from an online subtitle database.

*Why:* WebVTT drops ASS styling and positions, which signs and typesetting need.
Burning in a subtitle forces an encode of the video, costs worker capacity, and replaces the stream session on every subtitle change.
A layer above the platform's player keeps that player for video and audio (ADR 0045) and draws each format the same way over HLS and direct play.
Subtitle files beside the source are a common way for a library to carry subtitles.
An online subtitle database needs an account with an outside service and learns which files the library holds.

[[decision-subtitles]]
=== Text subtitles are WebVTT renditions extracted once per media file

The server delivers each text subtitle track that the stream decision selects.
Each selected text subtitle track becomes a WebVTT subtitle rendition.
Text subtitle tracks are SubRip, ASS and SSA, mov_text and WebVTT.
A durable job extracts every text track of a media file to WebVTT in one pass.
The job runs once per media file, never once per stream session.
The job runs after the probe of the media file (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]).
The job survives a server restart, like the probe of link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032].
A worker extracts the tracks, because the server runs no FFmpeg (link:0033-transcode-worker-is-a-separate-service.adoc[ADR 0033]).
Under link:0033-transcode-worker-is-a-separate-service.adoc[ADR 0033], the server dispatches the extraction job only to a worker that advertises support for subtitle extraction.
Extracted cue times are in media time, the timeline that every job attempt shares.
A source container can start far from zero, so the extraction job shifts each cue from the container's timeline onto media time.
When the extraction job reads the source with `-start_at_zero`, it passes the same flags and the same start time correction as a job attempt (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-media-time-zero[ADR 0037]).
Otherwise it subtracts the container start time that the probe reports from each cue.

A subtitle rendition's media playlist lists one WebVTT segment per media segment of the stream session's segment list.
The segment list is the one from link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] that every variant of the stream session shares.
Where the segment list merges several 3 s slots of the grid into one media segment, the WebVTT segment spans the same slots.
Each WebVTT segment holds every cue that overlaps the segment's interval.
Each cue keeps its own media time.
A cue that spans a segment boundary appears in both segments, as RFC 8216 requires.
Every WebVTT segment carries the header `X-TIMESTAMP-MAP=MPEGTS:0,LOCAL:00:00:00.000`.
That header maps media time zero to cue time zero, so this ADR calls it the identity map.
Turning subtitles on or off starts no job attempt and never restarts the video.
A switch of subtitle language also starts no job attempt and never restarts the video.

*Why:* With the identity map, every player showed each cue at the same time.
Each player placed the cue on the timeline that the player uses for the picture.
An `X-TIMESTAMP-MAP` header with a constant `MPEGTS` offset of 900000 ticks put every cue 10 s late in every player.
When a viewer switched subtitle language while a line was due, every player except AVPlayer showed the new line within 43 ms.
AVPlayer's worst case was 131 ms.
Extracting subtitles reads a whole Matroska file, whether the job extracts one track or all of them.
On a test source whose container started at 601.4 s, an extraction that kept the container's timestamps put every cue 601.4 s late.
Evidence: <<evidence-subtitles>>, <<evidence-extraction>>, <<evidence-subtitle-cases>>.

[[decision-subtitle-selection]]
=== Players select only forced subtitle tracks by themselves

A forced track holds only the lines that every viewer needs, such as dialogue in a foreign language.
An SDH track, or subtitles for the deaf and hard of hearing, also describes sounds and names the speakers.
Every subtitle rendition carries `AUTOSELECT=NO`, except a forced track.
A forced track carries `FORCED=YES` and `AUTOSELECT=YES`.
An SDH track also carries `AUTOSELECT=NO`, although Apple's authoring guide asks for `AUTOSELECT=YES` on SDH tracks.
The Apple and Android clients read the system captions setting and select an SDH track themselves.
hls.js ignores `FORCED`.
So the web client selects a forced track itself when subtitles are off and the forced track's language matches the audio language.

*Why:* In one test, every subtitle rendition carried `AUTOSELECT=YES`.
The page turned subtitles off, and then the audio changed language.
WebKit's native HLS then turned the subtitles back on, in 3 of 3 runs.
In a test that followed Apple's authoring guide, with `AUTOSELECT=YES` on the regular and SDH tracks, WebKit's native HLS also turned on subtitles by itself, in 3 of 3 runs.
AVPlayer with automatic media selection showed a forced track's lines by itself.
No browser engine showed a forced track by itself.
WebKit's native HLS listed the forced track and never showed the forced track's lines.
Evidence: <<evidence-subtitles>>, <<evidence-subtitle-cases>>.

[[decision-names]]
=== Each rendition's name is unique within its group and includes the language

A subtitle rendition's `NAME` is the stream's title when that title is unique among the file's subtitle tracks.
Otherwise the server builds the name from the language and the flags, for example "English Forced".
Every name includes the language.
A track is SDH when the track's hearing-impaired flag is set, or when the track's title or handler name marks the track as SDH.
The SDH characteristics are the HLS `CHARACTERISTICS` values that mark a track as transcribing dialogue and describing music and sound.
When an SDH rendition carries the SDH characteristics, the rendition's name leaves out the SDH token.
The name keeps the SDH token only when leaving the token out would duplicate another name.
A stream's title is Matroska's `title` tag or MP4's `name` tag.
When a stream has no title, the server uses the stream's handler name, unless that handler name is generic.
Probe version 4 of link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032] reports each audio and subtitle stream's title, its raw handler name and its hearing-impaired flag.
The server, not the worker, decides which handler names are generic.
The server treats a handler name as generic when the name contains "Handler" or " handler", starts with "Gestionnaire ", "Module ", "Gestor ", "Procedura ", "Core Media " or "ISO Media file produced by", or equals the track's four-character handler type, such as `soun`.
The first three checks come from MediaInfo's rule for MP4 handler names.
The rule's test set comes from public sources (<<evidence-handler-names>>).
The rule leaves out every generic name in that set and keeps every track name written for the test, such as "English", "Commentary" and "Descriptive audio".
Names that still collide get a trailing number in file order, such as "English" and "English 2".
RFC 8216 section 4.3.4.1.1 says "All EXT-X-MEDIA tags in the same Group MUST have different NAME attributes."
An audio rendition's `NAME` follows the same rules, applied among the file's audio tracks, with these additions:

* The flags of an audio name are "Commentary" when ffprobe's `comment` disposition is set, "Audio description" when its `visual_impaired` or `descriptions` disposition is set, and "Music and effects" when its `clean_effects` disposition is set, for example "English Commentary".
link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032's probe version 7] reports these dispositions.
* When none of those dispositions is set, the track's title, or its handler name when that name is not generic, marks it.
A commentary word marks a commentary: "commentary", "commentaire", "kommentar", "comentario" or "commento".
An audio description word marks an audio description: "audio description", "descriptive", "described", "DVS" or "visually impaired".
The match ignores case and takes whole words only.
A track that a word marks counts as flagged for every rule of this ADR, the default track and the describes-video characteristic included.
The server owns both word lists, as it owns the generic handler names, so a fix needs no new probe.
* A music-and-effects rendition carries `AUTOSELECT=NO`, because it has no dialogue.
* When names built from the language and the flags still collide, each colliding name adds the source track's format and channel layout after a middle dot, such as "English · TrueHD 7.1" and "English · AC-3 5.1".
The format is the codec's common name: AAC, AC-3, E-AC-3, TrueHD, DTS, FLAC, ALAC, Opus, PCM or MP3.
A DTS track whose profile name, which probe version 5 reports, starts with "DTS-HD MA" shows as "DTS-HD MA".
A copied track whose profile name contains "Dolby Atmos" or "DTS:X" adds "Atmos" or "DTS:X" to its format, such as "English · E-AC-3 Atmos 7.1", because a copy keeps the objects.
An encoded track leaves them out, because an encode loses the objects, an E-AC-3 track with Atmos (JOC) included.
The layout is probe version 7's layout name without its parenthesized part, such as "5.1" for `5.1(side)`, with "Mono" for `mono` and "Stereo" for `stereo`.
A track without a known layout shows its channel count instead, such as "6 channels".
* A trailing number goes only on names that still collide, such as "English · AC-3 5.1" and "English · AC-3 5.1 2".
* An audio description rendition carries `CHARACTERISTICS="public.accessibility.describes-video"` and `AUTOSELECT=YES`, and its name keeps "Audio description".

A subtitle name also takes the "Commentary" flag from the same disposition.
The format and the layout apply to audio names only, because every text subtitle becomes WebVTT, so a subtitle's source format tells the viewer nothing.
The generic handler-name rule, the replacements below and the trailing number stay shared, so https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/60[streamarr/streamarr-server#60] and https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/503[streamarr/streamarr-server#503] use one name rule.
Before the server writes a title or handler name into `NAME`, it replaces each carriage return or line feed with a space, each double quote with an apostrophe, and each backslash with a slash.
RFC 8216 section 4.2 forbids a carriage return, a line feed and a double quote in a quoted string.

*Why:* AVPlayer appends " SDH" to the name of a rendition that carries the SDH characteristics.
So a rendition named "English SDH" showed as "English SDH SDH".
In a test where two renditions were both named "English", AVPlayer and WebKit's native HLS exposed only one of the two, in 3 of 3 plays.
A title such as "Commentary" tells apart two tracks in one language, where the language and the flags cannot.
Many muxers write a fixed name for the track's type, such as `SoundHandler`, into every MP4 track.
The list of generic handler names is open-ended.
A rule in the worker would need a new probe version for each fix, and every file would then need a new probe.
For example, a rule that treats a name as generic when the name ends with "Handler" would keep "ISO Media file produced by Google Inc." as a track name.
The server can fix its own rule without a new probe.
Of the open-source players and tools we read, only MediaInfo filters generic handler names, and its rule misses "Core Media Audio" and "ISO Media file produced by Google Inc.".
VLC, mpv and MKVToolNix never use a handler name as a track title.
But GPAC's MP4Box writes the track name that its `name` import option sets into the handler name, so a file from MP4Box carries that name nowhere else.
No HLS writer we read escapes a title in `NAME`, because none of them writes text from the file there.
VLC's and FFmpeg's HLS readers treat a backslash as an escape, so the server replaces the backslash too.
FFmpeg's MOV and WebM muxers write neither the commentary nor the audio description disposition (ADR 0032), so a file that they wrote marks a commentary only in its title.
The source's format and layout tell apart two untitled tracks in one language.
The delivered codec would tell apart nothing, because every audio rendition of a stream session has the same codec.
RFC 8216 lets an audio rendition carry the describes-video characteristic.
Apple's authoring specification requires that characteristic and `AUTOSELECT=YES` on an audio description rendition, and says that its name should show that it is audio description.
On macOS 26.6.2, AVFoundation's `displayName` for an audio rendition was the language alone, such as "English", whatever the `NAME`, and "English AD" with the describes-video characteristic.
The `NAME` reached the rendition's title metadata unchanged, middle dot included.
For a subtitle rendition, `displayName` used the `NAME`.
So an Apple client labels an audio rendition from its title metadata, and an audio description name that keeps its flag shows no doubled token in `displayName`.
FFmpeg reads an MPEG-TS audio description marked by an AC-3 or E-AC-3 descriptor as `descriptions` alone, so the flag reads both dispositions (ADR 0032).
A music-and-effects track has no dialogue, and a dependent track holds only what a receiver mixes into the main track, so a player that picks either by itself plays the film without its dialogue.
For the same reason the default track passes over commentary, audio description and music-and-effects tracks while another track remains.
A copied E-AC-3 track with Atmos keeps its JOC objects in identical packets, so its name can say Atmos.
Evidence: <<evidence-subtitle-cases>>, <<evidence-handler-names>>, <<evidence-audio-names>>.

[[decision-cue-text]]
=== Extraction repairs SubRip that is not UTF-8 and judges each track on its own output

A SubRip cue that is not valid UTF-8 is converted to UTF-8 before FFmpeg reads the cue.
The worker chooses the cue's source code page by the track's language.
Bytes that the chosen code page leaves undefined become U+FFFD, the Unicode replacement character.

Each extracted track is judged on the track's own output.
The server lists a damaged track like any other track.
A damaged track gets no label for viewers, and neither the UI nor the API changes for a damaged track.
The worker reports a track's loss only in a log line and in a worker metric that counts lost lines, labeled by reason.

This ADR records two limits of the sources and does not fix them.
A forced line can be missing from the full track of the same language.
The full track is the track that holds every line, not only the forced lines.
FFmpeg's SubRip decoder drops text between a `<` and a later `>`.

*Why:* FFmpeg's text decoders reject every cue that is not valid UTF-8.
When a track loses more than two thirds of its cues, FFmpeg exits with an error.
In a job that extracts every track in one pass, that error would fail every track.
On a synthetic German track in ISO-8859-1, FFmpeg kept only 45 of 100 cues.
The per-cue conversion gave 100 of 100 correct cues on each of 8 synthetic tracks in six code pages.
One of those tracks was a UTF-8 track with 5 windows-1252 cues, and a conversion of that whole track gave 35 of 100 correct cues.
Nothing in Matroska or MP4 ties a forced track's lines to the full track of the same language.
SubRip defines no escape for `<`, so no reader can tell such text from a tag for certain.
Evidence: <<evidence-subrip>>.

[[decision-captions]]
=== Captions carried in the video become WebVTT subtitle renditions

CEA-608 and CEA-708 captions are broadcast captions that a video stream carries inside its frames.
Captions carried in the video become WebVTT subtitle renditions.
The extraction job extracts those captions from the source.
The probe detects caption data by decoding the video's keyframes.
The server lists a caption rendition only when the extraction finds at least one cue.

*Why:* A header probe marks captions only when FFmpeg's decoder meets caption data in the few frames that it decodes to read the stream parameters.
A recording whose captions start later shows no captions in that report.
Decoding only keyframes found caption data in 0.11 to 0.14 s for the first 10 s of a 1080p H.264 file.
A full decode of the same 10 s took 0.71 to 0.77 s.
Caption data proves only that a caption channel exists, and a broadcast can send padding with no text.
The extracted WebVTT rendition showed in all six tested players.
With the captions left in the video and signaled by the playlist, WebKit's native HLS and AVPlayer listed the caption track but showed no cues.
SVT-AV1 encodes kept caption data on 0 of 2,038 frames, so the captions come from the source rather than from an encoded variant.
Evidence: <<evidence-captions>>.

[[decision-late-extraction]]
=== A stream session never waits for subtitle extraction

A playback session is one viewing of a media title.
A playback session survives the replacement of its stream sessions and holds the format-attempt budget.
The format-attempt budget limits how many output formats the server chooses in one playback session.
A stream session that starts before its file's extraction finishes plays at once without subtitle renditions.
The stream session never waits for the extraction.
When the extraction finishes, the server announces the finished extraction over the subscription of link:0042-graphql-subscriptions-run-over-websocket-until-token-expiry.adoc[ADR 0042].
The client then offers the new subtitle tracks.
When the viewer's preference asks for subtitles, the client switches to the subtitles by itself.
To switch, the client replaces the stream session inside the playback session at the viewer's position.
The automatic switch uses none of the format-attempt budget.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/508[streamarr/streamarr-server#508] adds the notice and the automatic switch.

*Why:* Viewers want video to start fast.
The extraction job reads the whole of a Matroska file.
On network storage at 8 MB/s, a 20 GB Matroska file would take about 40 minutes, a figure derived from the read rate.
A subtitle choice is the viewer's choice, not a format error, so the switch spends none of the format-attempt budget.

[[decision-probe-facts]]
=== Probe version 4 reports the titles, handler names, hearing-impaired flags and end times, and a later ticket adds caption detection

Renditions need five facts that probe version 3 does not record.
The facts are each audio and subtitle stream's title, handler name and hearing-impaired flag, each stream's end time, and whether the video carries captions.
Probe version 4 of link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032] already reports every one of these facts except the captions.
The titles, handler names, flags and end times come from the tags, dispositions and headers that the probe's one ffprobe pass reads.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/503[streamarr/streamarr-server#503] uses the audio titles and handler names.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/60[streamarr/streamarr-server#60] uses the subtitle titles, handler names and hearing-impaired flags.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/511[streamarr/streamarr-server#511] uses the end times.
These three issues leave the probe version unchanged.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/505[streamarr/streamarr-server#505] adds caption detection, which decodes the video's keyframes.
That ticket increases the probe version when the ticket merges.
The server then probes existing media files again by itself.
link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032's probe version 7] adds each audio stream's channel layout, sample format and bit depth, the dispositions that the names and the default track use, each FLAC stream's minimum block size, and the `comment` disposition of each subtitle stream.
The audio dispositions are `comment`, `visual_impaired`, `descriptions`, `original`, `dub`, `clean_effects` and `dependent`.
Until the server stores a file's probe version 7 outcome, the file's names carry only the flags that titles mark, its audio names show channel counts instead of layouts, its encodes take their bit depth from the source's codec, and no Apple client gets a copy of its FLAC tracks.

[[decision-image-subtitles]]
=== For a client without a subtitle layer, an image subtitle that the viewer selects is burned into the video

This section applies to a client without a subtitle layer, and to an image format that the client's layer does not draw (<<decision-subtitle-layer>>).
An image subtitle track is a PGS, VobSub or DVB track, whose lines are pictures rather than text.
An image subtitle track is burned into the video when the viewer selects the track.
To burn in a subtitle is to draw the subtitle's pictures into the video frames while the worker encodes the video.
An image subtitle track never becomes a rendition.
The server never converts an image subtitle track to text.
A stream session with a burned-in subtitle always encodes its video (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013]), even when the client could play a stream copy.
On a change to or from an image subtitle, the client replaces the stream session inside the playback session at the viewer's position.
The playback session is the one viewing of a media title that holds the stream sessions and the format-attempt budget.
The format-attempt budget limits how many output formats the server chooses in one playback session.
That replacement uses none of the format-attempt budget.
Under link:0033-transcode-worker-is-a-separate-service.adoc[ADR 0033], the server dispatches a job that burns in a subtitle only to a worker that advertises burn-in.

When subtitles are off, a forced image subtitle track whose language matches the selected audio is burned in automatically.
So the stream session encodes its video.
A forced image track that is burned in automatically counts as a selected subtitle.
Under link:0045-clients-direct-play-covered-files-and-use-platform-players.adoc[ADR 0045], the client plays the source file directly only when the client's declaration covers every selected subtitle.
So the file plays over HLS rather than by direct play.
An audio switch that changes which forced image track applies replaces the stream session inside the playback session at the viewer's position.
That replacement uses none of the format-attempt budget.

*Why:* No code burns a subtitle into the video today, so no client can show an image subtitle.
Blu-ray discs carry graphic subtitles as PGS, and DVDs carry subtitles only as VobSub.
So a disc copy that keeps those subtitles holds image subtitles.
A subtitle choice is the viewer's choice, not a format error, so the replacement spends none of the format-attempt budget.
A forced track carries lines that a viewer needs even with subtitles off.

[[decision-seek-after-change]]
=== AVFoundation clients seek in two steps after an audio or subtitle change

AVFoundation is Apple's media framework, which AVPlayer and WebKit's native HLS use.
When a seek follows an audio or subtitle change inside the same media segment, AVFoundation clients seek in two steps.
The first step seeks to the start of that media segment, and the second step seeks to the target.
The iOS client also covers the picture with a still of the current frame, from the seek until the target frame is on screen.
If the cover fails on the iPhone, the iOS client seeks in place after every change instead of covering the picture.
A seek in place is a seek to the current position.
The two-step seek stays the minimum fix in every case.

*Why:* In AVFoundation, any change of audio or subtitle selection drops the buffered media before about the playhead.
The dropped media includes the keyframe of the current GOP.
A GOP, or group of pictures, is the run of frames from one keyframe to the next.
AVFoundation keeps the rest of the buffer, so a later seek into that GOP fetches nothing.
AVFoundation then has no keyframe to decode from, and holds the old picture until the next keyframe.
With the 6 s GOP that the worker used in the measurements, the freeze lasted up to about 5 s.
With the two-step seek, AVPlayer showed the target picture after a median of 216 ms, against 101 ms for a seek with no earlier change.
The cover keeps every earlier picture off the screen until the target frame appears.
hls.js did not freeze, so the web client needs neither fix where the web client plays through hls.js.
Evidence: <<evidence-subtitle-cases>>.

[[decision-device-checks]]
=== Mac browser checks gate the tickets, and the TV checks run once at the end of the epic

The player checks in Mac browsers, hls.js in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit, are merge gates on the ticket that delivers the behavior that they cover.
The checks on the Apple TV 4K with AVPlayer and on the SHIELD TV Pro with a Media3 player run once, after the epic https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/496[streamarr/streamarr-server#496] closes, and gate nothing.
The rules of this ADR hold whatever a check shows.
A failing Mac browser check comes back to its ticket as a question before the ticket merges, and a failing TV check becomes a new issue.
The TV pass plays multichannel FLAC, padded layouts, a copied E-AC-3 track with Atmos under `CHANNELS="16/JOC"` and multichannel Opus through the receiver, and compares a copy's level with an encode's.
It also checks whether AVPlayer trims a kept priming frame by its edit list (<<decision-priming>>), and listens for junctions between attempts, FLAC copied from Matroska, 96 kHz FLAC, switches between renditions at different sample rates and the audio description choice under tvOS's accessibility settings.
The player check of the audio renditions ticket, https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/503[streamarr/streamarr-server#503], runs the real server and worker.
That check shows again that hls.js plays audio renditions without the priming frame.
That check also plays a Matroska source whose copied AAC starts off the AAC encoder's frame grid, beside encoded tracks whose grids count from the copy's first sample.
It also checks that WebKit drops a FLAC copy whose minimum block size is below 192 samples, as AVFoundation does, and plays the FLAC encode that replaces it.
The Apple client's ticket, https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-apple/issues/4[streamarr/streamarr-apple#4], checks the cover of <<decision-seek-after-change>> on an iPhone.

Four cases have no measurement.
The cases are an ALAC source, sources at 44.1 kHz and at 96 kHz with a switch between renditions at different sample rates, and an audio request for the segment just before a relocated attempt's first segment.
A relocation starts a new job attempt at the requested segment when that segment lies before the running attempt's first segment, or too far beyond the running attempt's output (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019]).
The acceptance tests of https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/503[streamarr/streamarr-server#503] cover the four cases.

[[explicitly-deferred]]
=== Explicitly deferred

* Deliver image subtitles as renditions.
* Deliver teletext subtitles, which come from DVB broadcast recordings.
* Declare more than 6 channels from the web client.
The web client's declaration (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-web/pull/130[streamarr/streamarr-web#130]) always carries an AAC capability entry for HLS.
It adds AC-3, E-AC-3, Opus, FLAC and ALAC entries only when the browser's `decodingInfo()` confirms them.
It probes 6 and 2 channels for each codec, so no entry accepts more than 6 channels, and Opus stays at 2 channels in WebKit.
Lifting the 6-channel cap waits for the speaker-free test of https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-web/issues/29[streamarr/streamarr-web#29], which splits the player's output into its channels with a `ChannelSplitterNode` and checks which channel carries each tone.
Real speakers only confirm that test.
Firefox plays 7.1 AAC in AAC's own channel order and plays 5.1 AAC in WAVE order.
* Mix a dependent narration track into the main track, so that a viewer who needs audio description hears both.
* A night mode, a dialogue boost or a loudness normalisation that the viewer turns on.
The viewer's platform already offers such a mode, for example tvOS's Reduce Loud Sounds.
The server's audio work ends at a bit-perfect copy or a correct downmix (link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc[ADR 0050]), and such a mode would change the mix after that.
A later decision that adds one would read each track's integrated loudness, loudness range and true peak from a background scan (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-version-7[ADR 0032]).
* Measure the FLAC encodes of a file without a lossless track in ADR 0054's bitrate scan.
* Allow more than one audio codec per stream session for Apple clients.
Apple clients get that exception only if a check shows that their switches between codecs stay in sync.
* Test audio and subtitle renditions on cast receivers.
* Test audio and subtitle renditions with the VAAPI, QSV, AMF and RKMPP encoders, which https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/14[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#14] adds.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/56[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#56] adds NVENC, and issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/57[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#57] adds VideoToolbox, so neither encoder is deferred.

[[consequences]]
== Consequences

* A viewer switches audio language and text subtitles, and the video does not restart.
On a client without a subtitle layer, choosing an image subtitle track, or leaving one, replaces the stream session, because the job attempts burn that track into the video.
Playback then resumes at the viewer's position.
* Every stream session with a burned-in subtitle encodes its video, which uses worker capacity.
Automatic burn-in applies only while subtitles are off, to a file with a forced image track in the selected audio's language.
Each such file then plays over HLS with an encoded video, instead of a stream copy or direct play.
* In some players, a switch holds the picture for a short time.
During a hold, the same frame stays on screen.
The median hold within 3 s of a switch was about 0.2 s in WebKit with hls.js and 0.17 s in AVPlayer.
In WebKit's native HLS with the sound unmuted, the median hold was 0.45 s.
In Chromium and Firefox, the median hold was 64 to 85 ms.
Playback without a switch held the picture about as long.
* hls.js flushes the whole audio buffer on every switch and then fetches the buffer again.
In the measurements, hls.js fetched 25 to 45 segments again after each switch.
Clients may cap their forward buffer to bound that traffic.
* Every language exists from the start of the stream session.
A relocation restarts only the requested variant's job (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019]).
The relocation's new job attempt writes every selected audio track, so no language needs a relocation of its own.
The server needs no audio-only job kind.
The server counts no worker slots for audio.
The server needs no policy for when to start a language.
* Every running job attempt carries every selected language.
Each encoded language adds about 1.2 CPU seconds per minute of media to each running job attempt.
Three languages added 8 % to a 360p attempt and 0.7 % to a 1080p attempt.
* Whenever several variants run, each running variant uploads every language.
Each language adds about 17 to 25 KB of upload per second of media.
The measurements found 100 to 150 KB per language in each 6 s segment.
* An audio rendition's segment `N` is ready as soon as any running variant delivers that rendition's segment `N`.
Each audio segment comes from whichever running variant delivers that segment first.
* Consecutive audio segments can come from different job attempts.
That happens after a replacement attempt, after a relocation, and when two running variants started at different segments.
A junction is the segment boundary where one job attempt's output ends and another attempt's output begins.
Copied audio stays byte-identical across a junction.
Encoded audio differs from the earlier attempt's encoded audio, because the decoders start on a different packet.
After decoding, the junction adds at most 0.2 dB to that difference.
Every encode meets the earlier attempt's output without a gap or an overlap, and a FLAC or ALAC encode of a lossless track decodes to the same samples across the junction.
* Each encoded track's decode starts one segment period before its attempt's first segment, so every attempt decodes up to one period of audio that it discards.
* Two copied tracks on different grids meet with a gap or an overlap of up to half a frame when the viewer switches between them.
* The first 21 ms of an AAC rendition decode without the overlap that the priming frame would supply, at about 3 dB signal-to-noise ratio for a tone.
No player showed an error.
An AC-3 or E-AC-3 rendition loses about 27 ms of real audio at the start of the stream, and an Opus rendition loses about 14 ms.
* A file's dependent narration track gets no rendition.
A viewer who needs audio description hears it only when the file also holds a complete audio description track.
* A copied E-AC-3 track with Atmos keeps its objects, but FFmpeg 8.1.3 wrote FATE's Atmos sample's `dec3` box without `flag_ec3_extension_type_a`.
Media3 reports E-AC-3 with Atmos only when that flag is set, so a Media3 player treats such a copy as plain E-AC-3.
Whether a receiver still shows Atmos for the copy is an Apple TV and SHIELD check at the end of the epic.
* An Apple client loses the copy of a FLAC or E-AC-3 track when the file's default track uses another codec.
The one-codec rule encodes that track to the session's audio codec.
* When the session copies a FLAC or ALAC default track, every other track that is not already in that codec is encoded to it.
A FLAC or ALAC encode of a lossy track writes the decoded audio at 16 bits with dither, so its bitrate follows the decoded audio, not the source track's bitrate.
In the measurements, FLAC of a 128 kb/s AAC film track ran 573 to 818 kb/s, and of a speech-like synthetic track 193 kb/s.
The server counts such a stereo track at about 1,000 kb/s, eight times a 128 kb/s source, and its peak at 1,536 kb/s.
Such an encode is not lossless and adds no quality, because the lossy source already lost it.
* Under link:0054-workers-measure-each-stream-bitrate-and-a-copy-advertises-its-measured-peak.adoc[ADR 0054], every variant's cost and `BANDWIDTH` count the largest audio rendition.
Until ADR 0054's bitrate scan measures it, a FLAC rendition counts 65 % of its output's PCM bitrate, as an ALAC rendition always does, and peaks at the full rate, so an encoded FLAC or ALAC track can count larger than the copied default track, and it then raises every variant's cost and `BANDWIDTH`.
Such a track then becomes the step track (ADR 0050).
A step below the session's audio caps every rendition.
* A client that declares DTS or TrueHD keeps the copy of such a default track, and with it the Atmos or DTS:X objects.
A track in another codec then reaches that client only through a replacement of the stream session, which restarts the video at the viewer's position.
* No tested client declares TrueHD today: AVPlayer has no decoder, and Media3's HLS player does not regroup TrueHD for passthrough.
Until a client declares it, a TrueHD track reaches every client as FLAC or as ADR 0050's lossy encode, and its Atmos objects reach a client only through an E-AC-3 track with Atmos.
* A job that copies TrueHD runs FFmpeg's MP4 muxer under `-strict experimental`.
* A client without FLAC, TrueHD or DTS gets a 7.1 lossless track as at most 5.1 E-AC-3, because FFmpeg's E-AC-3 encoder stops at 5.1.
* A client that declares FLAC gets a lossy default track that it cannot copy, such as DTS, Opus or MP3, as FLAC of the decoded audio.
That encode is much larger than the source, about 573 to 818 kb/s for 128 kb/s AAC in stereo, and ADR 0054's scan does not measure it, so it counts 65 % of its PCM bitrate and can push a session under a maximum bitrate to a lossy step sooner.
* A FLAC track whose STREAMINFO minimum block size is below 192 samples reaches Apple clients as a FLAC encode with the same samples, and so does every FLAC track until the server stores the file's probe version 7 outcome.
* The server writes `16/JOC` for every copied E-AC-3 track with Atmos, whatever the track's real complexity index.
* A track marked by a word in its title can be wrong when a title uses a word in another sense; the default track then passes over it while another track remains.
* A FLAC or ALAC session above 48 kHz under a maximum bitrate keeps its codec at 48 or 44.1 kHz, resampled, before it goes lossy.
* A client that declares FLAC with eight channels, but neither TrueHD nor DTS, gets a TrueHD or DTS-HD MA default track as lossless 7.1, and every other track of the file as FLAC.
The Atmos or DTS:X objects of such a track are lost.
* A lossless `2.1`, `3.1`, `3.0(back)`, `4.0`, `4.1`, `6.0` or `7.0` track reaches the player as FLAC with silent channels, and the player shows the padded layout, such as 6.1 for a `4.0` track.
A `7.1(wide)` or `7.1(wide-side)` track loses its lossless encode to ADR 0050's downmix.
* A player that receives multichannel FLAC decodes it to multichannel PCM, which only some audio paths carry.
A soundbar on legacy HDMI ARC takes stereo PCM or a Dolby bitstream, so there the player plays the FLAC rendition as stereo, where an E-AC-3 encode would have reached the soundbar as 5.1.
The server cannot tell such a soundbar from a receiver, so the client's declaration carries that choice, for example from a viewer setting for lossless audio.
The Apple TV check stays an epic-end check and does not gate this decision.
* An encode of an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track plays at the level that a receiver gives the copy, which is quieter than a decode without dialogue normalization by 31 dB less the track's dialogue level, 4 to 5 dB for the FATE samples.
* Every 16-bit encode of decoded audio carries TPDF dither noise, about 5 dB above plain 16-bit rounding noise, in place of distortion that follows the signal.
* The worker encodes FLAC, Opus and ALAC as well as AAC, AC-3 and E-AC-3.
Each new audio encode only adds to the worker contract, as link:0033-transcode-worker-is-a-separate-service.adoc[ADR 0033] requires.
* An audio track that ends at least one media segment before the video is always encoded, even when the client could play a copy of the track.
* Once the server delivers audio renditions, video-only variants replace link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037]'s multiplexed variants.
An init per rendition then replaces ADR 0037's single init per variant.
* The worker contract gains a rendition label on uploads, audio and WebVTT content types, the extraction job and burn-in.
Each change only adds to the contract, as link:0033-transcode-worker-is-a-separate-service.adoc[ADR 0033] requires.
The worker advertises a capability for each addition.
* Every player places a cue relative to the first video frame, exactly as the player places the picture.
WebKit starts each cue about 0.2 s late, both in its native HLS and with hls.js.
WebKit starts each cue late because WebKit evaluates cues only as often as it fires its `timeupdate` event.
* In AVFoundation players, a seek after an audio or subtitle change into the same GOP froze the picture until the next keyframe.
With the 6 s GOP that the worker used in the measurements, the freeze lasted up to about 5 s.
The two-step seek and the iOS client's cover remove that freeze.
The target picture then shows about 115 ms later than after a seek with no earlier change.
* With `AUTOSELECT=NO`, Safari no longer turns on subtitles by itself when the audio language differs from the viewer's language.
The client chooses subtitles.
A forced track still selects itself in AVFoundation players.
The web client selects a forced track itself, because hls.js ignores `FORCED`.
The Apple and Android clients select an SDH track themselves when the system captions setting is on.
* A damaged subtitle track appears in the menu with lines missing.
Only the worker's log line and metric show the loss.
* Extracting captions decodes the video of the whole file once.
No measurement covers that cost on a full-length file.
* A viewer who starts a file before its extraction finishes watches without subtitles until the extraction finishes.
After the extraction, a viewer who wants subtitles gets a new stream session at the same position.
* Each ticket that adds a probe fact makes the server probe every media file again.
* The extraction job reads a whole Matroska file once per media file.
From an MP4 file, the extraction job reads only the subtitle samples.
On network storage, subtitle extraction from a large Matroska file takes minutes.
The durable job does that work before playback.
* For a client without a subtitle layer, WebVTT drops ASS styling.
* Every client needs a subtitle layer, with libass for ASS and SSA and a PGS decoder, to show every subtitle without burn-in.
* On a client with the layer, a subtitle choice never replaces the stream session and never keeps a file off direct play.
* The server sends original subtitle data, so a client that has the layer parses SubRip, ASS, SSA and PGS itself.
* A Profile's language overrides grow with each series, season and title that the viewer changes.
* The web and Apple clients gain audio and subtitle menus.

== Rejected Alternatives

* An override that names one track fails on the next episode, whose file can list its tracks in another order.
* Overrides shared by every Profile of a Household force one viewer's language on another.
* Remembering a switch only for the episode makes the viewer switch again in every episode.
* Converting ASS and SSA to WebVTT for every client loses their styling and positions.
* Burning in every image subtitle for every client encodes the video whenever a viewer turns on a PGS track.
* Downloading missing subtitles from an online subtitle database needs an account with an outside service and tells that service which files the library holds, so it is out of scope.

* Audio that stays multiplexed into every variant forces new job attempts for every variant when the viewer changes language.
The video then restarts.
That design also encodes every language once per variant.
* One FFmpeg job per audio track reads the source region again for each language.
At three languages, those jobs read 3.0 times the video attempt's bytes.
An earlier measurement found that one such job delayed the video's first segment by 0.45 s on 8 MB/s network storage.
In one form of that design, a language's job started only when a viewer selected the language.
Every player then froze the picture for about as long as the job took to produce its first segment.
In the other form of that design, every job started with the stream session.
Each job then needed its own worker slot, and a worker with one slot refused the second job.
A backward seek then relocated four jobs instead of one.
* The server could designate one variant whose job attempts publish the audio renditions.
link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019] starts only the requested variant.
So the designated variant may not be running after the viewer's variant changes.
The server would then track which variant is designated.
The server would also need a rule that moves the designation to the newly requested variant.
Keeping the first delivery of each audio segment needs no designation and no such rule.
* One separate job per stream session could write every audio rendition.
That job reads the source region a second time and needs its own worker slot.
A seek relocates that job as well as the variant's job attempt.
* An audio rendition whose segment 0 keeps the AAC priming frame at a negative decode time can stop hls.js for good, with or without a language switch.
* Keeping the priming frame with an edit list for every client before the Apple TV check plays the first 21.3 ms late in Firefox, and possibly in AVPlayer.
* Packaging each rendition twice, with the frame for Chromium and AVPlayer and without it for Firefox, doubles the audio packaging.
* Snapping a copied track to the encoder's grid moves its authored sync by up to half a frame, 16 ms for AC-3 at 48 kHz and 64 ms for AAC at 8 kHz.
FFmpeg's AC-3 encoder starts each frame 256 samples before a 32 ms point, so the snap moved an authored +12 ms delay to +26.7 ms.
* Starting each encode at a plain seek to the boundary leaves gaps or overlaps of up to 59 ms between attempts.
* Snapping only the non-default of two copies on different grids moves a bit-exact copy.
* Dropping audio before media time zero from multiplexed variant segments as well changes segments that need no change.
hls.js places a multiplexed fragment by the fragment's video track, and ADR 0037 keeps that audio in multiplexed segments.
* An init that also writes the language in an `elng` box, or that keeps the source's language tag, stores the language in a second place.
That second copy of the language can disagree with the playlist's `LANGUAGE` attribute.
* Mixed audio codecs in one stream session, with the web client seeking to its own position after a switch that changes the codec, rely on a seek that nobody tested.
* One audio codec per stream session for hls.js clients only needs a second rule for the other clients.
Nobody measured whether Apple players stay in sync after a switch that changes the codec.
* Accepting the drift leaves the audio 90 to 140 ms late after a switch that changes the codec, until the next seek.
* Keeping the copied default track and encoding every other track to AAC mixes codecs in one stream session.
After a switch that changed the codec, hls.js left the audio 90 to 140 ms late until the next seek.
* Switching the whole stream session to AAC whenever another track needs an encode loses the copy of a lossless or passthrough default track, such as FLAC or E-AC-3.
* Encoding DTS or TrueHD with FFmpeg's `dca` or `truehd` encoder relies on encoders that FFmpeg marks as experimental.
* Encoding a default track that cannot be copied always to AAC gives up surround that the client declares in E-AC-3 or AC-3.
* Counting a FLAC or ALAC encode at its full PCM bitrate counts a 128 kb/s commentary track at 1,536 kb/s, while FLAC of film audio averaged at most 53 % of that rate.
* Keeping the 65 % count for good lets a lossy commentary's FLAC encode cap a lossless default track at a lossy step.
* Counting an ALAC encode as its FLAC measurement plus 5 % understates it: ALAC of synthetic tones and noise wrote 34 to 39 % more than FLAC.
* Taking the FLAC bytes from the segments that the worker delivers during playback measures only the segments that viewers watched, after the first stream session already chose its steps.
* Capping a FLAC or ALAC encode's peak below its PCM bitrate understates a noisy segment, such as the 94 % that decoded white noise reached.
* Encoding a lossless default track to E-AC-3 before FLAC keeps 5.1 behind a soundbar on legacy ARC.
It also turns a 7.1 lossless track into 5.1 lossy audio for every client that declares eight-channel FLAC.
The client's declaration can make that choice for its own audio path.
* Decoding AC-3 and E-AC-3 with FFmpeg's default compression changes the dynamics of every encode, a FLAC encode included.
* Decoding without dialogue normalization makes a step from a copy to an encode louder by the track's normalization, up to 5 dB in the FATE samples.
* Converting floating point to 16 bits without dither leaves distortion products that follow the signal, at −106 dBFS for a −90 dBFS tone.
* FFmpeg's default resampler settings roll off the top octave by 2 dB and fold content above the new Nyquist frequency back at −14 dB.
* Unconstrained Opus VBR takes bits from the dialogue channel and runs 10 % over its rate in its busiest fixed 6 s window.
* Opus at Xiph's starting rates, 5.1 at 384 kb/s, left 11.5 to 21.4 % of frames disturbed in the worst channel, against 2.4 to 6.9 % at 448 kb/s.
* Never copying Opus gives up copies that every tested player plays, because stereo Opus played in all six players.
* Writing a lossless track that FLAC does not carry under its own layout name moves its channels, such as a `4.0` track's centre to the back left.
* Encoding such a track to E-AC-3 or AC-3 keeps its layout but loses samples, and a stereo FLAC downmix changes the mix and can run ADR 0050's limiter.
* FFmpeg's default Opus mapping plays a 5.0 track's dialogue from the rear right.
* Renditions for the default track and the viewer's preferred languages only leave the file's other languages out of the menu.
* Encoding DTS whatever the client declares refuses a copy to a client that can play the codec, such as Media3 with an HDMI receiver.
* Never copying TrueHD loses the Atmos objects for a client that decodes TrueHD or passes it to a receiver, although FFmpeg's experimental muxing kept every packet byte-identical.
* Encoding a TrueHD or DTS default track whenever another track needs its codec keeps every switch free of restarts, but a commentary track then costs the viewer the Atmos copy.
* Leaving the tracks that cannot reach a TrueHD or DTS session's codec out of the menu hides a commentary or another language from the viewer.
* Encoding a lossy default track that cannot be copied to E-AC-3, AC-3 or AAC when the client declares FLAC adds a second lossy generation.
* Choosing among same-language tracks by file order can pick a stereo track over a surround one, and ignores whether a track is lossless.
* Copying a disc's lossy track before a FLAC encode of its lossless track gives up lossless audio for the client that declares FLAC.
* Refusing every FLAC copy whose block size is below 192 samples, for every client, gives up copies that other players accept.
* Writing the bed's channel count for a copied E-AC-3 track with Atmos leaves AVFoundation nothing above the segments that marks object audio.
* Matching a wider word list, which adds "director", "description" and "narration", marks a main track titled "Director's Cut" as commentary and a dependent narration as a complete description.
* Encoding a TrueHD track with Atmos to FLAC when the file holds a copyable E-AC-3 track with Atmos keeps a lossless bed but loses the height objects that the E-AC-3 copy keeps.
* Stepping a hi-res FLAC or ALAC session straight to a lossy codec gives up a FLAC or ALAC step at 48 kHz that adds no coding loss beyond the resampling.
* Marking the segments after an audio track's end with `EXT-X-GAP` stopped hls.js for good on an iPhone after a switch into the ended track.
* Padding a copied track with silence in the track's own codec, without encoding the track, needs a way to make silence in every codec that the worker copies.
Encoding the track pads every codec in the same way.
* Leaving the ended track out of later job attempts needs `EXT-X-GAP` or a playlist that ends early.
AVPlayer stopped the whole stream where a rendition's playlist ended early.
* Writing every job attempt with FFmpeg's `empty_moov` flag does not help an attempt whose encoded track has already ended.
* Accepting the stall leaves an attempt that starts after a track's end without output until the input ends.
* Padding audio renditions only leaves multiplexed audio unpadded, and AVPlayer stopped at the end of multiplexed audio in 12 of 12 runs.
* A 10 s worker closing margin adds a second 10 s wait after FFmpeg's interleave limit and saves no audio.
The audio that arrives late comes only at the very end of the output, where no margin reaches.
* Subtitle extraction for each stream session reads a whole Matroska file every time a viewer starts playback.
At 8 MB/s, a 20 GB file would take about 40 minutes, a figure derived from the read rate.
An extraction of only a time window misses a line that is already on screen when the window starts.
* Recording image subtitles as unsupported leaves every disc copy that keeps PGS or VobSub subtitles without them.
* Delivering image subtitles as timed-image renditions in this work would widen the work beyond audio and text tracks.
* Burning in a forced image track only when the viewer selects the track leaves out lines that a viewer needs even with subtitles off.
* Seeking in place after every change as the first fix lengthens the longest gap between shown frames at every change.
That seek also fetches the whole forward buffer again.
The seek in place stays the fallback if the iOS cover fails.
* Relying on 2 to 3 s segments to bound the freeze does not remove the freeze, because the freeze lasts until the next keyframe.
A 2 s GOP still froze the picture for about 0.58 s, and a shorter GOP costs bitrate on every stream.
* Holding the tickets until the TV checks run delays them, while the Mac browser checks already gate each ticket.
* Gating each ticket on the Apple TV and SHIELD checks ties every merge to hardware sessions that the end-of-epic pass covers once.
* Optical character recognition could convert image subtitles to text.
That conversion adds a recognition engine to the worker.
The engine's output can differ from the image.
No measurement assessed the engine's accuracy.
* A constant `X-TIMESTAMP-MAP` offset of 900000 ticks suits MPEG-TS output whose timestamps start at 10 s.
On Streamarr's zero-based fMP4 timeline, that offset put every cue 10 s late in every player.
* `AUTOSELECT=YES` on every subtitle rendition lets Safari override the viewer's choice to turn subtitles off.
* `AUTOSELECT=YES` on SDH renditions, as Apple's authoring guide says, let WebKit turn on subtitles by itself in 3 of 3 runs.
* Leaving forced tracks to hls.js on the web never shows forced lines, because hls.js ignores `FORCED`.
* Names from the language and the flags alone ignore the stream's title, which can tell apart tracks in one language that the flags cannot, such as a commentary.
* Names without the SDH token can duplicate the name of the regular track in the same language.
* Numbered names as the naming scheme tell the viewer nothing about the track.
The server numbers only names that still collide.
* Always keeping the SDH token doubles the token in AVPlayer, which appends " SDH" itself.
* Naming an audio rendition by its delivered codec tells apart nothing, because every audio rendition of a stream session has the same codec.
* Leaving "Audio description" out of an audio description rendition's name, as the SDH rule does for SDH, leaves only the language in players that show the `NAME`, such as hls.js clients.
AVFoundation's `displayName` for audio ignores the `NAME`, so keeping the flag doubles nothing there.
* Leaving duplicate names violates RFC 8216.
AVPlayer and WebKit's native HLS then exposed only one of two renditions named "English".
* Telling colliding names apart by the stream index shows the viewer a number that means nothing outside the file.
* Listing a dependent narration track with `AUTOSELECT=NO` lets a viewer pick a track that plays the narration alone.
* Leaving Atmos and DTS:X out of every name hides that a copy keeps the objects.
* Converting a whole SubRip track with FFmpeg's `-sub_charenc` damages the track's UTF-8 cues.
On a UTF-8 track with 5 windows-1252 cues, that conversion gave 35 of 100 correct cues.
* Skipping a SubRip track with invalid cues drops tracks that are mostly readable, such as a track with 80 of 100 valid cues.
* Replacing invalid bytes with U+FFFD alone loses every letter of a Hebrew or Russian track.
* Labeling a damaged subtitle track "(incomplete)", or hiding the track from viewers, adds states that viewers see.
The track's remaining lines still help the viewer, and the log line and the worker metric expose the loss.
* Failing the whole extraction job for one damaged track loses every other track of the file.
* Showing the loss to a ServerAdmin in the UI or the API adds UI or API work that the log line and the worker metric make unnecessary.
* Merging forced cues into the full track changes the source's text.
* A SubRip parser of the worker's own replaces FFmpeg's decoder, and it would still have to guess, because SubRip defines no escape for `<`.
* Keeping captions deferred leaves the captions of broadcast recordings unused.
* Keeping CEA-608 captions in the video and signaling them with the `CLOSED-CAPTIONS` attribute shows no cues in WebKit's native HLS and AVPlayer.
SVT-AV1 encodes also drop that caption data.
* Delivering captions both as a rendition and in the video lists the captions twice in players.
* Showing subtitles only from the next playback, when extraction finishes during playback, leaves the viewer without subtitles until the viewer starts again.
* Waiting for extraction when the viewer's preference asks for subtitles delays the start by as long as the extraction takes.
For a 20 GB Matroska file on 8 MB/s storage, that wait is about 40 minutes.
* Folding caption detection into probe version 4 would hold probe version 4 behind a change that decodes the video's keyframes.
The stream titles, handler names and hearing-impaired flags come from the same ffprobe pass as the other version 4 facts, so probe version 4 reports all three.

== Evidence

The first measurements used one 300 s excerpt of a Creative Commons film at 1080p and 23.976 fps.
The excerpt had a keyframe every 2 s, and the measurements used 6 s segments.
The excerpt carried three audio tracks.
The English track was AAC, and the worker copied it.
The two other tracks were AC-3, and the worker encoded them to stereo AAC at 128 kb/s.
The excerpt carried four text subtitle tracks: SubRip, ASS, UTF-8 SubRip and WebVTT.
The source was muxed as Matroska, MP4 and MPEG-TS.
Three players ran hls.js 1.7.0, in Chromium 151, Firefox 153 and WebKit 26.5.
The other two players were WebKit's native HLS and AVPlayer on macOS 26.6.2.
Every encode ran in the pinned worker image.
The played variants were libx264 at 360p and 480p.
An independent re-check with new harness code repeated the main results.

[[evidence-switching]]
=== Switching audio renditions

* In 15 runs with 135 audio switches, no run restarted the video and no player hit a fatal error.
The re-check played 12 runs with 156 switches and found no backward jump outside a seek.
* hls.js asked for the new language within 2 to 17 ms.
The native players asked for the new language within 43 to 86 ms.
* The median delay before the new language played was 0.30 s in Chromium and 1.05 s in Firefox.
The re-check measured 0.27 s and 0.99 s.
* The browser's audio output queue causes the delay.
The old language plays on, and then the new language plays.
No silence falls between the two languages.
* The median hold within 3 s of a switch was 64 to 72 ms in Chromium and 82 to 85 ms in Firefox.
Playback without a switch held the picture about as long.
The median hold was 200 ms in WebKit with hls.js and 160 to 171 ms in AVPlayer.
* With the sound unmuted, WebKit's native HLS held the picture at 39 of 39 switches.
The holds lasted 236 to 631 ms, and the median hold was 449 ms.
With the sound muted, WebKit's native HLS held the picture for 52 to 83 ms.
* Chromium's rendered audio kept its offset to the media clock within −0.1 to +0.4 ms across 27 switches.
* hls.js flushed the audio buffer at every switch and fetched 25 to 45 segments again.
In the re-check, hls.js flushed the audio buffer at 91 of 91 switches and fetched 25 to 42 segments again.

[[evidence-video-job]]
=== Audio carried in the video job

* One FFmpeg process per attempt wrote the video and three languages as separate tracks.
A prototype split those tracks.
The split video-only variant segments matched FFmpeg's 7,199 video packets.
Each split encoded language matched its 14,080 packets.
* In the five players, the split output played like one audio-only job per language.
Each player ran each design twice on the same scenario.
Chromium played a switched language after 262 ms with the split output and after 272 ms with separate jobs.
Firefox played a switched language after 1,034 ms with the split output and after 1,004 ms with separate jobs.
Holds, stalls and seek times differed by no more than the spread between runs.
* In 37 runs across Matroska, MP4 and MPEG-TS, no run failed or jumped backwards.
* A junction is the segment boundary where one job attempt's output ends and another attempt's output begins.
In these measurements, each junction joined an attempt that started at 0 and a replacement attempt.
At 96 of 96 audio junctions, the step between attempts was 0 samples.
At every audio junction, the replacement attempt's first audio frame sat on the frame grid of the attempt that started at 0.
Those junctions covered only pairs of source and encoder whose frame lengths match, and a plain seek misses the grid for the other pairs (<<evidence-audio-junctions>>).
At 24 of 24 video junctions, the replacement attempt's first segment started at the frame time that the attempt from 0 gave that segment.
* Copied audio was byte-identical across junctions.
Encoded audio differed, because AC-3 dither and AAC noise substitution depend on where the decoder starts.
Away from a junction, the replacement attempt's encoded audio already differs from the earlier attempt's encoded audio.
After decoding, the junction added −11.1 to +0.2 dB to that difference.
* Audio segments were byte-identical across variants whose job attempts started at the same segment, in 2,208 of 2,208 pairs.
The re-check found 272 of 272.
* Carrying three languages read 0 to 0.5 % more source bytes.
Three audio-only jobs would read 3.0 times the video attempt's bytes for Matroska and MPEG-TS.
For MP4, three audio-only jobs would read 1.6 times the video attempt's bytes.
* Each language added 100 to 150 KB to each 6 s segment.
The encoded languages ran at 128 kb/s, and the copied language ran at 192 kb/s.
* One encoded language cost about 1.2 CPU seconds per minute of media.
The cost was the same in the video attempt and in a separate job.
* Over 5 runs each, the first 1080p segment after a seek took a median of 2.27 s without audio and 2.40 s with three languages.
* In 1,170 of 1,170 cases per track, each audio segment completed in the same FFmpeg fragment as its variant's video segment.
The re-check found 760 of 760.
* In a model of the server's relocation rule, a backward seek relocated once.
With one job per language, the same seek relocated four times.
* After 23 of 1,160 switches, hls.js first asked for the segment before the playhead's segment.
Right after a relocation, that request lies before the new attempt's first segment, so the rule of link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019] would relocate again.
In 20 runs of a model of the server's relocation rule, no second relocation occurred.
* In the audio-only job design, every player froze the picture when a language's job started only at the viewer's selection.
The freeze lasted 0.34 to 1.07 s when the first segment took 0.7 s.
The freeze lasted 5.2 to 7.3 s when the first segment took 6 s.
At low load, FFmpeg alone produced that first segment in 0.04 s when it copied the audio and in 0.23 s when it encoded the audio.
* On MPEG-TS, mapping English alone put media time zero 170.7 ms later than mapping all three languages.
With `-discard none` on the input, media time zero stayed at the container's earliest stream, whatever streams the job mapped.
A source with a subtitle or data stream that starts 1 s or more before the others behaves differently, and link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#evidence-timeline[ADR 0037] measures one.
* From an MP4 source, Firefox with hls.js played the split output from position 0 in 6 of 6 runs.
The previous producer concatenated FFmpeg's fragments.
The previous producer's multiplexed output played 0 of 6 runs cleanly.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037]'s rebuilt segments solve that case on their own.

A control init declared the bitrate.

In that test, segment 0 held several `moof` and `mdat` pairs, and Firefox kept only the first pair.

No parser rejects a bitrate of zero.

[[evidence-priming]]
=== The priming frame

* The AAC priming frame's decode time is −1024 samples.
A version 1 `tfdt` box writes that time as 2^64^ − 1024.
* hls.js 1.7.0 treats that value as an invalid signed decode time.
hls.js keeps 2^32^ − 1 samples as the segment's start.
hls.js logs a warning about that value only when debug logging is on.
* When segment 0 kept the frame, switches at 2, 5 and 9 s stalled playback for good.

32-bit pink noise written as 24-bit FLAC changed 97.6 % of its samples by 0 to −255 32-bit steps, at most −138.5 dBFS, because the encoder drops the low 8 bits.
* `ffmpeg -resamplers` lists only `swr`.
A 21 kHz tone at 44.1 kHz resampled to 48 kHz came out 2.09 dB low with `aresample=48000` and 0.03 dB low with the tuned settings.
A 24.5 kHz tone at 96 kHz resampled to 48 kHz folded to 23.5 kHz at −14.4 dB with the defaults and at −138.3 dB with the tuned settings.
* The tuned resample of 120 s of 96 kHz 7.1 pink noise to 48 kHz took 2.68 s of wall time in one FFmpeg process, about 45 times real time, against 0.58 s with the defaults.

[[evidence-opus-bitrates]]
=== Opus bitrates

The measurements ran on 2026-11-02 with FFmpeg 8.1.3 and libopus 1.5.2 from the pinned worker image.
The sources were the first 734 s of Xiph's Creative Commons 5.1 masters of Tears of Steel (24 bits) and Sintel (16 bits).
Stereo and mono were BS.775 downmixes, and 6.1 and 7.1 took the other film's surround pair as their extra channels, so those channels are synthetic.
A noise-to-mask measure scored each channel of each encode against the master, as the share of frames with audible noise and the worst channel's noise-to-mask ratio.
The measure compares waveforms, which penalizes Opus's band folding and favours AC-3 and E-AC-3, and it has no temporal masking, so it under-scores pre-echo and gives no credit for libopus's surround masking.
ViSQOL could not be built on the host, so no standard measure checked these numbers.

* At 6.1 512 kb/s, Sintel's centre missed the yardstick by 4.3 points and 1.8 dB.
* libopus 1.5.2 coded 5.1 as two coupled pairs plus centre and low-frequency channel, 6.1 as three coupled pairs, one of them centre and back centre, plus the low-frequency channel, and 7.1 as three coupled pairs plus centre and low-frequency channel.
* On Tears of Steel, the worst channel's noise-to-mask ratio and disturbed frames were −3.0 dB and 80 % for AAC 5.1 at 384 kb/s, −8.8 dB and 34 % for E-AC-3 5.1 at 448 kb/s, −9.9 dB and 7 % for Opus 5.1 at 448 kb/s, −15.2 dB and 4 % for E-AC-3 5.1 at 640 kb/s and −15.4 dB and 2.5 % for AC-3 5.1 at 640 kb/s.
* A re-run on 2026-11-02 encoded all 734 s of Tears of Steel 5.1 at a nominal 448 kb/s.
Constrained VBR averaged 451.3 kb/s, and its largest fixed 6 s window ran 1.0 % over nominal.
Unconstrained VBR averaged 405.9 kb/s, and its largest fixed 6 s window ran 10.6 % over nominal.
Constrained stereo at 160 kb/s averaged 160.4 kb/s, with its largest window 0.6 % over.

[[evidence-lossless-size]]
=== The size of FLAC and ALAC encodes

The measurements ran on 2026-11-01 with FFmpeg 8.1.3 from the pinned worker image.
The sources were Creative Commons only: the Xiph.Org archive's lossless masters of Sintel (888 s, stereo and 5.1, 16 bits) and Tears of Steel (734 s, stereo, 24 bits), and Tears of Steel's official AC-3 5.1 track at 448 kb/s.
The pinned encoders made the lossy sources from the masters.
Two synthetic 300 s stereo signals bounded the range: pink noise band-limited to 100 to 4,000 Hz and gated in syllable-length bursts with pauses, as speech-like audio, and independent white noise in each channel.
Each lossy source was decoded and encoded to FLAC at 16 bits with FFmpeg's default settings, as the worker would encode it.
The rates count FLAC frames only, without fMP4 framing.
The peak is the largest fixed window of 6 s or 3 s.
Each percentage is of the PCM bitrate at 48 kHz and the written depth: 1,536 kb/s for stereo at 16 bits, 2,304 kb/s for stereo at 24 bits and 4,608 kb/s for 5.1 at 16 bits.

* Per channel, the decoded film tracks ran 197 to 409 kb/s on average, and the speech-like track 97 kb/s.
* FLAC of a decoded lossy track was about as large as FLAC of its lossless master: Sintel's stereo AAC track wrote 573 kb/s against the master's 644 kb/s.
* ALAC of the same decoded audio was 2 to 5 % larger than FLAC: Tears of Steel's AAC track wrote 854 kb/s, 56 % of the PCM bitrate.
* On 2026-11-02, in the pinned image, 6 s of synthetic 48 kHz stereo at 16 bits wrote 39 % more as ALAC than as FLAC with 2,400-sample frames for a 1 kHz tone (134,633 against 97,208 bytes), 34 % more for white noise and 39 % more for pink noise, so a FLAC measurement does not bound an ALAC encode.
