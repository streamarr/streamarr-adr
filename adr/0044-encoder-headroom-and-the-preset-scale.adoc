== Context

In this ADR, a variant is one HLS variant in the sense of RFC 8216's `EXT-X-STREAM-INF`.
A stream session's variants form its quality ladder.
The quality ladder holds the same media at several sizes and bitrates.
The player switches between the variants of the quality ladder.
A variant is never an HLS rendition.
An HLS rendition is an `EXT-X-MEDIA` audio or subtitle track.
Production speed is the number of media seconds that a job attempt produces per second of wall-clock time.
A variant produced at 2× real time delivers each media segment in half the segment's media duration.

Before this decision, the server started every variant of a session together whenever the worker had free job slots.
A worker with four slots and no hardware encoder therefore ran four software encodes at once.
The worker passed no `-preset` option to FFmpeg.
libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1 therefore each ran at their library's default preset.
Those defaults suit offline encoding.
Those defaults are slow for live transcoding.

On a 10-core arm64 host with libx264 at its default preset, running four variants at once made every variant about three times slower.
The 1080p variant was then produced at about real time.
That speed is too slow for a player to keep fetching the 1080p variant instead of switching down.
Free job slots say nothing about how fast each job will run.
The server needs a measure of the encoding load that a host can carry.
The server also needs a rule that decides which variants may run within that encoding load.

Streamarr can serve several users from one worker at once.
A stream session's extra variants are the variants that ADR 0019's on-demand rules would not run.
Extra variants use up encoder headroom.
Encoder headroom is the encoding load that a worker can still take on.
Another user's stream session may need that encoder headroom.
A seek also needs its first media segment quickly.
On a busy worker, producers that already run ahead of their viewers compete with that seek for CPU.

An encoder preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
The preset also decides how much encoding load each variant costs.
libx264 and libx265 name their presets with words from `ultrafast` to `veryslow`.
libsvtav1 numbers its presets instead.
A ServerAdmin is an account that holds the global superuser authority of link:0024-identity-authority-by-relationship.adoc[ADR 0024].
A ServerAdmin needs one name that means the same trade-off for every software encoder.

Some workers have a hardware encoder backend, such as NVENC on an NVIDIA GPU or VideoToolbox on macOS.
Such a worker can also run the software encoder for the same codec.
The server needs a rule that decides which encoder backend each new variant uses.

== Decision

[[decision-pixel-rate]]
=== Pixel rate is the unit of encoding load

Pixel rate is width × height × frames per second.
Streamarr expresses encoding load in pixel rate wherever it models, compares or reports that load.
The output pixel rate uses a variant's output size and output frame rate.
The source pixel rate uses the decoded size and frame rate of the source video stream.
Streamarr never expresses encoding load as a resolution or as a preset name.

*Why:* At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film encodes about 2.5 times faster relative to real time than a 60 fps source of the same size.
A resolution cannot tell those two sources apart.
A faster preset saved less at small variants.
`superfast` cost 0.28× `medium`'s CPU at 1080p but 0.53× at 360p.
The saving shrank because the cost of decoding the 1080p source does not shrink with the output.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-tonemap>>.

[[decision-headroom-model]]
=== A headroom model predicts each variant's production speed

The headroom model predicts the encoding load that each job attempt puts on a worker.
The model states that load as the job attempt's cost in CPU seconds per media second.
The cost of one job attempt is the sum of three terms.

* The encode term is an encoder-and-preset constant `a` × the output pixel rate.
The encoder and its preset select `a`.
* The decode term is a decoder constant `d` × the source pixel rate.
The source's codec and whether the job attempt decodes in hardware together select `d`.
* The tone-mapping term is the cost of tone mapping.
The tone-mapping term applies when the job attempt tone-maps.
It also applies when the job attempt reshapes a Dolby Vision source into PQ without a tone map (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013]).
Such a reshape is a tone-mapping chain of its own, with its own tone-mapping constant.

Every variant's job decodes the source in its own FFmpeg process.
Each job attempt's cost therefore includes its own decode term.
The model predicts the production speed of every running variant, not only the worker's total CPU.
The model derives each running variant's production speed from that variant's share of the worker's cores.
The model sizes a worker's encoder headroom for several users' encodes at once, not for a single session.
The tone-mapping term is a tone-mapping constant × the source pixel rate.
The tone-mapping chain selects the tone-mapping constant.

A worker's CPU limit is the share of the host's CPU that the worker and the worker's FFmpeg processes may use.
The CPU limit is the smaller of two counts: the CPUs that the worker's process may run on, and the CPUs that its CPU quota allows.
The CPUs that the process may run on come from its CPU affinity, which a container's CPU set restricts.
A worker without a CPU quota counts only those CPUs, and a worker with neither restriction has the whole host's CPU.
The worker reports its CPU limit when the worker registers.
Each job attempt that encodes video on a worker gets an equal part of the worker's CPU limit.
A job attempt never gets more cores than one job attempt uses when it runs alone on that worker.
A job attempt that needs fewer cores than its equal part leaves the rest to the other job attempts on that worker.
The model predicts a running variant's production speed as the variant's share of the cores divided by the variant's cost.

A seek attempt is a job attempt that starts after media time zero.
Under link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037], a seek attempt on a VideoToolbox or NVDEC worker decodes in hardware.
The decoder constant of such a seek attempt is the hardware decoder's constant, not the software decoder's constant.
While a worker decodes in software after its hardware encoder backend failed (<<decision-hardware-failure>>), every job attempt on that worker takes the software decoder's constant, seek attempts included.

*Why:* With constants fitted per source and preset, the model predicted the CPU of each measured libx264 variant within about 7 %.
One four-variant ladder at `medium` needed 6.1 CPU seconds per media second.
On paper, 10 cores therefore held 1.6 such ladders.
Yet when one four-variant ladder of the 24 fps film ran at `medium` on the 10-core arm64 host, its 1080p variant ran at only 0.93× real time.
The 360p variant of the same ladder ran at 3.6× or faster.
In that ladder, each FFmpeg process received an equal share of the cores.
That equal share was too small for the 1080p variant to keep up with real time.
The 360p variant needed less than its equal share.
Hardware decoding changes a job attempt's CPU cost.
On an RTX 5090 in the pinned worker image, one 1080p libx264 replacement attempt at `faster` used 16.47 CPU seconds with NVDEC decode, against 19.26 s with software decode.
That attempt covered 421 frames, and each figure is the median of 5 runs.
A decoder constant that ignores the hardware decoder would therefore overstate the cost of seek attempts on such a worker.
Equal parts of the cores predicted the measured production speeds of 180 running variants with a median error of 5 %.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-hardware-decode>>.

[[decision-speed-floor]]
=== Every running variant keeps the speed floor

A player switches down to a lower variant when the player's current variant arrives too slowly.
The speed floor is the production speed that every running variant must keep.
The speed floor is the larger of two speeds.
The first speed is 1.35× real time.
Below 1.35× real time, hls.js switches down.
The second speed is the floor of the neediest Apple player at the stream session's segment length.
An Apple player's floor is the lowest production speed at which the first media segment after a seek still arrives within that player's limit.
AVPlayer abandons a segment request for which the server has sent no bytes after one segment length.
Safari's native player switches down when a held segment request waits longer than the player allows.
The neediest Apple player is the Apple player with the higher floor.
At 3 s segments, the neediest Apple player is Safari's native player.
At 3 s segments, the speed floor is therefore about 2.10× real time.
The 2.10× floor also covers AVPlayer's lower floor of about 1.70×.

A stream session can have merged-slot segments under link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
In such a stream session, a slot is one 3 s interval of the zero-based media timeline.
A merged-slot segment spans several slots.
The segments of such a stream session therefore differ in length.
In a stream session with merged-slot segments, the server computes the speed floor at the session's slot length, 3 s.

Every rule in this ADR that protects a running variant's speed protects the speed floor.
No rule in this ADR protects 1.35× real time alone.

*Why:* hls.js 1.7.0 keeps a variant while 0.95 × the hls.js throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate.
The server advertises `BANDWIDTH` as 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`.
Those three facts give 1.33× to 1.37× across the ladder.
The Apple floors are derived, not measured.
On a 10-core arm64 host, two or three users each started a seek at the same time.
The runs used 3 s and 4 s segments, with 3 runs for each combination.
Each seek started a 1080p libx264 job attempt at `faster`.
A fit of the measured waits against each stream's measured production speed gives each Apple player's floor.
At 3 s segments, the fit gives 1.64× for AVPlayer and 2.01× for Safari's native player.
Over 20 seeks of a 1080p variant at 3 s segments, a real server and worker added about 0.1 s on top of the worker's own production time.
Other work shared the host, so 0.1 s is an upper bound.
That overhead raises the floors to about 1.70× and 2.10×.
The worker's measurement of production speed leaves out that overhead, and the floors already include the overhead.
1.35× alone protects hls.js but not the Apple players.
Evidence: <<evidence-floor>>, <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-calibration]]
=== The server measures each worker's constants continuously and keeps them

The worker measures each job attempt's production speed from the attempt's own media segments.
The worker reports each media segment's production time with the segment's upload, as link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc#probe-capacity[ADR 0032] decides.
The production time leaves out the worker's waits for earlier uploads, so the speed measures the encoder.
The server derives each job attempt's production speed from those reports.
The worker's own probe capacity brake uses the same measurement, so Streamarr measures production speed once.
The server fits each worker's constants to those speeds continuously while sessions play.
Each media segment's production time gives one measurement of the job attempt's cost.
That cost is the job attempt's share of the cores divided by the job attempt's production speed.
That holds for a job attempt whose stages all run on the worker's cores.
A job attempt on a hardware encoder backend has two costs instead: CPU seconds per media second for the stages that run on the cores, and encode-engine seconds per media second for its encode.
The worker reports such a job attempt's CPU time and the GPU's encode-engine busy time with each media segment's production time.
The fit takes that job attempt's CPU cost from its CPU time and its encode cost from the encode-engine busy time, never from its share of the cores divided by its speed.
The model predicts such a job attempt's production speed as the smaller of two speeds: its share of the cores divided by its CPU cost, and its share of the GPU's encode engines divided by its encode cost.
A job attempt that decodes in hardware likewise has a decode cost in decode-engine seconds per media second, which the fit takes from the GPU's decode-engine busy time that the worker reports.
Every job attempt that decodes in hardware on one GPU shares that GPU's decode engines, whatever encoder the job attempt uses, a software encoder included.
Such a job attempt's predicted speed is also at most its share of the decode engines divided by its decode cost.
The fit weighs recent measurements more than old measurements.
The fit starts from the benchmark's constants or from a kept calibration.
The pull of those starting constants fades as old measurements do.
The fit moves each constant only as far as the measurements support.
A constant that no recent measurement prices keeps the value that the fit last gave it.
A new constant stays unknown until the measurements give it a positive value, and an unknown constant never stops the fit of the known constants.
A measurement that prices a constant without a value moves no known constant until every constant that the measurement prices has a value.
Waiting measurements that share no constant without a value give their constants values apart from each other, so two constants that the measurements cannot separate never hold back a third.
A waiting measurement whose constant comes out not positive moves no other constant.

A calibration is a worker's fitted constants, together with the hardware, the CPU limit and the FFmpeg build that the constants describe.
The worker reports its hardware, its CPU limit and its FFmpeg build when the worker registers.
The hardware is the CPU model, the number of logical CPUs and the model of each GPU.
The server keeps the last calibration for each combination of hardware, CPU limit and FFmpeg build.
The server keeps a calibration without the worker's identifier.
A worker that registers with the hardware, the CPU limit and the FFmpeg build of a kept calibration takes jobs at once.
The server starts that worker from the kept calibration and keeps calibrating the worker continuously.
The server tells the worker whether to run the benchmark when the server accepts the worker's session.
A worker whose hardware, CPU limit or FFmpeg build matches no kept calibration runs the benchmark before the worker accepts any job.
A worker that registers for the first time therefore runs the benchmark.
A ServerAdmin who changes a worker's CPU limit, for example to keep part of a shared host free for other software, therefore makes that worker run the benchmark again.
The server takes that worker's first constants from the benchmark.

The benchmark runs for a bounded time.
The benchmark encodes with each verified encoder at each preset that Automatic can pick for that encoder.
The benchmark measures the decode cost of each source codec that the worker decodes, on each decoder backend that the worker has.
The benchmark measures how many cores one job attempt uses when the job attempt runs alone.
The benchmark also records the peak resident memory of its FFmpeg processes, which sizes the native-memory reservation of link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-memory[ADR 0037].
The benchmark adds concurrent encodes until a running encode falls below the speed floor or a GPU limit stops the benchmark (<<decision-gpu-limits>>).
The worker reports the benchmark's results through the worker's capabilities.
A ServerAdmin can see each worker's last benchmark, including the limit that stopped the benchmark.
A ServerAdmin can run a worker's benchmark again, for example after a driver update.
The benchmark's results replace that worker's calibration.

A measurement validates the constants before any early start of an extra variant or any preset choice depends on the constants.
The measurement has two parts.
The first part runs once, before https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/499[streamarr/streamarr-server#499] merges.
In the first part, several concurrent stream sessions run on real encodes, and every running variant stays above the speed floor.
The second part runs for each calibration.
In the second part, the server predicts each measured production speed before the server fits that measurement.
The server validates each encode configuration on its own.
An encode configuration is one encoder and preset, one source decoder on one decode backend, and one tone-mapping chain.
An encode configuration's predictions are the predictions of the job attempts that run that configuration, at any pixel rates.
The server keeps each encode configuration's latest 20 predictions.
A prediction leaves those predictions 7 days after its measurement.
An encode configuration's own predictions validate it when all of the following hold:

* The median error of 20 predictions is at most 10 %.
* At least 5 of those predictions come from job attempts that ran beside other job attempts that encode video.
* Two of those predictions have ratios of output pixel rate to source pixel rate that differ by at least a factor of 2.

An encode configuration's own predictions fail it when at least 5 predictions have a median error above 10 %.
A failed encode configuration is not validated.
Own predictions that neither validate nor fail an encode configuration are too little evidence.
Such an encode configuration counts as validated while the encode configuration that differs only by using the encoder's Automatic preset is validated by its own predictions.
Automatic may therefore pick another preset, such as `veryfast` for libx264, before that preset has any predictions of its own.
While no measurement prices that preset's constant, the constant moves with the constant of the encoder's Automatic preset, at the ratio between the two constants that the benchmark measured.
A failed preset becomes too little evidence again once its failed predictions are 7 days old, and the fit has by then learned from those failed measurements.
Each prediction also keeps its job attempt's pixel rates and measured cost.
The server checks the current constants against those measurements as well.
An encode configuration stays validated only while the current constants also predict its live predictions' measurements with a median error of at most 10 %.
A larger median error of the current constants fails the encode configuration, however few live predictions it has, so such a configuration cannot share the Automatic preset's validation either.
A change to a constant that encode configurations share, such as an encode constant that another source decoder's measurements move, therefore removes the validation of every encode configuration whose measurements the changed constants no longer predict.
A kept calibration keeps each encode configuration's predictions with their measurement times, and therefore each encode configuration's validation.
A kept calibration also keeps which constants a measurement has priced, so a preset measured before a restart keeps its own constant instead of following the Automatic preset's constant.
A benchmark's constants start without predictions, and therefore without validation.
An early start of an extra variant or a preset choice depends on the model only when the encode configuration of every running variant on the worker, and of the variant that the decision concerns, is validated.
Otherwise ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply on that worker, and Automatic keeps the fixed meaning that <<decision-preset-setting>> gives Automatic.
Every decision that depends on the model keeps each running variant's predicted speed, and the predicted speed of the variant that the decision concerns, above the speed floor by a margin.
Those decisions are an early start of an extra variant and a preset choice under Automatic.
An encode configuration's margin is the largest overprediction among its own live predictions, and among the current constants' predictions of those predictions' measurements.
An encode configuration that shares the validation of the configuration with its encoder's Automatic preset also takes that configuration's live predictions into its margin.
An encode configuration whose predictions never overpredicted has a margin of zero.
A decision's margin is the largest margin among the encode configurations of the running variants and of the variant that the decision concerns.
Every validated encode configuration has a margin, because either its own 20 live predictions or the 20 live predictions of the configuration that it shares validation with validate it.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/499[streamarr/streamarr-server#499] builds the headroom model and runs both parts of the measurement.

*Why:* The same preset cost different amounts on different content.
At every variant, a grain-heavy clip cost 1.3× to 1.9× as much per media second as a 24 fps film.
A single set of constants fitted across four sources missed individual ladders by up to about a quarter.
With one set of three variants running, 1080p measured 1.19× real time in the first run and 1.39× in a repeated run.
A benchmark at every registration would delay every restart of a worker whose hardware and FFmpeg build have not changed.
The CPU limit sets the cores that the model divides among the job attempts.
Constants measured under one CPU limit therefore predict wrong speeds under another CPU limit.
A Kubernetes Deployment gives each new pod a new worker identifier.
A calibration kept by worker identifier would therefore make every restarted pod run the benchmark.
A benchmark runs for a bounded time on the benchmark's own content.
The worker's own production speeds show whether the constants also fit the content that the worker serves.
A prediction made before the fit sees its measurement tests the constants on a speed that they did not fit.
Accurate predictions of libx264 say nothing about the constant of another encoder, so each encode configuration needs its own predictions.
Measurements of one ratio of output to source pixel rate cannot separate the encode term from the decode and tone-mapping terms.
A wrong encode constant and a wrong decode constant can then predict that ratio exactly and miss every other ratio.
Within one encode configuration, a second ratio at least twice the first separates the terms.
Across encode configurations, a second ratio does not: errors in two encoders' constants and two decoders' constants can cancel in four configurations that each run at one ratio.
Without the benchmark's ratio between presets, Automatic could never pick `veryfast` for libx264, because only a validated encode configuration allows that choice and only running `veryfast` would supply predictions.
A single running variant keeps one ratio, so a preset whose own accurate predictions cover one ratio keeps the Automatic preset's validation instead of losing it.
A preset that has failed gets no new predictions while Automatic avoids it, so its failed predictions age out instead of blocking it for good.
A starting constant that kept its full pull would drag a constant that has gone dormant back to the benchmark's value, while the constant's old predictions would still validate it.
Fitted per source and preset, the model predicted libx264's CPU within about 7 %, so a median error of 10 % leaves room for content that changes between segments.
Equal parts of the cores predicted up to 21 % faster than the measured speed, so the margin for overprediction keeps a running variant above the speed floor when the model errs in that direction.
A preset stays with its variant for the variant's lifetime, so a preset chosen without the margin cannot be repaired by later calibration.
A preset that shares the Automatic preset's validation may have no predictions of its own, so the Automatic preset's predictions bound its error.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-eager-start]]
=== Extra variants start only while every running variant keeps the speed floor

The requested variant is the variant whose media segment the player has just requested, as ADR 0019 defines it.
ADR 0019's on-demand rules decide which variants run when the headroom model allows no extra variant.
Under those rules, creating a stream session starts the variant that the multivariant playlist lists first.
Once the stream session exists, a variant starts only when the player requests one of that variant's segments.
ADR 0019's only-requested stop is the rule that a media-segment request for one video variant stops the producers of the stream session's other video variants.
An extra variant is a variant that runs only because the headroom model allows it.
ADR 0019's on-demand rules would not run an extra variant.

The server starts extra variants only while the headroom model predicts that every running variant on the worker, in every stream session, stays at or above the speed floor with the extra variants running.
One stream session's extra variants therefore never push another stream session's requested variant below the speed floor.
The server starts the extra variants nearest the requested variant first.
At each distance from the requested variant, the server starts the variant below the requested variant before the variant above.
The server continues in that order up to the whole ladder.

An extra variant that the headroom model started is exempt from ADR 0019's only-requested stop.
An extra variant runs while headroom allows.
The server stops an extra variant when headroom runs out.
When the player switches to another variant, the variant that the player leaves keeps running as an extra variant while headroom allows.
When the model predicts no headroom for an extra variant, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply.
Until a measurement validates the model's constants, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply unconditionally.
The headroom model never starts a lower variant beside a copy in Auto early, and never keeps one running as an extra variant (link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc#decision-lower-variants[ADR 0050]).
When the player switches back to the copy, the lower variant that the player leaves stops once the copy delivers a media segment.

*Why:* With libx264 at `medium` and 6 s segments, four variants running together pushed 1080p down to 0.94× to 1.06× real time.
That speed is below the speed floor.
An extra variant is worth starting only when every running variant keeps the speed floor with the extra variant running.
Without the exemption, the player's next media-segment request would stop every extra variant.
A quality switch would then find no segments of the new variant ready.
ADR 0019 gives the player measurements behind starting variants on demand.
Evidence: <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-capacity-shortfall]]
=== A capacity shortfall never refuses playback

A capacity shortfall exists when no preset that the preset setting allows keeps a variant at the speed floor.
A 60 fps source or many concurrent users can cause a capacity shortfall.
During a capacity shortfall, the server never refuses playback.
The requested variant always runs.
The server stops the extra variants on that worker.
A capacity metric shows the shortfall to the ServerAdmin.

No separate rule removes variants from the advertised quality ladder when capacity is short.
ADR 0019's on-demand start and the headroom model decide which variants run when capacity is short.

*Why:* Stopping extra variants frees encoder headroom for every requested variant and keeps every viewer playing.
The capacity metric tells the ServerAdmin that the worker needs more capacity.

[[decision-headroom-decides-encodes]]
=== Encoder headroom decides which encodes run, and no fixed count bounds them

No fixed count bounds the job attempts that encode video.
A worker advertises no playback slots.
The server has no transcode limit across workers.
A shortfall of encoder headroom never keeps a requested variant from starting.
Only a worker that cannot run the variant's job, or a worker's memory budget, keeps a requested variant from starting on that worker.
The headroom model decides which extra variants run.
The headroom model also decides which encoder backend each new variant uses (<<decision-hardware-first>>).
The headroom model also decides which encodes keep up with the player in link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#encode-range[ADR 0013]'s order of encodes.
An encode on a software encoder keeps up when the model predicts that a preset that the preset setting allows keeps the encode at the speed floor.
Otherwise the encode on the software encoder falls behind.
An encode on a hardware encoder that can take the encode keeps up.
At the same frame rate, every encode that keeps up comes before every encode that falls behind.
ADR 0013's groups decide among the encodes that keep up, and again among the encodes that fall behind.
A PQ or HLG encode on a software encoder that falls behind therefore gives way to every SDR encode at the same frame rate that keeps up, a hardware H.264 encode and a software SDR encode included.
When no SDR encode at that frame rate keeps up, such a PQ or HLG encode still comes before every SDR encode at that frame rate.
A lower variant on a software encoder is listed beside a copy only under the same prediction (ADR 0013).
The prediction chooses between encodes before a variant starts, so a shortfall of encoder headroom still never keeps a requested variant from starting.
Until a measurement validates the model's constants, the prediction chooses no encode, and ADR 0013's order applies without it.

The worker's memory budget guards the worker against runaway output (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-memory[ADR 0037]).
The memory budget does not decide how many encodes run.
A worker refuses a job attempt whose expected output the worker's memory budget cannot hold.
That refusal names worker memory with its own failure value.
The server treats that refusal as a full worker, not as a failed encoder.
ADR 0019 decides how long the server tries other workers after that refusal (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc#decision-requested-variant[ADR 0019]).

The capacity metric reports each worker's predicted load against the load that the worker can carry, in pixel rate.
The capacity metric also reports each capacity shortfall and each memory refusal.
The capacity metric replaces the playback slot metrics.

*Why:* A fixed count either has no effect or overrides the headroom model.
On the 10-core arm64 host, the most concurrent 1080p encodes that each stayed at 1.2× real time or faster ranged from two at `medium` to six at `veryfast`.
With the default of one playback slot, a player that switched variants sometimes requested the new variant before the server had stopped the old variant.
The server then answered that no connected worker could run the variant, although the host had spare CPU.
A memory refusal shows that the worker's output budget is full.
A memory refusal does not show that the worker's encoder failed or that the host's CPU is busy.
A software PQ or HLG encode below the speed floor drops the player to a lower variant or makes it stop to buffer, while an SDR encode that keeps up could play the full picture.
Buffering is worse than dropping down to SDR, whichever encoder makes the SDR encode.
Every encode, and not only the PQ or HLG encode, takes its place by speed, so that ADR 0013's order has no cycle.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-preset-setting]]
=== The preset is Automatic unless a ServerAdmin fixes one

A preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
The preset setting is one server-wide setting.
The web client shows the preset setting on its ServerAdmin settings page.
The preset setting's default is Automatic.
A ServerAdmin can replace Automatic with one fixed preset from the preset scale in <<decision-preset-scale>>.
One fixed preset covers every software encoder: libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1.
The same fixed preset covers every hardware encoder that has a column in the preset scale.
A variant's resolved preset is the encoder's own preset that the server derives from the preset setting for that variant.
Every job carries its variant's resolved preset to the worker.
The worker has no preset setting.
The worker passes the job's preset to the encoder.

Automatic means `faster` for libx264.
Automatic means `veryfast` for libx265.
Automatic means preset `9` for libsvtav1.
On the preset scale, libsvtav1 preset `9` is `fast`.
An NVENC encoder takes the NVENC preset of its codec family's preset name.
Automatic therefore means `p4` for `h264_nvenc` and `p3` for `hevc_nvenc`.
`av1_nvenc` takes no preset until it is keyframe-verified, and this decision picks none for it (<<decision-preset-scale>>).
Until a measurement validates the headroom model's constants, Automatic keeps exactly those presets.

Once a measurement validates the headroom model's constants, headroom may move an encoder's preset under load.
The server picks a variant's preset when that variant's first job attempt starts.
The server picks the slowest preset in the encoder's range that keeps every running variant at or above the speed floor, with the margin that <<decision-calibration>> gives.
The server keeps that preset for that variant, as <<decision-fixed-preset>> requires.
Each encoder has its own range under Automatic.

* libx264 runs at `faster`.
libx264 moves to `veryfast` only when `faster` would push a running variant below the speed floor.
The server never picks a libx264 preset slower than `faster`.
* libx265 stays at `veryfast` until libx265's seek waits are measured.
* libsvtav1 runs at preset `9`.
libsvtav1 moves to preset `10` only when preset `9` would push a running variant below the speed floor.
libsvtav1 moves to preset `11` only when preset `10` would too.
The server never picks a libsvtav1 preset slower than `9`.

*Why:* The server picks each headroom-driven preset.
The headroom model needs to know which preset each job runs.
The server therefore owns the preset setting.
The job therefore always carries the preset.
On a 10-core arm64 host, with libx264 over four sources and the four-variant ladder, `faster` used 0.59× `medium`'s CPU per media second at 1080p.
VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
At equal VMAF, `faster` needed about 10 % more bitrate than `medium`.
At equal VMAF, `veryfast` needed 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium`.
At 3 s segments, the first 1080p media segment after a seek took 1.28 s with `faster` and 2.09 s with `medium`.
Those two times are medians of 12 runs.
Automatic therefore starts libx264 at `faster` and moves libx264 only toward `veryfast` under load.
A slower preset lengthens the wait for the first media segment after a seek.
libx265's seek waits have not been measured, so libx265 runs no slower than its Automatic preset.
On a 14-core arm64 host with a synthetic 1080p30 source, libx265 at `veryfast` already spent about three times the CPU of libx264.
SVT-AV1 4.3.0, the version in the pinned FFmpeg, logs a warning at presets 10 and 11.
The warning says that, outside SVT-AV1's real-time mode, those presets are meant for automated tools, and that visual artifacts may occur.
SVT-AV1 logs no such warning at preset 9.
A measurement therefore compared presets 9, 10 and 11, on three public-domain clips, with the worker's arguments at 3 s segments.
Two checks were fixed before the measurement.
A 1080p encode at preset 9 had to run at 2.54× real time or faster.
That is the speed floor of about 2.10×, raised by the 21 % of the model's largest overprediction (<<evidence-model>>).
Preset 9's median seek wait had to stay within 0.3 s of preset 10's.
The runs that count here were pinned to 10 cores of a larger host.
There, the slowest clip's 1080p encode at preset 9 ran at 11.5× real time after its first segment, and at 8.8× counted from the start of the process.
Preset 9's median seek wait was 0.09 s to 0.26 s longer than preset 10's, clip by clip.
Preset 9 ran at 76 % to 94 % of preset 10's speed.
In one encode of each clip, preset 9 scored 0.35 dB to 1.02 dB higher in PSNR than preset 10, at a bitrate 1 % to 3 % lower.
Preset 9 therefore passes both checks, and Automatic starts libsvtav1 there.
A variant below the speed floor makes the player switch down or stop to buffer, which costs more than the artifacts that the warning names.
A libsvtav1 variant therefore still starts at preset 10 or 11 under load.
SVT-AV1's documentation uses preset 10 as its example for fast and real-time encoding, so preset 10 is the first choice under load.
Preset 8 and slower presets were not measured, so libsvtav1 runs no slower than preset 9.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-svt-av1>>, <<evidence-svt-av1-presets>>.

[[decision-preset-scale]]
=== One preset scale serves every software encoder

The preset scale maps each preset name to one preset of every software encoder.
libx264 and libx265 use the name itself.
libsvtav1 uses the number in its column.
The keyframe-verified NVENC encoders, `h264_nvenc` and `hevc_nvenc`, use the NVENC preset in their column.

[cols="2,2,2,1,1,4",options="header"]
|===
|Preset name |libx264 |libx265 |libsvtav1 |NVENC |Role under Automatic

|`superfast` |`superfast` |`superfast` |`12` |`p2` |Not used
|`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`11` |`p3` |Automatic for libx265 and `hevc_nvenc`, the choice under load for libx264 and `h264_nvenc`, and the second choice under load for libsvtav1
|`faster` |`faster` |`faster` |`10` |`p4` |Automatic for libx264 and `h264_nvenc`, and the first choice under load for libsvtav1
|`fast` |`fast` |`fast` |`9` |`p5` |Automatic for libsvtav1
|`medium` |`medium` |`medium` |`8` |`p6` |Not used
|`slow` |`slow` |`slow` |`7` |`p7` |Not used
|`slower` |`slower` |`slower` |`6` |`p7` |Not used
|`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`5` |`p7` |Not used
|===

"Not used" means that Automatic never picks the preset.
A ServerAdmin can still choose that preset as the fixed preset.
The scale has no name for a libsvtav1 preset below `5`.
A ServerAdmin therefore cannot fix libsvtav1 at a preset below `5`.
The scale has no `ultrafast` row, so a ServerAdmin cannot fix `ultrafast` either.
The scale therefore has no name for libsvtav1 preset `13`.
The scale has no name for NVENC's `p1`.
`slow`, `slower` and `veryslow` all take `p7`, NVENC's slowest preset.
A hardware encoder backend gets a column in the preset scale only once that backend is verified.
`av1_nvenc` is not keyframe-verified, so `av1_nvenc` takes no preset and runs at FFmpeg's default.

*Why:* libx264's `ultrafast` preset turns off CABAC, 8×8 transforms and B-frames.
libx264 at `ultrafast` therefore writes the Constrained Baseline profile, even when the job asks for the High profile.
The worker compares each initialization segment's codec string with the job's codec string, as https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/pull/64[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#64] adds.
On a mismatch, the worker fails the job attempt with `OUTPUT_MISMATCH`.
Every libx264 encode at `ultrafast` would therefore fail that comparison.
libx264 at `superfast` writes the High profile that the job asks for.
SVT-AV1's documentation reserves presets 1 to 3 for extremely high efficiency when encoding time does not matter.
The documentation reserves presets 4 to 6 for a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
The documentation describes presets 7 to 13 as the range for fast and real-time encoding.
The documentation names 12 as the fastest preset intended for human use.
SVT-AV1's default preset is 8.
The scale places 8 beside `medium`.
`medium` is the default preset of libx264 and libx265.

The NVENC column places `p4`, FFmpeg's default NVENC preset, beside `faster`.
Automatic's H.264 preset therefore keeps the encode that `h264_nvenc` wrote while the worker passed no preset.
The recordings' `p4` initialization segments were byte-identical to those of that earlier encode.
On an RTX 5090 at 1080p and 5 Mb/s, `p5` to `p7` scored at most 0.20 VMAF above `p4`.
`p5` and `p6` ran at 54 % to 76 % of `p4`'s speed with `h264_nvenc`.
`p6` and `p7` ran at 40 % to 55 % of `p4`'s speed with `hevc_nvenc`.
`p1` scored up to 1.4 VMAF below `p4` with `h264_nvenc`.
The scale therefore leaves `p1` out, as it leaves out `ultrafast`.
`hevc_nvenc` wrote byte-identical output at `p1`, `p2` and `p3`, so Automatic's `veryfast` gives `hevc_nvenc` the encode of NVENC's fastest presets.
That encode scored at most 0.08 VMAF below `p4`, and still scored above libx264 at `faster` at 1080p.
The NVENC recordings repeat the keyframe checks of link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] at every NVENC preset in the column.
Every forced keyframe was a closed IDR sync sample, and every replacement attempt wrote its first attempt's initialization segment.
Evidence: <<evidence-svt-av1>>, <<evidence-nvenc-presets>>.

[[decision-fixed-preset]]
=== A variant keeps its preset for its whole life

The preset stays fixed for a variant.
Every replacement attempt of a variant uses the preset of the variant's first job attempt.
Neither Automatic nor the headroom model changes a running variant's preset.
A preset change, whether by a ServerAdmin or by Automatic, reaches only variants that start afterwards.

*Why:* The preset changes the initialization segment.
Under ADR 0037, the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.
Within one preset, variant and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts.
The initialization segment stayed identical with 3 s, 4 s and 6 s segments and after every seek.
The initialization segment differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` for all 16 combinations of variant and source.
Evidence: <<evidence-init>>.

[[decision-seek-priority]]
=== Producers well ahead of their viewers pause while a seek's first segment is pending

A producer is the worker module that owns one job attempt's FFmpeg process.
A seek's first segment is the first media segment that a player needs after a seek.
The seek's first segment is pending from the relocation that starts the seek's job attempt until the server delivers that segment to the player.

A job attempt's lead is the media that the server has stored for its variant without a gap after the media segment at its viewer's position, measured in time.
A job attempt that started past that media has no lead.
A job attempt that starts before media that is already stored, after a relocation, a recovery replacement or an on-demand restart of a stopped variant, reproduces that media before it makes anything new.
That media counts toward its lead only as the job attempt's own uploads store it again.
The server attributes each stored media segment to the job attempt whose upload stored it, so an upload of another job attempt, even one that a disconnected worker finishes late, counts toward no other job attempt's lead.
Stored media that lies beyond the following gap never counts, because the job attempt would reach it only by reproducing it too.
The viewer's position comes from the viewer's latest timeline report.
While the viewer plays, the server advances that position with its clock from the time the report arrived.
A segment request does not renew the report.
The server uses the report only while the report is at most 30 s old and arrived after the stream session's latest relocation.
The viewer's position is the farther of that report's position and the highest media segment that the player has requested since that relocation, starting with the relocation's own segment or a newer request's, since an older request can relocate after a newer one.
A seek within stored media relocates nothing, so the player's requests can run ahead of its last report.
A job attempt whose own output has reached a later island of stored media is never paused for the rest of its run, because its next work is reproducing that media rather than the segment that its lead budgets.

While a seek's first segment is pending, the worker that runs the seek's job attempt pauses some of its other job attempts.
It pauses every job attempt on it, of any stream session, whose lead outlasts the hold limit, the longest segment from the job attempt's next one to the end of the media and a position margin of 3 s together.
On the plain 3 s grid that is a lead of more than 3 media segments.
A merged segment ahead keeps the job attempt running until its lead covers that segment too.
It never pauses a job attempt that a player's segment request waits on.
A request that starts to wait on a paused job attempt resumes it at once.
A request that moves the viewer past a paused job attempt's lead, such as one within stored media, also resumes it at once.
When the seek's job attempt is replaced, the pending seek moves with the replacement to the replacement's worker.
When the seek's variant stops, the seek is no longer pending.
The server names those job attempts in a command to that worker.
The server names them again at every new seek on that worker.
Once a pending segment is delivered, the server sends the list that the seeks still pending on that worker need.
That list is empty when no seek remains pending there.
Each command replaces the list of the previous one.

The worker pauses a job attempt by stopping every FFmpeg process of the job attempt with SIGSTOP.
The worker signals only through a Linux pidfd that names the FFmpeg process and that the pause keeps until it resumes.
A signal therefore never reaches a process that reused the pid of an FFmpeg process that has exited.
A job attempt whose FFmpeg no pidfd names keeps running, on another system or when the kernel opens no pidfd.
The worker resumes the job attempt with SIGCONT when a later command leaves the job attempt out.
The worker also resumes the job attempt 3 s after the command that paused it, even when no later command arrives.
A later command that names a job attempt that is still paused keeps that deadline, so a pause lasts at most 3 s however many seeks name the job attempt.
After a job attempt resumes, early or at its deadline, the worker keeps it running, even when a command names it, until it has run for as long as it was paused and has delivered a media segment whose delivery began after the resume.
A delivery in flight across the pause does not count, because the server may have stored its segment before the pause.
The worker measures time on its own monotonic clock, so a delayed command cannot shorten that run.
A job attempt whose FFmpeg no signal stops, because no pidfd names it or the kernel refuses the signal, counts as never paused.
The worker resumes a paused job attempt before it asks FFmpeg to quit, before it terminates FFmpeg and once FFmpeg's output ends.
The time FFmpeg spends paused counts neither as production time (<<decision-calibration>>) nor toward the worker's encoder stall timeout.

Streamarr holds back a producer that runs far ahead of its viewer with the same pause under its own command (<<decision-hold-back>>).

*Why:* Pausing delivered a seek's first segment sooner than lowering the producers' CPU priority, and as soon as a seek on an idle worker.
Three viewers ran real 1080p libx264 encodes at `faster` on one host, limited to 10 CPUs.
Two viewers played steadily while their producers ran ahead, and the third viewer seeked.
The first segment after the seek arrived after a median of 2.74 s with no change.
It arrived after 1.52 s when the steady producers ran at nice 19, and after 0.98 s when they were paused.
A seek with no other producer on the same 10 CPUs took 1.10 s.
No steady viewer's producer fell behind playback in any run.

Lowering the priority is also one-way for an unprivileged worker.
Raising a process's priority again needs `CAP_SYS_NICE` or a raised `RLIMIT_NICE`.
A default container grants neither.
A producer held back by a lower priority would therefore stay slow for the rest of its life.
SIGSTOP and SIGCONT need no privilege for the worker's own child processes.

A pause lasts at most the 3 s hold limit, which is how long AVPlayer waits for a segment at 3 s segments.
After the pause, a producer that runs at least at real time needs at most a segment's duration to produce it.
A lead longer than the hold limit and the longest segment ahead together therefore keeps the viewer playing through the worst pause.
Budgeting only the next segment would let a run of seeks wear the lead down until a long merged segment after it came too late.
The 3 s margin on top covers errors in the server's estimate of the viewer's position.
Because a later command keeps the first deadline, seeks in quick succession never chain pauses into one that outlasts the hold limit.
The run between pauses also keeps them from taking more than half of a producer's time, and a short pause needs only a short run.
The delivered segment between pauses means that at most one pause falls between two of a job attempt's media segments.
The worker enforces both bounds because only the worker knows when a pause began and ended; the server's view lags by the command's delivery time.
Each new pause starts from a lead that the server measures again.
The arithmetic assumes that the producer runs at least at real time.
A producer slower than real time falls behind a playing viewer with or without a pause.
A pause can then bring that rebuffer forward by several segments, not only by the hold limit, because every later segment arrives later by the paused time while the viewer's buffer shrinks.
Budgeting the next segments at the producer's measured speed (<<decision-calibration>>) instead of real time is left to the server's production speed model.
The server's stall clock (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019]) measures from the job attempt's latest stored segment and keeps running through a pause.
Because at most one pause falls between two media segments, pauses shorten that clock by at most the 3 s hold limit against the 10 s stall threshold, however many seeks follow one another.
A hold-back (<<decision-hold-back>>) never counts toward that clock.
The server finds a segment that a job attempt published when a segment request that waits on the variant finds it stored, and restarts the stall clock then.
A hold begins at a segment that the job attempt publishes while no request waits on it, so that segment is still unfound when the first request that waits on the job attempt releases the hold.
That request finds the segment and restarts the stall clock as the hold ends.
A count of segments alone would not hold for a stream copy's merged segments, which can span many slots (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-segment-list[ADR 0037]).
A report older than the latest seek or older than 30 s would misplace the viewer.
The highest requested segment lies at or ahead of the viewer's playhead, so it can only understate the lead.
Evidence: <<evidence-seek-priority>>.

[[decision-hold-back]]
=== Producers far ahead of their viewers are held back until a viewer nears their output

A job attempt's own lead is the media that its own uploads stored without a gap after the media segment at its viewer's position, measured in time.
The viewer's position is the one that seek priority uses (<<decision-seek-priority>>).
A job attempt has no own lead when a stored segment that is not its own output follows that media.
When a job attempt's own lead exceeds the high-water mark, 60 s by default, the server holds it back.
The hold lasts until the own lead falls to the low-water mark, 30 s by default.
Both marks are server settings.
The high-water mark must exceed the low-water mark, and the low-water mark must cover the target segment duration, a seek's pause and the position margin.
When the longest media segment from the job attempt's next one to the end of the media outlasts the target segment duration, as a long merged segment can, both marks rise by the difference for that job attempt.

The server lists each held job attempt in a hold-back command to the job attempt's worker, with a release time.
The release time is how long a viewer playing at real time takes to reach the low-water mark.
The worker pauses each listed job attempt with the same SIGSTOP as a seek's pause, and resumes it with SIGCONT at its release time even when no later command arrives.
A later command that lists a held job attempt can only move its release time earlier, and a command that leaves it out resumes it at once.
A job attempt whose hold ended at its release time stays unheld, whatever later commands list it, until a command leaves it out.
The server sends a release time earlier than the one it sent when the viewer moves ahead faster than real time.
Each hold-back command replaces the list of the previous one.
The worker keeps a job attempt paused while either a seek's command or a hold-back command lists it.
A released hold is followed by no run: the run after a pause belongs to seek priority alone.

The server releases a held job attempt at once when a player's segment request waits on it, when it has no own lead, when it stops and when its stream session ends.
The server decides the holds again at every stored media segment, segment request, waiting request, variant change and stream session end.
It sends a worker a new list only when the list differs from the last one it sent, by its job attempts or by a release time at least 1 s earlier.
A hold whose release time the server counts as past stays in the last list sent, so a waiting request still sends its release to a worker that received the hold late.
Once the worker resumes a job attempt at its release time, the job attempt is held again at its next stored media segment while its own lead still exceeds the high-water mark.
That new hold follows a command that leaves the job attempt out.
A hold is not a viewer's activity, so idle and paused stream sessions are still suspended after the session timeout.

*Why:* A seek's pause lasts at most 3 s and is followed by a run as long, so a producer held with it would still run half the time, and a 3× encoder would keep running ahead at 1.5×.
A hold-back needs a long release time for each job attempt, a new hold once that time passes, and no run after it.
The pause itself is the one that the seek-priority proof chose, so a hold stops no job attempt and starts none at an offset.

Releasing at 30 s leaves a viewer 2.5 times what a resume needs.
A resume needs 12 s: the next 6 s segment at real time, the 3 s position margin and a seek's 3 s pause.
ADR 0037 bounds no merged segment's duration, so a fixed low-water mark alone could release a producer too late to produce its next segment.
A hold delays every segment that the producer makes after it, so a long merged segment beyond the next one would reach the viewer too late as well.
Raising both marks by the longest such segment's excess keeps the same 18 s beyond what a resume needs for every segment ahead.
Holding above 60 s keeps the lead at the low end of the 60 s to 180 s that established media servers keep ahead, which frees worker capacity sooner.
At 3× real time, the speed measured for 1080p libx264 at `medium`, a held producer runs about 15 s and then rests about 30 s, so its worker receives a few commands a minute.
The release time travels in the command, so a lost command still resumes the job attempt before its viewer reaches the low-water mark.
A late first command delays the hold itself, while the producer still runs and its lead grows.
A late later command cannot extend a hold, because it can only move a release time earlier.
A command sent before a release time but received after it cannot start a hold again, because the worker waits for a command that leaves the job attempt out.
The server sends hold-back and seek commands without waiting for a segment that another upload is publishing, so a request that releases a hold never waits on another job attempt's file I/O.
The server cannot tell whether its worker still keeps a hold that the server counts as past, so it sends that command before every new hold of the job attempt.
The bounds in this decision and in seek priority assume that a worker session delivers each command promptly.
A command carries no time after which it no longer applies, so a command that a worker session delivers seconds late acts on a lead that the server measured before the delay.
A late new hold keeps a job attempt paused through the delay, and late seek commands can start pauses that the lead no longer covers.
A viewer can then wait for a segment, and a player's request that waits releases the job attempt at once.
Proving that the worker resumed before a new hold would need a report from the worker, because a segment that a paused job attempt uploads may have been produced before the pause.
A viewer who moves ahead faster than real time gets an earlier release time at once.

[[decision-copies-take-no-slot]]
=== A job attempt that copies the video takes no playback slot

A job attempt that copies the video takes no playback slot on its worker.
That holds even when the job attempt encodes audio renditions beside the copied video.
The server's transcode limit, `streaming.max-concurrent-transcodes`, leaves out every such job attempt as well (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc#decision-requested-variant[ADR 0019]).
Until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/499[streamarr/streamarr-server#499] retires the playback slots and the transcode limit (<<decision-headroom-decides-encodes>>), a job attempt that encodes video takes a playback slot and counts against the transcode limit, as before.
Until then, a worker runs only as many video encodes at once as its playback slots allow.
A worker bounds its copies by a copy cap, which is how many copies the worker's memory budget holds at two segment caps each (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-memory[ADR 0037]).
The worker refuses a copy beyond that cap before FFmpeg starts.
The copy's refusal names its reason with `JOB_ATTEMPT_FAILURE_COPY_CAP_REACHED` (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/769[streamarr/streamarr-server#769]).
That refusal is a memory refusal, because copies and encodes share the worker's memory budget.
The server handles a memory refusal as ADR 0019 decides (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc#decision-requested-variant[ADR 0019]).
A copy that fails to start for any other reason stops no left variant.
Streamarr has no production instances, so the change makes no provision for a worker that still counts a copy against its playback slots.
The server and the worker that leaves copies out of its playback slots ship together.

*Why:* A copy decodes and encodes no video, so it costs a small part of the CPU of a video encode.
Each encoded audio language adds about 1.2 CPU seconds per minute of media (link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc[ADR 0043]).
With the worker's default of one playback slot, one viewer's copy refused a second viewer in the same Household, although the worker had CPU to spare.
Two video encodes at once still need the headroom model to show that both keep the speed floor.

[[decision-hardware-first]]
=== A hardware encoder backend takes new variants first

An encoder backend is the encoder that a worker actually runs for a codec family, together with whether that encoder is hardware or software.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398] reports which encoder backends each worker has verified.
On a worker that has verified both a hardware encoder backend and the software encoder for the same codec, each new variant encodes with the hardware encoder backend first.
The headroom model predicts each hardware encoder backend's headroom as well as the worker's CPU.
When the headroom model predicts that the hardware encoder backend has no headroom left, each further new variant encodes with the software encoder for the same codec.
libx264 takes those H.264 variants.
libx265 takes those HEVC variants.
libsvtav1 takes those AV1 variants.
A variant keeps its first job attempt's encoder backend for every replacement attempt, as link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] and https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398] require.

VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
Streamarr measures each hardware encoder backend's VMAF against libx264 at `faster`, at the ladder's bitrates.
A hardware encoder backend's bitrates rise only where that measurement shows the backend falling short of libx264.
Streamarr fixes no bitrate margin for a hardware encoder backend in advance.

*Why:* A hardware encoder backend has its own encoding limit.
The worker's CPU does not show that limit.
Automatic gives software H.264 encodes libx264 at `faster`.
The VMAF comparison therefore shows whether a hardware encoder backend matches that software encode at the ladder's bitrates.

[[decision-hardware-failure]]
=== A variant whose hardware encoder fails continues on the software encoder of the same codec

When a hardware encoder backend fails during a stream, the server continues that variant's playback with a new variant on the software encoder of the same codec family.
The new variant keeps the range and the bit depth of the failed encode (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#hardware-failure-range[ADR 0013]).
The new variant runs on any worker that has that software encoder and whose memory budget can hold the new variant's job attempt (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-memory[ADR 0037]).
For a PQ, HLG or 10-bit SDR encode, that software encoder must also list everything that the range and the bit depth need (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#encode-range[ADR 0013]).
A worker with room is such a worker.
The server prefers the worker whose hardware encoder failed.
When no such worker has room for an 8-bit SDR encode, the stream session fails.
When no such worker has room for a 10-bit SDR encode, the encode continues as 8-bit SDR on the software encoder when a capability entry of the client accepts the 8-bit encode, as ADR 0013 decides.
When no such worker has room for a PQ or HLG encode, or a 10-bit SDR encode cannot continue in 8 bits, the playback choice decides, as ADR 0013 decides.
In Auto, the format attempt ends as a failed encode of that range and bit depth, and the next format attempt can use another codec family, or the same codec family on a software encoder.
In a fixed quality, playback ends and the playback session reports the failure.
The server never continues a PQ or HLG encode in another range.
With one worker, the new variant therefore runs on that worker's software encoder, or one of those outcomes follows.
These rules apply to the variant that the player requests.
An encoded lower variant beside a copy in Auto is the exception: when the player requests that lower variant, its hardware encoder failure withdraws the lower variants, and the copy plays on (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#lower-variant-failure[ADR 0013]).
The withdrawal replaces the stream session with a new stream session that lists the copy alone, and that replacement counts no format attempt.
When the copy does not fit the upload budget, the new stream session plays an encode that leaves the failed lower variant's encode out, as ADR 0013 decides.
An extra variant or a left variant whose hardware encoder backend fails stops, as it stops when encoder headroom runs out, and the stream session keeps playing.
A lower variant beside a copy that the player has left is such a left variant, so its hardware encoder failure withdraws nothing.
The new variant has its own initialization segment, so ADR 0037's rule that a replacement attempt keeps its variant's backend still holds.
The worker stops offering the failed hardware encoder backend until the backend passes its check again.
The worker also stops decoding in hardware, so every job attempt on that worker decodes in software, seek attempts included (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-hardware-decode[ADR 0037]).
The worker decodes in hardware and offers the hardware encoder backend again only when both pass their checks again.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/56[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#56] settles how the player moves to the new variant, before NVENC encodes any stream.
After a hardware encoder backend fails or refuses the encode of the variant that the player requests, no later format attempt of the playback session encodes that codec family on a hardware encoder, on any worker (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#failed-hardware-encoder[ADR 0013]).
When no connected worker has a software encoder of that codec family, the server refuses the new variant's stream session for lack of capacity, as ADR 0013 decides.

*Why:* A hardware encoder can fail in the middle of a stream, for example after a driver reset.
The software encoder of the same codec writes a codec that the client already accepted, so the viewer keeps playing.
A software encoder that cannot write the failed encode's PQ or HLG range would change the picture with no format error and no counted format attempt, so it does not continue the variant.
A 10-bit SDR encode that continues in 8 bits stays SDR, so the TV keeps its mode.
Another worker with room can keep the viewer playing when the failed worker has none.
The worker whose hardware encoder failed comes first, so the fallback leaves other workers' room to the stream sessions that the server places there.
A replacement attempt on the software encoder would write a different initialization segment, which ADR 0037 refuses.
A worker that kept offering a failed backend would send new variants to an encoder that fails again.
A worker keeps offering a hardware encoder that refused at its driver's session limit, so the playback session keeps that codec family off every hardware encoder.
A driver reset that takes down the hardware encoder can take down the hardware decoder on the same device too.
The fallback prefers the same worker, so a variant on its software encoder that still decoded in hardware would fail again.

[[decision-gpu-limits]]
=== A GPU's session limit, memory and video engine limit its hardware encoder backend

The headroom model charges a job attempt on a hardware encoder backend in two places.
The encode term counts against the capacity of the GPU's video encode engines.
Every hardware encoder backend on one GPU, such as `h264_nvenc` and `av1_nvenc`, shares those engines.
Each hardware encoder backend keeps its own cost constants, and the model adds their demands before it picks a backend for a new variant or starts an extra variant.
The benchmark therefore also runs encodes of different codecs on one GPU at once, and the server validates that mixed load like any other.
Each of the decode and tone-mapping terms counts where its own stage runs.
The decode term counts against the worker's cores unless the job attempt decodes in hardware.
The tone-mapping term counts against the worker's cores unless the job attempt tone-maps or reshapes on the GPU.
A job attempt that decodes in hardware and tone-maps with CPU `tonemapx`, as link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc[ADR 0013] requires until GPU tone mapping passes its equivalence check, therefore still charges its tone-mapping term to the worker's cores.
A GPU has three limits that a model of cores and pixel rate does not predict.

* Some drivers refuse a new encode session past a fixed count while the GPU still has capacity.
The worker assumes no session limit.
The worker keeps no list of GPUs or drivers.
When the driver refuses the session of a job attempt of the variant that the player requests because the session limit is reached, the refusal counts as that hardware encoder's failure.
The server continues that variant's playback as <<decision-hardware-failure>> decides, with a new variant on the software encoder of the same codec, whether the refused job attempt was the variant's first or a replacement attempt.
An encoded lower variant beside a copy in Auto withdraws the lower variants instead, as that section decides.
The playback session then keeps that codec family off every hardware encoder in its later format attempts (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#failed-hardware-encoder[ADR 0013]).
When the driver refuses the session of another variant's job attempt, that variant stops, as an extra variant or a left variant whose hardware encoder fails stops.
The worker keeps offering the hardware encoder backend.
The worker learns no cap from the refusal.
* Running out of GPU memory stalls every stream on that GPU at once.
The worker counts GPU memory as a hard limit.
The worker refuses a new GPU job when the GPU's memory cannot hold that job.
* A full video engine counts as a full hardware encoder backend, even while the CPU and the GPU's memory have room.

A job attempt that decodes on the GPU keeps its decoded frames on the GPU.
A silent fallback to software decoding then fails the job attempt instead of costing CPU that the model does not charge.
The job's replacement attempt decodes in software.
The benchmark stops at the first of these limits that the worker reaches.
The ServerAdmin's view of the benchmark names that limit (<<decision-calibration>>).

*Why:* In 2026, testers of the hardware checks of established media servers hit each of these limits.
Consumer NVIDIA drivers refused a 13th encode session on an RTX 3060 or 3080 while the GPU still had capacity.
Workstation cards and patched drivers have no such limit.
An RTX 5090 with NVIDIA driver 615.78.08 refused the 13th concurrent NVENC session (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/pull/166[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#166]).
On an Intel Arc A380, running out of GPU memory dropped every stream to 0 fps together.
On Intel integrated graphics, the video engine reached 100 % before the CPU or the memory did.
A cap learned from one refusal would leave the hardware encoder backend idle after a driver update raises or removes the limit.

== Consequences

* Consider one software worker on a host like the measured one.
On that host, two concurrent `veryfast` ladders slowed the 1080p variants to about real time.
The model therefore leaves little room for extra variants once several users play at once.
Whenever the model predicts that an extra variant would push a running variant below the speed floor, variants start only under ADR 0019's on-demand rules.
* The speed floor limits how many encodes a worker carries.
On a 10-core arm64 host, concurrent 1080p encodes at `faster` each ran at 2.80× real time for two streams and 1.90× for three.
A 2.10× floor therefore admits two such encodes on that host, not three.
AVPlayer's 1.70× floor would have admitted about 24 % more load per worker.
That lower floor would leave Safari's native player below Safari's own floor.
* The server now owns the production speed of each attempt and the constants of each worker.
The server needs the production time that the worker reports with each media segment to fit each worker's constants.
A worker that returns with the same hardware, CPU limit and FFmpeg build starts from the server's kept calibration.
Only new hardware, a new CPU limit or a new FFmpeg build delays a worker's first job by a short benchmark.
No fixed count bounds how many jobs a worker accepts.
Headroom decides which extra variants run.
Headroom also decides which preset Automatic picks within an encoder's range.
Headroom also decides when new variants move from a hardware encoder backend to the software encoder for the same codec.
* Once the model decides, a server plays an HDR source in SDR when no hardware encoder that can take the encode writes the source's range, the model predicts that every software PQ or HLG encode falls below the speed floor, and an SDR encode keeps up.
A TV then leaves its HDR mode.
That SDR encode can come from a hardware encoder of any codec family or from a software encoder.
* Once the model decides, a 10-bit SDR encode in software that falls below the speed floor gives way to an 8-bit encode at the same frame rate that keeps up, and color bands can appear.
* A worker's memory budget, not a count, bounds how many job attempts the worker runs.
A ServerAdmin sizes a worker's encoding load through the worker's CPU limit, not through a number of jobs.
* After a GPU worker hits its driver's session limit, the refused variant that the player requests continues in software, or the lower variants beside a copy are withdrawn, and the GPU keeps encoding other playback sessions' new jobs.
* On a worker with a verified hardware encoder backend, one codec's variants can come from two encoder backends at once.
Each of those variants keeps one encoder backend for every replacement attempt.
* A hardware encoder backend that scores below libx264 at `faster` gets higher bitrates, so the backend's variants cost more bandwidth.
* During a capacity shortfall, a ServerAdmin sees a capacity metric instead of refused playback.
Viewers keep playing, but a quality switch then waits for an on-demand start under ADR 0019.
* Encoders, presets and sources become comparable in one unit, pixel rate.
Metrics can therefore show load against capacity directly.
* A faster preset costs compression efficiency at a given bitrate.
In libx264 at equal VMAF, Automatic's `faster` needs about 10 % more bitrate than `medium`.
Under load, `veryfast` needs 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium`.
Automatic accepts those costs to keep every running variant at or above the speed floor.
* A preset change, whether by a ServerAdmin or by Automatic, reaches only variants that start afterwards.
* Every job carries a preset, even while Automatic keeps its fixed presets.
A ServerAdmin changes the preset on the web ServerAdmin settings page, never in a worker's configuration.
* Under Automatic, a libsvtav1 variant starts at a preset without SVT-AV1's warning of visual artifacts.
Only a variant that starts under load takes preset `10` or `11`.
* A seek into a libsvtav1 encode waits longer than at preset `10`: by 0.09 s to 0.26 s in the runs that were pinned to 10 cores.
The difference grows with fewer cores, and no host with 10 cores or fewer was measured.
* A libsvtav1 encode at preset `9` ran at 76 % to 94 % of its speed at preset `10`, so a worker reaches the speed floor with fewer libsvtav1 variants.
* A source that libsvtav1 already encodes below the speed floor, such as the tone-mapped 2160p source at 60 fps of <<evidence-tonemap>>, falls further below it at a slower preset.
* Until a measurement validates the headroom model's constants, libsvtav1 stays at preset `9` and does not move under load (<<decision-preset-setting>>).

== Rejected Alternatives

* Resolution as the unit of load ignores frame rate and decode cost.
At the same cost per frame, a 1080p60 source costs about 2.5 times a 1080p24 source.
* Preset names as the unit of load mean different costs in different encoders.
At the encoders' own default preset, `medium`, libx265 spends about twice the CPU of libx264 on the same encode.
At `veryfast`, which Automatic uses for libx265, libx265 spends about three times the CPU of libx264.
libsvtav1 numbers its presets instead of naming them.
* A fixed number of variants, or free job slots, cannot track the host, the preset, the content or other sessions.
On the same host, the number of concurrent 1080p encodes that each stayed at 1.2× real time or faster ranged from two at `medium` to six at `veryfast`.
A grain-heavy clip fits proportionally fewer encodes.
* Raising the default number of playback slots, so that a copy and an encode fit, would also let two video encodes run on a host too small for both.
* Ending the stream session whenever a hardware encoder fails stops a viewer whom the software encoder could keep playing.
* Falling back only on the worker whose hardware encoder failed ends a stream session that another worker could keep playing.
* Planning the range and the bit depth again from the software encoders lets a PQ encode continue as 8-bit SDR with no format error, also in a fixed quality with HDR chosen (link:0013-stream-decision-architecture.adoc#hardware-failure-range[ADR 0013]).
* A replacement attempt of the same variant on the software encoder writes a different initialization segment, which ADR 0037 refuses.
* Counting a copy that encodes audio as an encode would refuse viewers for work that costs little CPU.
* Keeping hardware decoding on a worker whose hardware encoder failed would let a driver reset fail the software fallback on that worker too.
* Changing a variant's preset mid-stream changes the variant's initialization segment.
The server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.
A mid-stream preset change would therefore amount to a new variant.
* Keeping 1.35× real time as the whole speed floor protects hls.js but not the Apple players.
At 3 s segments, the derived floors are about 1.70× for AVPlayer and 2.10× for Safari's native player.
* Pacing producers protects no speed by itself.
The server still needs a speed floor to know how fast each running variant must be produced.
* A floor for each client kind would need the server to know which player fetches each variant.
One floor protects hls.js and both Apple players without that knowledge.
* AVPlayer's floor of about 1.70× would admit about 24 % more load per worker.
That lower floor would leave Safari's native player below Safari's own floor of about 2.10×.
* Letting ADR 0019's only-requested stop also stop the variants that the headroom model started would stop every extra variant at the player's next media-segment request.
A quality switch would then never find an extra variant's segments ready.
* Starting extra variants nearest first, with no preference between the variant below and the variant above, leaves the order of two equally near variants undefined.
* Starting every lower variant first makes the variant just above the requested variant wait behind variants further from the requested variant.
* Rewriting https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/402[streamarr/streamarr-server#402] for the headroom model would add a separate rule that removes variants from the advertised ladder when capacity is short.
On-demand start and the headroom model already decide which variants run.
* Refusing new stream sessions during a capacity shortfall takes playback away from new viewers.
Stopping extra variants keeps every viewer playing.
* Starting new stream sessions at a lower quality during a capacity shortfall lowers a new viewer's quality before the server stops any extra variant.
* Benchmarking every worker at every registration, before the worker takes jobs, delays every restart of a worker whose hardware and FFmpeg build have not changed.
* Mapping NVENC's seven presets one to one onto the scale's fastest seven names would put `p1` beside `superfast` and `p4` beside `fast`.
Automatic's `faster` would then give `h264_nvenc` `p3`, below FFmpeg's default, and `p1` scored up to 1.4 VMAF below `p4`.
* Resolving Automatic for each encoder backend, so that `hevc_nvenc` gets `p4` while libx265 keeps `veryfast`, would make the server's preset depend on the worker's encoder and not only on the codec family.
`hevc_nvenc` at `p3` scored at most 0.08 VMAF below `p4`, so that gain does not justify the change.
* Taking jobs at once with cautious default constants, and benchmarking when the worker is idle, runs a new worker on constants that no measurement supports.
A busy worker may also never be idle.
* A fixed `veryfast` default with a ServerAdmin override ignores the headroom that a worker actually has.
At equal VMAF, `veryfast` also needs 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium`.
* Keeping each encoder's default preset, `medium`, makes the first 1080p media segment after a seek take 2.09 s instead of 1.28 s at 3 s segments.
On the measured host, `medium` also kept only two concurrent 1080p encodes at 1.2× real time or faster.
* Starting libx264 at `faster` with a headroom range from `veryfast` to `medium` would let a worker with spare headroom pick `medium`.
`medium` lengthens the wait for the first media segment after a seek.
* Keeping `veryfast` as libx264's Automatic preset needs 34 % to 39 % more bitrate than `medium` at equal VMAF, against about 10 % for `faster`.
* Using `faster` for 1080p and 720p and `medium` for 480p and 360p adds a preset rule for each resolution.
After a seek at 360p, `medium` still cost about 1.4 times the CPU of `faster`, averaged over four sources.
* Using `fast` for libx264 costs 0.80× `medium`'s CPU at 1080p, against 0.59× for `faster`.
At 6 s segments, `fast` also took 2.58 s to the first 1080p media segment after a seek, against 1.95 s for `faster`.
* Mapping Automatic to libsvtav1 preset `11`, so that `veryfast` names Automatic for every encoder, puts every libsvtav1 encode on a preset at which SVT-AV1 warns of visual artifacts.
* Keeping preset `10` as libsvtav1's Automatic preset keeps every libsvtav1 encode on such a preset, although preset `9` passed the speed and seek-wait checks.
* Never moving libsvtav1 to presets `10` and `11` under load, because of the warning, would let a variant fall below the speed floor, and a player that switches down or stops to buffer costs more than the artifacts that the warning names.
* Running libsvtav1 in SVT-AV1's real-time mode at presets `10` and `11`, to which the warning does not apply: that mode was not measured, and preset `9` passes both checks without it.
* A libsvtav1 range that reaches down to preset `8` would let libsvtav1 run at a preset whose seek waits are not measured.
* A preset setting on the worker would hide each job's preset from the headroom model, which needs that preset.
* Keeping `ultrafast` on the preset scale would let a ServerAdmin pick a preset at which every libx264 encode fails the worker's codec string check.
Automatic never picked `ultrafast`, and no measurement in this ADR covers `ultrafast`.
* Running libx264 at `superfast` whenever the ServerAdmin picks `ultrafast` would show the ServerAdmin one preset and run another.
* Planning the Constrained Baseline profile for libx264 at `ultrafast` would add a special case for one preset.
That special case would ship the lowest picture quality that the scale allows.
* Applying seek priority only when a worker accepts a new job leaves running producers untouched.
Those running producers hold the CPU that a seek's first segment needs.
* Lowering the CPU priority of the producers ahead of their viewers, with nice 19, delivered the seek's first segment after 1.52 s, against 0.98 s with paused producers.
An unprivileged worker also cannot raise that priority again.
* Pausing every other producer on the worker would stall a viewer whose producer runs barely ahead of playback.
* Letting the worker decide which producers pause would need every viewer's position, which only the server knows.
* Separate pause and resume commands could leave a producer paused when a resume is lost.
One command that replaces the whole list, and a limit on every pause, cannot.
* Holding back a producer far ahead of its viewer with the seek's command would cap each hold at 3 s and follow it with a run as long.
A 3× encoder would still run ahead of its viewer at 1.5×.
* A hold-back without a release time in the command would leave a producer paused when the release command is lost, and its viewer would stall.
* A later command that sets a held job attempt's release time anew would extend the hold by the command's delay.
* The same ladder bitrates for every encoder, hardware or software, would give a lower picture quality wherever a hardware encoder backend scores below libx264 at `faster`.
* Sending only some jobs to a hardware encoder backend leaves the hardware encoder backend's headroom unused while software encodes take the worker's CPU.
* A ServerAdmin setting on each worker that picks hardware or software asks the ServerAdmin to make a choice that the headroom model can predict.
* Keeping the playback slots as an upper bound beside the headroom model either has no effect or refuses an encode that the model predicts every running variant can carry.
* Keeping the server's transcode limit has the same effect across workers.
* Keeping a calibration by worker identifier makes every restarted pod of a Kubernetes Deployment run the benchmark.
* Leaving the CPU limit out of a kept calibration's key lets a worker whose CPU limit changed start from constants that predict the worker's old share of the host.
* Reading the CPU limit from the CPU quota alone gives a worker pinned to two CPUs of a 16-CPU host the whole host, so the model would predict shares that the worker cannot have.
* Validating the constants once for every worker trusts each worker's benchmark on content that the benchmark did not encode.
* Validating a calibration as a whole lets accurate predictions of one encoder validate another encoder's constant that only the benchmark measured.
* Validating each constant from every prediction that uses it lets errors in the constants of different encode configurations cancel.
* Letting a preset's first 20 predictions replace the Automatic preset's validation disables a preset whose single running variant kept one ratio.
* Keeping a failed preset's predictions until they are replaced blocks that preset for good, because Automatic gives it no new predictions.
* Rejecting every new solution when one new constant comes out not positive stops the fit of every known constant.
* Validating from past prediction errors alone keeps a dormant encode configuration validated after measurements of another configuration move a constant that both share.
* Leaving FFmpeg's memory to the container runtime lets requested variants of a 2160p source exhaust the container once no job limit caps them.
* Assuming a session limit from a list of GPUs and drivers refuses sessions on workstation cards and patched drivers that have no limit.
* Learning a session cap from one refusal leaves the hardware encoder backend idle after a driver update raises or removes the limit.
* Turning a hardware encoder backend off after a session-limit refusal sends every later job to software, although the GPU still has capacity.
* Charging GPU memory in pixel rate cannot predict a stall that stops every stream on the GPU at once.
* Giving each hardware encoder backend its own capacity counts a GPU's shared video encode engines once for each codec.
* Fitting a hardware encode from its share of the cores divided by its speed charges a GPU-bound attempt's whole cost to its CPU stages and drives its encode constant to zero.
* Leaving hardware decoding out of the speed limits lets extra variants of high-resolution sources exceed a GPU's decode engines while the cores and encode engines still have room.
* Letting a GPU decode fall back to software silently costs CPU that the headroom model does not charge.

== Evidence

Each group below holds the measurements behind one part of the Decision.
Unless a group says otherwise, the measurements ran on a 10-core arm64 host, in the pinned worker image with FFmpeg 8.1.2 and libx264.
The ladder was 1080p at 5 Mb/s, 720p at 3 Mb/s, 480p at 1.5 Mb/s and 360p at 800 kb/s, with 6 s segments.
The sources were a 24 fps live-action film, a 30 fps animation, a grain-heavy clip at 25 fps and a dark clip at 24 fps.
Unless a measurement names another preset, libx264 ran at its default preset, `medium`.
These measurements do not describe 3 s segments or the presets that Automatic picks.
A measurement that ran at 3 s segments says so.

[[evidence-contention]]
=== Contention across variants

* 170 timed runs compared each variant alone with all four variants together, both after a seek and at first play.
* With all four together, every variant took 2.78 to 3.22 times as long to complete its first segment.
After a seek, the first 1080p segment took 9.0 s instead of 2.9 s.
* One variant alone already used 6.9 to 8.7 of the 10 cores.
* While all four ran, 1080p was produced at 0.94× to 1.06× real time, against 2.96× to 2.99× alone.
720p was produced at 1.64× to 1.87×, against 5.41× to 5.45× alone.
* An independent re-run reproduced the slowdown ratios at 2.95 to 3.22.
The re-run measured 1080p at 1.06× to 1.08× real time with all four variants running.
* 36 simulated sessions compared restarting all four variants with restarting only the requested variant.
The sessions ran in hls.js 1.7.0 in Chromium and Firefox, in WebKit's native player, and in AVPlayer on macOS 26.6.2.
ADR 0019 gives the playback results of those sessions.
* When all four variants restarted, hls.js made 12 to 16 emergency aborts per session, about 6 s apart.
hls.js also reversed its choice of variant 30 to 34 times per session.
* In a control run, every variant ran at its single-variant speed.
The control run produced no cycle of emergency aborts.
The abort cycle therefore comes from the production speed.

[[evidence-floor]]
=== The speed floor

* The 1.35× part of the speed floor comes from the source code of hls.js 1.7.0.
An independent reading of that source code confirmed the 1.35× figure.
* hls.js keeps a variant while 0.95 × the hls.js throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
hls.js switches up at 0.7 × that estimate.
* When a segment load receives no byte for half a segment duration or more, and hls.js predicts that the load will not finish before the player's buffer runs out, hls.js abandons that load.
hls.js then resets its throughput estimate to the `BANDWIDTH` of the lower variant that hls.js switches to.
* A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate.
With the hls.js rule for keeping a variant and with `BANDWIDTH` at 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`, that download speed gives 1.33× to 1.37× for this ladder.
* The runs measured 15 sets of running variants on the animation.
1080p fell below 1.35× in only two sets.
1080p ran at 1.06× with all four variants, and at 1.19× with 720p and 480p.
The set with 720p and 480p measured 1.39× in the independent re-run.
* The Apple floors are derived, not measured.
On the 10-core arm64 host, two users and then three users each started a seek at the same time.
Each seek started a 1080p libx264 job attempt at `faster`.
The runs used 3 s and 4 s segments, with 3 runs for each combination.
* AVPlayer abandons a segment request for which the server has sent no bytes after one segment length.
Safari's native player switches down when a held segment request waits longer than the player allows.
* For each Apple player, a fit of the measured waits against each stream's measured production speed gives one speed.
At that speed, the slowest stream's first media segment after a seek arrives just at that player's limit.
The derived floors are adjusted to 250 ms fragments.
At 3 s segments, the fit gives 1.64× for AVPlayer and 2.01× for Safari's native player.
* A real server and worker started a 1080p variant at a new position 20 times, at 3 s segments.
The player saw about 0.1 s on top of the worker's own production time.
Other work shared the host, so 0.1 s is an upper bound.
Adding 0.1 s raises the floors to about 1.70× for AVPlayer and 2.10× for Safari's native player.
At 4 s segments, the floors would be about 1.49× and 1.85×.
* 1.35× decides the speed floor only when the neediest Apple floor is lower than 1.35×.
No derived Apple floor at 3 s or 4 s segments is lower than 1.35×.
* On the same host, concurrent 1080p encodes at `faster` each ran at 2.80× real time for two streams, 1.90× for three and 1.42× for four.
Those speeds are measured, with 3 runs for each load.
A 2.10× floor therefore admits two concurrent 1080p seeks on that host.
AVPlayer's 1.70× floor would have admitted three.
Both counts are derived.
* From the ratio of the two floors, AVPlayer's lower floor would admit about 24 % more load per worker.
That figure is derived.
The 2.10× floor protects both Apple players.

[[evidence-seek-priority]]
=== Seek priority

* The proof ran on a 16-core, 32-thread x86-64 host in the pinned FFmpeg 8.1.3, in two configurations: limited to 10 cores and with all 32 threads.
* The source was a synthetic 7-minute 1080p24 H.264 file with temporal noise and a 5 s GOP.
* Each producer ran the worker's encode: libx264 at `faster`, 5 Mb/s, a forced keyframe every 3 s, fragmented MP4 on its output, one fragment for each 3 s segment, and no audio.
* Two steady producers started from the beginning and ran for 8 s, so they led their viewers by many segments.
A median steady producer led its viewer by 17 s with 10 cores and by 37 s with 32 threads when the seek started.
* The seek's producer then started at a random 3 s boundary between 150 s and 327 s.
The time to the seek's first segment ran from that start to the end of the segment's last byte on the producer's output.
* With nice 19, the steady producers stayed at nice 19 to the end of each run, because the measuring process could not raise their priority again.
With pausing, they paused at the seek's start and resumed once the seek's first segment was complete.
* Each configuration ran each case 15 times, in a shuffled order within each of 15 rounds.
* Other builds shared the host.
The 1-minute load average ranged from 26 to 69.
Before each run started, 2 to 31 of the 32 threads were busy with other work.

[[evidence-presets]]
=== Preset cost and capacity

* After a seek at 1080p, CPU per media second relative to `medium`, averaged over four sources, was 0.80 for `fast`, 0.59 for `faster`, 0.38 for `veryfast` and 0.28 for `superfast`.
At 360p the same presets cost 0.86, 0.72, 0.59 and 0.53.
* At 1080p, `medium` needed 2.8 to 4.3 CPU seconds per media second.
`veryfast` needed 1.0 to 1.6.
The grain-heavy clip was the costliest source in every cell, at 1.3 to 1.9 times the 24 fps film.
* Over 12 runs, the median time to the first 1080p media segment after a seek was 3.18 s for `medium`, 2.58 s for `fast`, 1.95 s for `faster`, 1.35 s for `veryfast` and 0.99 s for `superfast`.
* At 3 s segments, over 12 runs, the median time to the first 1080p media segment after a seek was 2.09 s for `medium` and 1.28 s for `faster`.
* The most concurrent 1080p encodes of the 24 fps film that all stayed at 1.2× or faster were 2 at `medium`, 3 at `fast`, 4 at `faster`, 6 at `veryfast` and 8 at `superfast`.
* No full four-variant ladder kept 1080p at real time at `medium`.
In one run of a single ladder at `medium`, the 1080p variant ran at 0.93× real time.
One ladder did at `fast`.
At `veryfast`, two ladders did with no margin, and one ladder did with margin.
* On the 24 fps film, one ladder used 6.11 CPU seconds per media second at `medium` and 2.61 at `veryfast`.
* On a 14-core arm64 host with a synthetic 1080p30 source, libx265 ran at 53 fps at its default preset and 82 fps at `veryfast`.
libx264 ran at 110 fps at its default preset and 251 fps at `veryfast`.
libsvtav1 ran at 98 fps at its default preset and 222 fps at `10`.
* 364 encodes of 30 s excerpts from the four sources measured each preset's quality against `medium`.
VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
At equal VMAF, each preset needed more bitrate than `medium`: about 8 % more for `fast`, 10 % for `faster`, 34 % to 39 % for `veryfast` and 65 % to 68 % for `superfast`.
At the ladder's bitrates, `veryfast` scored about 3 VMAF points below `medium`.
On the grain-heavy clip, `veryfast` scored up to 7 VMAF points below `medium`.
The pinned worker image has no libvmaf.
A separate FFmpeg 8.0.1 build with libvmaf 3.0.0 therefore scored the encodes.

[[evidence-model]]
=== Model fit

* With constants fitted per source and preset on the steady-state runs, the model predicted the CPU per media second of each libx264 variant within about 7 %.
* One set of constants fitted across all four sources missed individual ladders by up to about 25 %.
The continuous measurement of each worker's constants absorbs that error.
* Fewer ladders ran with 1080p at real time than the CPU budget from the fitted costs allows.
At `veryfast`, 10 cores held 3.8 ladders of the 24 fps film on paper.
Only two of those ladders ran with 1080p at real time.
* In every ladder run, each FFmpeg process received an equal share of the cores.
The 1080p variant was always the slowest variant.
In the single-ladder runs, the 360p variant ran at 3.6× to 4.2× real time.
* Equal parts of the cores predicted the production speeds of the capacity runs.
The fit used the 24 clean capacity runs of the 24 fps film at five libx264 presets, with 2 to 16 variants running at once.
One encode constant for each preset and one decoder constant predicted the 180 running variants' production speeds with a median error of 5 % and a largest error of 21 %.
That fit is derived from the measured speeds.
The largest errors came at `superfast` and `veryfast` with 10 to 16 variants running at once, where the model predicted up to 21 % faster than the measured speed.

[[evidence-tonemap]]
=== Tone-mapping cost

* These runs used 60 fps HDR sources, libx264 and libsvtav1, and an 8-bit tone-mapping chain with the BT.2390 curve.
The cost of the curve that Streamarr chooses is measured again when tone mapping is implemented.
* Tone mapping added no measurable CPU on PQ and HLG sources at 1080p.
Tone mapping added 0.4 to 0.9 CPU seconds per media second on 2160p PQ and HLG sources.
* The Dolby Vision profile 5 reshape added about 1.1 to 1.2 CPU seconds per media second at 1080p60.
The reshape added 1.9 to 2.0 CPU seconds per media second at 2160p60.
* With tone mapping, one 1080p variant of a 60 fps 2160p source ran at 0.90× to 1.01× real time with libx264 and at 0.81× to 0.94× with libsvtav1.
Each encoder ran at its default preset.
Without tone mapping, the same variant ran at 1.05× to 1.14× with libx264 and at 0.87× to 1.00× with libsvtav1.
At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film would encode about 2.5 times faster relative to real time.

[[evidence-hardware-decode]]
=== Hardware decoding

* These runs used an RTX 5090 in the pinned worker image.
* One 1080p libx264 replacement attempt at `faster` covered 421 frames.
Each figure is the median of 5 runs.
* The attempt used 16.47 CPU seconds with NVDEC decode and 19.26 s with software decode.
NVDEC decode therefore cut the attempt's CPU by 14 %.
* With NVDEC, decoding alone fell from 2.57 CPU seconds to 0.14 s.

[[evidence-init]]
=== Initialization segments and presets

* Within one preset, variant and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts.
The initialization segment stayed identical with 3 s, 4 s and 6 s segments and after every seek.
* Across presets, the initialization segment differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` for all 16 combinations of variant and source.
* In the pinned libx264, `veryfast` and `superfast` produced identical initialization segments.
That match is a coincidence of libx264's current preset tables.
This decision does not rely on that match.
* In 592 of 592 attempts, every preset placed keyframes exactly at the forced keyframe times.
* Every preset passed the junction check at 20 of 20 junctions.
The junction check compares a replacement attempt's first segments with the first attempt's segments at the same positions.
The segments must open at the same times, hold the same frame times and show the same source frames.

[[evidence-svt-av1]]
=== SVT-AV1's preset guidance

* SVT-AV1 accepts presets −1 to 13.
SVT-AV1 reserves negative presets for debugging.
SVT-AV1's default preset is 8.
* SVT-AV1's documentation calls presets 1 to 3 extremely high efficiency.
The documentation calls presets 4 to 6 a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
The documentation calls presets 7 to 13 fast and real-time encoding.
* For fast or real-time encoding, the documentation says the preset must be high enough that the CPU encodes without stuttering.
The documentation gives preset 10 as its example.
* SVT-AV1 names 13 as its highest preset.
SVT-AV1 names 12 as the highest preset intended for human use.
