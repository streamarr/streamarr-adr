= ADR 0044: Encoder Headroom, Measured in Pixel Rate, Decides When Extra Variants Start, and One Preset Scale Serves Every Software Encoder

== Status

Proposed

link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018] left its capacity policy for later, until a working playback path existed.
This decision fills in that capacity policy.
This decision builds on link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019].
ADR 0019 starts and restarts the jobs that produce each variant.
ADR 0019 starts each variant on demand unless this ADR's policy allows more.
This decision relies on a rule in link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
Under that rule, the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.

== Context

In this ADR, a variant is one HLS variant in the sense of RFC 8216's `EXT-X-STREAM-INF`.
A stream session's variants form its quality ladder.
The quality ladder holds the same media at several sizes and bitrates.
The player switches between the variants of the quality ladder.
A variant is never an HLS rendition.
An HLS rendition is an `EXT-X-MEDIA` audio or subtitle track.
Production speed is the number of media seconds that a job attempt produces per second of wall-clock time.
A variant produced at 2× real time delivers a 6 s media segment every 3 s.

Before this decision, the server started every variant of a session together whenever the worker had free job slots.
A worker with four slots and no hardware encoder therefore ran four software encodes at once.
The worker passed no `-preset` option to FFmpeg.
libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1 therefore each ran at their library's default preset.
Those defaults suit offline encoding.
Those defaults are slow for live transcoding.

On a 10-core arm64 host with libx264 at its default preset, running four variants at once made every variant about three times slower.
The 1080p variant was then produced at about real time.
That speed is too slow for a player to keep fetching the 1080p variant instead of switching down.
Free job slots say nothing about how fast each job will run.
The server needs a measure of the encoding load that a host can carry.
The server also needs a rule that decides which variants may run within that encoding load.

Streamarr can serve several users from one worker at once.
A stream session's extra variants are the variants that ADR 0019's on-demand rules would not start yet.
Extra variants use up encoder headroom.
Encoder headroom is the encoding load that a worker can still take on.
Another user's stream session may need that encoder headroom.

An encoder preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
The preset also decides how much encoding load each variant costs.
libx264 and libx265 name their presets with words from `ultrafast` to `veryslow`.
libsvtav1 numbers its presets instead.
A ServerAdmin is an account that holds the global superuser authority of link:0024-identity-authority-by-relationship.adoc[ADR 0024].
A ServerAdmin needs one name that means the same trade-off for every software encoder.

== Decision

[[decision-pixel-rate]]
=== Pixel rate is the unit of encoding load

Pixel rate is width × height × frames per second.
Streamarr expresses encoding load in pixel rate wherever it models, compares or reports that load.
The output pixel rate uses a variant's output size and output frame rate.
The source pixel rate uses the decoded size and frame rate of the source video stream.
Streamarr never expresses encoding load as a resolution or as a preset name.

*Why:* At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film encodes about 2.5 times faster relative to real time than a 60 fps source of the same size.
A resolution cannot tell those two sources apart.
A faster preset saved less at small variants.
`superfast` cost 0.28× `medium`'s CPU at 1080p but 0.53× at 360p.
The saving shrank because the cost of decoding the 1080p source does not shrink with the output.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-tonemap>>.

[[decision-headroom-model]]
=== A headroom model predicts each variant's production speed

The headroom model predicts the encoding load that each job attempt puts on a worker.
The model states that load as the job attempt's cost in CPU seconds per media second.
The cost of one job attempt is the sum of three terms.

* The encode term is an encoder-and-preset constant `a` × the output pixel rate.
The encoder and its preset select `a`.
* The decode term is a decoder constant `d` × the source pixel rate.
The source's codec selects `d`.
* The tone-mapping term is the cost of tone mapping.
The tone-mapping term applies only when the job attempt tone-maps.

Every variant's job decodes the source in its own FFmpeg process.
Each job attempt's cost therefore includes its own decode term.
The model predicts the production speed of every running variant, not only the worker's total CPU.
The model derives each running variant's production speed from that variant's share of the worker's cores.
The model sizes a worker's encoder headroom for several users' encodes at once, not for a single session.

*Why:* With constants fitted per source and preset, the model predicted the CPU of each measured libx264 variant within about 7 %.
One four-variant ladder at `medium` needed 6.1 CPU seconds per media second.
On paper, 10 cores therefore held 1.6 such ladders.
Yet in a single ladder, the 1080p variant ran at only 0.93× real time.
The 360p variant of the same ladder ran at 3.6× or faster.
In that ladder, each FFmpeg process received an equal share of the cores.
That equal share was too small for the 1080p variant to keep up with real time.
The 360p variant needed less than its equal share.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-threshold]]
=== A variant holds in hls.js at about 1.35× real time

A variant holds in a player when the player keeps fetching the variant instead of switching down.
A variant holds in hls.js when the worker produces the variant at about 1.35× real time or faster.
This ADR calls that speed the hold threshold.
The eager-start rule in <<decision-eager-start>> protects the hold threshold for every running variant.
The Automatic preset in <<decision-preset-setting>> protects the hold threshold as well.

*Why:* hls.js 1.7.0 keeps a variant while 0.95 × the hls.js throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate.
The server advertises `BANDWIDTH` as 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`.
The hls.js rule for keeping a variant, the download speed and the advertised `BANDWIDTH` together give a threshold of 1.33× to 1.37× across the measured ladder.
With four variants running, the 1080p variant was produced at 0.94× to 1.06× real time.
hls.js then made 12 to 16 emergency aborts per session.
In an emergency abort, hls.js abandons a segment load that hls.js predicts will not finish before the player's buffer runs out.
hls.js makes that prediction once the load has received no byte for half a segment duration or more.
hls.js showed mostly 360p on a 25 Mb/s link.
Evidence: <<evidence-threshold>>, <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-calibration]]
=== The server measures each worker's constants continuously

The server derives each job attempt's production speed from the arrival times of the attempt's media segments.
The server fits each worker's constants to those speeds continuously while sessions play.
A new worker starts with a short benchmark.
The server takes the new worker's first constants from that benchmark.
The encoder-headroom issue is the server issue that builds the headroom model.
The encoder-headroom issue is still to be filed under epic https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/399[streamarr/streamarr-server#399].
The encoder-headroom issue validates the constants by measurement before the eager-start rule or Automatic depends on them.

*Why:* The same preset cost different amounts on different content.
At every variant, a grain-heavy clip cost 1.3× to 1.9× as much per media second as a 24 fps film.
A single set of constants fitted across four sources missed individual ladders by up to about a quarter.
With one set of three variants running, 1080p measured 1.19× real time in the first run and 1.39× in a repeated run.
Evidence: <<evidence-model>>, <<evidence-presets>>.

[[decision-eager-start]]
=== Extra variants start only while every running variant stays above the hold threshold

The requested variant is the variant whose media segment the player has just requested.
An extra variant is any variant of a stream session that ADR 0019's on-demand rules would not start yet.
The server starts extra variants eagerly only when the headroom model predicts that every running variant stays at about 1.35× real time or faster with the extra variants running.
That prediction must leave room for several users' concurrent encodes.
One stream session's extra variants never slow another stream session's requested variant below 1.35×.
When those conditions do not hold, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply.
Under those rules, creating a stream session starts the variant that the multivariant playlist lists first.
Once the stream session exists, a variant starts only when the player requests one of that variant's segments.
Until the encoder-headroom issue validates the model's constants, ADR 0019's on-demand rules apply unconditionally.

*Why:* Four variants running together pushed 1080p down to 0.94× to 1.06× real time.
That speed is below the hold threshold.
An extra variant is worth starting only when every running variant stays above the hold threshold with the extra variant running.
ADR 0019 gives the player measurements behind starting variants on demand.
Evidence: <<evidence-contention>>.

[[decision-preset-setting]]
=== The preset is Automatic unless a ServerAdmin fixes one

A preset is the encoder setting that trades encoding time for compression efficiency.
The preset setting is a server setting.
The web client shows the preset setting on its ServerAdmin settings page.
The preset setting's default is Automatic.
A ServerAdmin can replace Automatic with one fixed preset from the preset scale in <<decision-preset-scale>>.
One fixed preset covers libx264, libx265 and libsvtav1.
Every job carries its variant's preset to the worker.
The worker has no preset setting.
The worker passes the job's preset to the encoder.

Today Automatic means `veryfast` for libx264 and libx265.
Today Automatic means preset `10` for libsvtav1.
On the preset scale, libsvtav1 preset `10` is `faster`.

Once the encoder-headroom issue validates the headroom model's constants, Automatic becomes headroom-driven.
The server then picks each variant's preset when the stream session starts.
The server picks the slowest preset from `veryfast` to `medium` that keeps every running variant at about 1.35× real time or faster.
For libsvtav1, that range runs from `faster` to `medium`.
Those two names map to libsvtav1 presets `10` and `8`.
The preset then stays fixed for each variant, as <<decision-fixed-preset>> requires.

*Why:* The server picks each headroom-driven preset.
The headroom model needs to know which preset each job runs.
The server therefore owns the preset setting.
The job therefore always carries the preset.
After a seek at 1080p, `veryfast` used 0.38× `medium`'s CPU per media second.
With 6 s segments, the first 1080p media segment after a seek completed in 1.35 s with `veryfast` and in 3.18 s with `medium`.
With `veryfast`, six concurrent 1080p encodes of a 24 fps film each stayed at 1.2× real time or faster.
With `medium`, only two concurrent encodes stayed that fast.
SVT-AV1's documentation uses preset 10 as its example for fast and real-time encoding.
Evidence: <<evidence-presets>>, <<evidence-svt-av1>>.

[[decision-preset-scale]]
=== One preset scale serves every software encoder

The preset scale maps each preset name to one preset of every software encoder.
libx264 and libx265 use the name itself.
libsvtav1 uses the number in its column.

[cols="2,2,2,1,4",options="header"]
|===
|Preset name |libx264 |libx265 |libsvtav1 |Role under Automatic

|`ultrafast` |`ultrafast` |`ultrafast` |`13` |Not used
|`superfast` |`superfast` |`superfast` |`12` |Not used
|`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`veryfast` |`11` |Automatic today for libx264 and libx265, and the fastest headroom-driven choice for them
|`faster` |`faster` |`faster` |`10` |Automatic today for libsvtav1, and its fastest headroom-driven choice
|`fast` |`fast` |`fast` |`9` |Inside the headroom-driven range for all three encoders
|`medium` |`medium` |`medium` |`8` |The slowest headroom-driven choice for all three encoders
|`slow` |`slow` |`slow` |`7` |Not used
|`slower` |`slower` |`slower` |`6` |Not used
|`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`veryslow` |`5` |Not used
|===

"Not used" means that Automatic never picks the preset.
A ServerAdmin can still choose that preset as the fixed preset.
The scale has no name for a libsvtav1 preset below `5`.
A ServerAdmin therefore cannot fix libsvtav1 at a preset below `5`.
Hardware encoders get rows only when https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398] verifies them.

*Why:* SVT-AV1's documentation reserves presets 1 to 3 for extremely high efficiency when encoding time does not matter.
The documentation reserves presets 4 to 6 for a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
The documentation describes presets 7 to 13 as the range for fast and real-time encoding.
The documentation names 12 as the fastest preset intended for human use.
SVT-AV1's default preset is 8.
The scale places 8 beside `medium`.
`medium` is the default preset of libx264 and libx265.
Evidence: <<evidence-svt-av1>>.

[[decision-fixed-preset]]
=== A variant keeps its preset for its whole life

The preset stays fixed for a variant.
Every replacement attempt of a variant uses the preset of the variant's first job attempt.
Neither Automatic nor the headroom model changes a running variant's preset.

*Why:* The preset changes the initialization segment.
Under ADR 0037, the server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.
Within one preset, variant and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts.
The initialization segment stayed identical with 3 s, 4 s and 6 s segments and after every seek.
The initialization segment differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` for all 16 combinations of variant and source.
Evidence: <<evidence-init>>.

=== Implementation

Worker issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/23[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#23] makes the worker pass the job's preset to every software encoder.
The server adds the preset setting, with Automatic as its default.
The server puts the preset on every job.
The web client shows the preset setting on its ServerAdmin settings page.
The encoder-headroom issue is still to be filed under epic https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/399[streamarr/streamarr-server#399].
The encoder-headroom issue adds pixel rate, the headroom model, the continuous measurement of each worker's constants and the eager-start rule.
The encoder-headroom issue validates the constants by measurement before anything depends on them.
The headroom-driven Automatic preset is the last step of the encoder-headroom issue.

[[explicitly-deferred]]
=== Explicitly deferred

* Rows for hardware encoders in the preset scale, and their constants and session limits in the model (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/398[streamarr/streamarr-server#398]).
* The validated model and its constants.
This decision fixes the model's form, not the values of `a`, `d` or the tone-mapping cost.
The measurements cover libx264 only.
libx265 and libsvtav1 presets have not been measured on a ladder.
* The rate-control choices, including any CRF values.
* Which variants a ladder includes when capacity is short (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/402[streamarr/streamarr-server#402]).

== Consequences

* Consider one software worker on a host like the measured one.
On that host, two concurrent `veryfast` ladders slowed the 1080p variants to about real time.
The model therefore leaves little room for extra variants once several users play at once.
Whenever the model predicts that an extra variant would push a running variant below the hold threshold, variants start only under ADR 0019's on-demand rules.
* The server now owns the production speed of each attempt and the constants of each worker.
The server needs segment arrival times to measure each worker's constants.
Until the server has measured a new worker's constants, the server uses the constants from the new worker's short benchmark.
Free job slots still bound how many jobs a worker accepts.
Headroom decides only whether extra variants start.
* Encoders, presets and sources become comparable in one unit, pixel rate.
Metrics can therefore show load against capacity directly.
* A faster preset costs compression efficiency at a given bitrate.
Automatic accepts that cost today to keep variants above the hold threshold.
* A preset change, whether by a ServerAdmin or by Automatic, reaches only variants that start afterwards.
* Every job carries a preset, even while Automatic stays at today's presets.
A ServerAdmin changes the preset on the web ServerAdmin settings page, never in a worker's configuration.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Resolution as the unit of load ignores frame rate and decode cost.
At the same cost per frame, a 1080p60 source costs about 2.5 times a 1080p24 source.
* Preset names as the unit of load mean different costs in different encoders.
libx265 ran at about half libx264's speed at their default presets.
libsvtav1 numbers its presets instead of naming them.
* A fixed number of variants, or free job slots, cannot track the host, the preset, the content or other sessions.
On the same host, the number of concurrent 1080p encodes that each stayed at 1.2× real time or faster ranged from two at `medium` to six at `veryfast`.
A grain-heavy clip fits proportionally fewer encodes.
* Changing a variant's preset mid-stream changes the variant's initialization segment.
The server refuses a replacement attempt whose initialization segment differs from the variant's initialization segment.
A mid-stream preset change would therefore amount to a new variant.

== Evidence

Each group below holds the measurements behind one part of the Decision.
Unless a group says otherwise, the measurements ran on a 10-core arm64 host, in the pinned worker image with FFmpeg 8.1.2 and libx264.
The ladder was 1080p at 5 Mb/s, 720p at 3 Mb/s, 480p at 1.5 Mb/s and 360p at 800 kb/s, with 6 s segments.
The sources were a 24 fps live-action film, a 30 fps animation, a grain-heavy clip at 25 fps and a dark clip at 24 fps.

[[evidence-contention]]
=== Contention across variants

* 170 timed runs compared each variant alone with all four variants together, both after a seek and at first play.
* With all four together, every variant took 2.78 to 3.22 times as long to complete its first segment.
After a seek, the first 1080p segment took 9.0 s instead of 2.9 s.
* One variant alone already used 6.9 to 8.7 of the 10 cores.
* While all four ran, 1080p was produced at 0.94× to 1.06× real time, against 2.96× to 2.99× alone.
720p was produced at 1.64× to 1.87×, against 5.41× to 5.45× alone.
* An independent re-run reproduced the slowdown ratios at 2.95 to 3.22.
The re-run measured 1080p at 1.06× to 1.08× real time with all four variants running.
* 36 simulated sessions compared restarting all four variants with restarting only the requested variant.
The sessions ran in hls.js 1.7.0 in Chromium and Firefox, in WebKit's native player, and in AVPlayer on macOS 26.6.2.
ADR 0019 gives the playback results of those sessions.
* When all four variants restarted, hls.js made 12 to 16 emergency aborts per session, about 6 s apart.
hls.js also reversed its choice of fetched variant 30 to 34 times per session.
* In a control run, every variant ran at its single-variant speed.
The control run produced no cycle of emergency aborts.
The abort cycle therefore comes from the production speed.

[[evidence-threshold]]
=== The hold threshold

* The threshold comes from the source code of hls.js 1.7.0.
An independent reading of that source code confirmed the threshold.
* hls.js keeps a variant while 0.95 × the hls.js throughput estimate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH`.
hls.js switches up at 0.7 × that estimate.
* When a segment load receives no byte for half a segment duration or more, and hls.js predicts that the load will not finish before the player's buffer runs out, hls.js abandons that load.
hls.js then resets its throughput estimate to the `BANDWIDTH` of the lower variant that hls.js switches to.
* A variant produced at speed s downloads at about s × its bitrate.
With the hls.js rule for keeping a variant and with `BANDWIDTH` at 1.2 × `AVERAGE-BANDWIDTH`, that download speed gives a threshold of 1.33× to 1.37× for this ladder.
* The runs measured 15 sets of running variants on the animation.
1080p fell below the threshold in only two sets.
1080p ran at 1.06× with all four variants, and at 1.19× with 720p and 480p.
The set with 720p and 480p measured 1.39× in the independent re-run.

[[evidence-presets]]
=== Preset cost and capacity

* After a seek at 1080p, CPU per media second relative to `medium`, averaged over four sources, was 0.80 for `fast`, 0.59 for `faster`, 0.38 for `veryfast` and 0.28 for `superfast`.
At 360p the same presets cost 0.86, 0.72, 0.59 and 0.53.
* At 1080p, `medium` needed 2.8 to 4.3 CPU seconds per media second.
`veryfast` needed 1.0 to 1.6.
The grain-heavy clip was the costliest source in every cell, at 1.3 to 1.9 times the 24 fps film.
* Over 12 runs, the median time to the first 1080p media segment after a seek was 3.18 s for `medium`, 2.58 s for `fast`, 1.95 s for `faster`, 1.35 s for `veryfast` and 0.99 s for `superfast`.
* The most concurrent 1080p encodes of the 24 fps film that all stayed at 1.2× or faster were 2 at `medium`, 3 at `fast`, 4 at `faster`, 6 at `veryfast` and 8 at `superfast`.
* No full four-variant ladder kept 1080p at real time at `medium`.
One ladder did at `fast`.
At `veryfast`, two ladders did with no margin, and one ladder did with margin.
* On the 24 fps film, one ladder used 6.11 CPU seconds per media second at `medium` and 2.61 at `veryfast`.
* On a 14-core arm64 host with a synthetic 1080p30 source, libx265 ran at 53 fps at its default preset and 82 fps at `veryfast`.
libx264 ran at 110 fps at its default preset and 251 fps at `veryfast`.
libsvtav1 ran at 98 fps at its default preset and 222 fps at `10`.
* 364 encodes of 30 s excerpts from the four sources measured each preset's quality against `medium`.
VMAF is a perceptual video quality score from 0 to 100.
At equal VMAF, each preset needed more bitrate than `medium`: about 8 % more for `fast`, 10 % for `faster`, 34 % to 39 % for `veryfast` and 65 % to 68 % for `superfast`.
At the ladder's bitrates, `veryfast` scored about 3 VMAF points below `medium`.
On the grain-heavy clip, `veryfast` scored up to 7 VMAF points below `medium`.
The pinned worker image has no libvmaf.
A separate FFmpeg 8.0.1 build with libvmaf 3.0.0 therefore scored the encodes.

[[evidence-model]]
=== Model fit

* With constants fitted per source and preset on the steady-state runs, the model predicted the CPU per media second of each libx264 variant within about 7 %.
* One set of constants fitted across all four sources missed individual ladders by up to about 25 %.
The continuous measurement of each worker's constants absorbs that error.
* Fewer ladders ran with 1080p at real time than the CPU budget from the fitted costs allows.
At `veryfast`, 10 cores held 3.8 ladders of the 24 fps film on paper.
Only two of those ladders ran with 1080p at real time.
* In every ladder run, each FFmpeg process received an equal share of the cores.
The 1080p variant was always the slowest variant.
In the single-ladder runs, the 360p variant ran at 3.6× to 4.2× real time.

[[evidence-tonemap]]
=== Tone-mapping cost

* These runs used 60 fps HDR sources, libx264 and libsvtav1, and an 8-bit tone-mapping chain with the BT.2390 curve.
The cost of the curve that Streamarr chooses is measured again when tone mapping is implemented.
* Tone mapping added no measurable CPU on PQ and HLG sources at 1080p.
Tone mapping added 0.4 to 0.9 CPU seconds per media second on 2160p PQ and HLG sources.
* The Dolby Vision profile 5 reshape added about 1.1 to 1.2 CPU seconds per media second at 1080p60.
The reshape added 1.9 to 2.0 CPU seconds per media second at 2160p60.
* One 1080p variant of a 60 fps 2160p source ran at 0.81× to 1.01× real time.
That speed is at or below real time even without tone mapping.
At the same cost per frame, a 24 fps film would encode about 2.5 times faster relative to real time.

[[evidence-init]]
=== Initialization segments and presets

* Within one preset, variant and source, the initialization segment was byte-identical across 592 attempts.
The initialization segment stayed identical with 3 s, 4 s and 6 s segments and after every seek.
* Across presets, the initialization segment differed between `medium`, `fast`, `faster` and `veryfast` for all 16 combinations of variant and source.
* In the pinned libx264, `veryfast` and `superfast` produced identical initialization segments.
That match is a coincidence of libx264's current preset tables.
This decision does not rely on that match.
* In 592 of 592 attempts, every preset placed keyframes exactly at the forced keyframe times.
* Every preset passed the junction check at 20 of 20 junctions.
The junction check compares a replacement attempt's first segments with the first attempt's segments at the same positions.
The segments must open at the same times, hold the same frame times and show the same source frames.

[[evidence-svt-av1]]
=== SVT-AV1's preset guidance

* SVT-AV1 accepts presets −1 to 13.
SVT-AV1 reserves negative presets for debugging.
SVT-AV1's default preset is 8.
* SVT-AV1's documentation calls presets 1 to 3 extremely high efficiency.
The documentation calls presets 4 to 6 a balance of efficiency and reasonable compute time.
The documentation calls presets 7 to 13 fast and real-time encoding.
* For fast or real-time encoding, the documentation says the preset must be high enough that the CPU encodes without stuttering.
The documentation gives preset 10 as its example.
* SVT-AV1 names 13 as its highest preset.
SVT-AV1 names 12 as the highest preset intended for human use.
