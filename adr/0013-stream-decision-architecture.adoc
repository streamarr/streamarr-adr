== Context

Streaming negotiates source media (ffprobe) against client capabilities (StreamingOptions).
Each stream type -- video, audio, subtitle -- needs an independent decision.

These independent decisions compose into a TranscodeDecision that drives FFmpeg command construction and HLS playlist generation.

The rest of this section describes the system before this amendment.
The video decision is implicit.
A codec family and a compatibility flag stand in for the video decision.
The server copies the video whenever the client names the source's codec family.
Otherwise, the server encodes the video.
The Apple TV app names `h264` and `hevc`.
The server therefore copies every HEVC source to the Apple TV app, including HDR sources.
Every client counts as naming `h264`.
Every client therefore receives a copy of a 10-bit H.264 source.
Tone mapping converts HDR brightness and color into the smaller SDR range.
PQ (perceptual quantizer) and HLG (hybrid log-gamma) are the two HDR transfer characteristics.
An encode keeps the source's bit depth and transfer characteristic.
A 10-bit source becomes 10-bit H.264.
An encode of an HDR source keeps the source's PQ or HLG transfer characteristic.
The worker does not tone-map that encode.
An encode of a Dolby Vision profile 5 source comes out teal.
A codec string is the RFC 6381 value that names a stream's codec, codec profile and level, such as `avc1.640028`.
A variant's `CODECS` attribute lists the codec strings of the variant's streams.
The playlist's codec strings are constants.
These constants often disagree with the media.
The stored probe cannot tell 8-bit from 10-bit or SDR from HDR.
The stored probe also ignores a source's rotation and container cropping.

== Decision

=== Each stream type has its own decision record

Model each stream type as a decision record: AudioDecision (COPY/TRANSCODE/NONE), SubtitleDecision (EXCLUDE/BURN_IN/SIDECAR/HLS/EMBED), and VideoDecision.
VideoDecision replaces the implicit codec family and compatibility flag.
VideoDecision records whether the video is copied or encoded.
VideoDecision also holds the planned output that link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036] defines.

Stream decisions compose into TranscodeDecision -> TranscodeMode -> FfmpegCommandBuilder.
Pipeline: probe -> decide per stream -> compose -> resolve mode -> build command.
Burning in a subtitle turns the video into an encode.
A stream session that burns in an image subtitle therefore follows every encode rule in this ADR.
The transcode job carries the video decision to the worker.
The video decision holds the source's dynamic range and the planned output's dynamic range.
The video decision also holds each variant's codec profile, tier, level and codec string.
The worker follows the video decision and never works those values out again.
link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036] leaves the field names of the job contract to the implementation.

The video decision reads facts that probe version 4 and later store.
link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]'s rules for probe versions govern those facts.
Those facts include the dynamic range, bit depth, chroma subsampling, rotation, container cropping, output frame rate and codec string of the video stream.
From probe version 5 on, the video decision also reads the video stream's typed codec profile.

=== Every video encode produces 4:2:0, and 8-bit SDR unless the decision plans a 10-bit or HDR encode

Every encode's planned output is 4:2:0.
The planned output is 8-bit SDR, unless the decision plans 10 bits or a PQ or HLG range for that encode.
<<encode-range>> decides which encodes keep 10 bits or a PQ or HLG range.
An SDR planned output uses BT.709 color in limited range.
A PQ or HLG planned output uses BT.2020 primaries and the BT.2020 non-constant luminance matrix, as the worker contract states.
The dynamic range of an SDR planned output has an SDR compatible base.
A compatible base is the standard picture format that a player could use without the advanced HDR information.
HDR10+ and Dolby Vision are absent from the planned output.
The server chooses how the worker converts the source to the planned output.
The worker builds its filters from the two dynamic ranges in the transcode job.
The worker never chooses a conversion on its own.
The worker tone-maps when the planned output is SDR and the source's compatible base is PQ, HLG or none.
The worker converts a source whose compatible base is SDR or unspecified to the planned output's bit depth without tone mapping.
The worker never tone-maps a stream copy.
Tone mapping uses the mobius curve with a 1000-nit peak.
The worker never uses another tone curve.
The worker tone-maps with the `tonemapx` filter of the pinned FFmpeg build that ADR 0029 describes.
<<gpu-stages>> decides when a GPU tone mapper replaces `tonemapx`.
A reference processing unit (RPU) holds the per-frame Dolby Vision picture-adjustment instructions.
Before tone mapping, the worker reshapes a Dolby Vision profile 5 source with the source's RPUs.
When the planned output is PQ, the worker reshapes such a source into PQ and runs no tone map (<<gpu-stages>>).
When such a source's RPUs do not follow their pictures, the worker first moves the RPUs under the conditions of <<reshape-rpus-starting-samples>>.
An encode of a Dolby Vision profile 7 or 8.x source reads only the Dolby Vision base layer and ignores the source's RPUs.
`tonemapx` leaks memory on every reshaped Dolby Vision profile 5 frame.
Streamarr accepts that leak.
The leaked memory returns when the job attempt's FFmpeg process exits.
Each replacement attempt starts a fresh FFmpeg process.
The tone-mapping work (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/26[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#26]) watches worker memory while a full-length Dolby Vision profile 5 film encodes.

*Why:* Before this amendment, the worker encoded every 10-bit HEVC source to 10-bit H.264 (High 10) in 13 of 13 encodes.
Firefox refused High 10 in 21 of 21 runs.
WebKit's native player and AVPlayer refused PQ-tagged H.264 in 16 of 16 runs.
The tone-mapped output was 8-bit BT.709 in limited range.
The tone-mapped Dolby Vision profile 5 output lost its teal cast.
The tone-mapping filter treats an untagged input as PQ.
The filter therefore darkened an untagged SDR source to a mean luma of 41 instead of 106.
Evidence: <<evidence-sdr-output>>, <<evidence-tone-mapping>>.

[[reshape-rpus-starting-samples]]
=== Before an encode of an HEVC Dolby Vision source with no compatible base, the worker moves each RPU behind the picture that it belongs to

The RPU of a picture belongs after the slices of that picture, as the last NAL unit of the same sample.
FFmpeg's HEVC decoder reads an RPU only when the RPU is the last NAL unit of the packet that it decodes.
An RPU anywhere else reshapes no picture, so the encode of a profile 5 picture keeps the color cast of an unreshaped picture.

Probe version 5 reports where the samples place the RPUs, as the stream's RPU placement (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036]):

* `AFTER_OWN_PICTURE`: each RPU is the last NAL unit of its own picture's sample, as Dolby Vision requires.
* `STARTS_SAMPLE`: every sample that holds an RPU starts with it.
* `UNSPECIFIED`: the probe could not tell the placement, as when it cannot delimit the packets, a packet holds two RPUs, the packets mix layouts, or the stream is AV1.

The probe decides the placement from the first packets, and the worker assumes that the rest of the stream keeps that layout.
Two layouts start samples with RPUs, and both report `STARTS_SAMPLE`:

* The shifted layout: each picture's RPU starts the next picture's sample, and the last sample holds an RPU and no picture.
mkvmerge wrote it before version 57, released in 2021.
A shifted file whose start was cut away keeps this layout, and its first picture's sample starts with the RPU of a picture that the cut removed.
* The own-first layout: each picture's sample starts with that picture's own RPU.
No known muxer writes this layout, but FFmpeg's MP4 and Matroska remuxes keep it.

The two layouts share one value for two reasons.
The first packets of a cut shifted file and of an own-first file look the same.
Both layouts also take the same repair before an encode, and the server copies neither.

The transcode job's video decision carries the source's codec family.
The server reads that codec family from the codec name that the probe stored.
Every job carries it, whether it copies or encodes.
A source of any codec family other than H.264, HEVC and AV1 has an unspecified codec family in the job.

Probe version 5 also reports whether the first packets of the chosen video stream carry presentation times (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]).
The probe decides it from the packets that its NAL unit walk reads, and the worker assumes that the rest of the stream keeps it.
The video decision of every job carries `source_packets_carry_presentation_times`, which is true only when the probe stored that every one of those packets carries a presentation time.
It is false when the probe stored that a packet carries none, and when the probe stored no value, because the probe read no packets.

The worker chooses this path from the source's codec family and dynamic range in the transcode job.
The server's video decision does not change, and the worker builds the same filters from it.
The job attempt runs two FFmpeg processes only when all five conditions hold:

* The job encodes the video.
* The source's codec family is HEVC.
* The source's compatible base is none.
* The source's Dolby Vision is present, and its RPU placement is not `AFTER_OWN_PICTURE`.
* The job's `source_packets_carry_presentation_times` is true.

Every other job attempt runs one FFmpeg process with the same command as before, and FFmpeg reads the source's samples unchanged.

A source whose packets carry no presentation times, or whose presentation times the probe could not see, therefore runs one FFmpeg process, whatever its RPU placement.
The move copies the source's samples, with their presentation times, into the stream that the encode decodes.
A copy of packets without presentation times stamps each picture with its decoding time, so the encode loses the pictures that B-frames reorder.
FFmpeg's raw H.264 and HEVC demuxers give no packet a presentation time, and neither did its AVI demuxer in any file measured.
The worker adds no `-fflags +genpts` and no input `-r` to such a source.
One FFmpeg still reshapes the pictures of the usual such source, a raw HEVC stream, because FFmpeg's HEVC parser cuts that stream into packets:

* The parser (`hevc_find_frame_end`) ends a packet just ahead of a NAL unit on layer 0 that begins the next access unit.
Those NAL units are a VPS, SPS or PPS, an access unit delimiter, an end of sequence or of bitstream (types 32 to 37), a prefix SEI NAL unit (type 39), a NAL unit of a reserved type 41 to 44 or 48 to 55, and the first slice segment of the next picture.
* An RPU begins no access unit, so the parser starts no packet at an RPU, except the first packet, which starts wherever the parser starts reading.
* An RPU that follows its picture's slices, where Dolby Vision places it, therefore ends that picture's packet, and the decoder reads it there.

The decoder still ignores two kinds of RPU in such a source.
One is an RPU that another NAL unit of its packet follows, such as an RPU between the NAL units that begin an access unit and its first slice.
The other is an RPU that starts a sample of a demuxer that keeps the samples as stored, as FFmpeg's AVI demuxer does for an HEVC stream tagged `HEVC` rather than `H265`.
Those pictures keep the color cast of an unreshaped picture, but the encode keeps every picture.

Every RPU of a raw HEVC profile 5 stream whose RPUs lead their samples, ahead of the slices, is of the first kind.
The probe reports such a stream as `STARTS_SAMPLE` without presentation times.
Streamarr keeps such a stream on one FFmpeg process.
One FFmpeg keeps every picture, but shows each picture without its reshaping.
On a measured stream of 100 pictures with B-frames, one FFmpeg kept all 100, with a mean Cr of 59.9 against 125.8 for the same pictures in MP4.
The move kept the color but lost 50 of the 100 pictures (<<evidence-reshape>>).
The server cannot play a raw elementary stream today in any case: the probe reports its duration as 0, so the stream has no media segment to play.

The two FFmpeg processes are joined by a pipe:

* The first FFmpeg copies only the source's chosen video stream into fragmented MP4, with the attempt's seek.
The copy keeps the source's raw timestamps, as the source's demuxer reads them: it passes `-copyts` without `-start_at_zero`.
The copy writes each packet as soon as it has one, in fragments of about one packet, with a new fragment at every keyframe.
Its movie timescale of one microsecond keeps every timestamp that the source's demuxer reads.
* The second FFmpeg reads the copy on its standard input as its first input, and the source as its second input.
The second input takes the single FFmpeg's seek and input options.
The second FFmpeg copies the source audio, or encodes it with the single FFmpeg's audio filters, as a single FFmpeg would.
Every audio codec that the job copies or encodes therefore works.
When the job copies the audio, holds no last frame, and the attempt's seek lies more than one segment period before its first segment, the second FFmpeg copies the audio from a third input of the source, seeked as link:0043-audio-tracks-and-text-subtitles-are-hls-renditions.adoc#decision-audio-grid[ADR 0043] decides for a copied track, with `-isync 1`.
* The second FFmpeg passes `-isync 1` with `-start_at_zero`.
`-isync` shifts the copy's timestamps by the difference between the start times of the two inputs (`apply_sync_offsets` in FFmpeg's `fftools/ffmpeg_opt.c`).
`-start_at_zero` then subtracts the source's start time from both inputs, so the video and the audio lie on a single FFmpeg's timeline.
* The second FFmpeg also writes the `framecrc` output of the source's streams, copied from its second input.
The worker reads where the source's streams end from that output, as it reads a single FFmpeg's.
* The worker reads the copy and moves the RPUs in its video samples:
** An RPU that starts a sample moves to the end of the previous picture's sample, which can lie in an earlier fragment.
The worker drops such an RPU when it has no previous picture, as for the first RPU after a seek or the first RPU of a file whose start was cut away.
** In the same jobs, an RPU that follows a slice of its sample but is not the sample's last NAL unit moves to the end of that sample.
** Within one sample, an RPU that already ends the sample stays last, because the decoder reads the last NAL unit, and the RPUs that move within that sample go ahead of it.
** A sample that holds RPUs and no slice decodes no picture, so its RPUs are copied behind the previous picture.
That sample keeps its bytes, because moving its RPUs would leave a video sample of zero bytes.
** The move counts NAL units as the probe's walk does (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036]).
A NAL unit that FFmpeg's NAL unit splitter drops before its decoders, and that the walk therefore skips, is never a slice or an RPU, and never keeps an RPU from being the last NAL unit of its sample.
** The worker moves the bytes that one length prefix delimits as a whole.
A length whose every NAL unit that reaches the decoder is an RPU, such as two RPUs joined by a start code, moves as one RPU, and the decoder reads only the last of those RPUs.
A length that also holds another NAL unit that reaches the decoder, such as a slice or a parameter set, keeps its place.
** An RPU that moves behind the previous picture from a later sample ends that picture's sample, after any RPU that the sample already held.
Of several RPUs that move behind one picture, the worker keeps only the last.
* The worker gives each video sample of the copy the duration that its packet had in the source.
The copy's MP4 muxer writes each video sample's duration as the gap to the next sample's decoding time, not the source's duration, such as a Matroska track's default duration.
The decoder gives each picture its packet's duration, and the second FFmpeg's frame-rate conversion repeats and drops pictures by that duration.
* The first FFmpeg therefore also writes the timing of each video packet that it copies, as FFmpeg's `framecrc` output, to a Unix socket that the worker opens for the attempt.
The worker pairs those packets with the copy's video samples in order and checks that their keyframe flags agree.
It does not compare timestamps, because FFmpeg repairs bad timestamps separately for each output, and the two outputs repair them differently after a Matroska seek with B-frames.
* In a fragment whose video samples change, each video sample gets a track fragment of its own that states its decoding time, so that a changed duration moves no later sample.
The worker rewrites only those track fragments, the sample sizes and the data offsets, and passes every other byte unchanged.
No timestamp and no other track changes.
* The second FFmpeg encodes the rewritten copy with the usual filters.
* The second FFmpeg trims the video at the attempt's start, because the copy keeps every packet from the keyframe at or before the seek.

The worker repairs `STARTS_SAMPLE` as the shifted layout, because the shifted layout is the one that a known muxer wrote.
The cost falls on a file in the own-first layout: the repair gets each of its pictures the next picture's RPU.
Reading every RPU that starts a sample as its own picture's would instead get each picture of a shifted file, cut or not, the previous picture's RPU.

The worker also moves the RPUs of a stream whose placement is `UNSPECIFIED`.
The move changes only the samples that hold an RPU which FFmpeg's decoder ignores, and the samples that such RPUs move behind.
A stream whose RPUs already end their samples therefore passes through the move unchanged.
A profile 5 picture has no compatible base, so it shows correctly only when the decoder reads its RPU.
The in-sample move also repairs an `UNSPECIFIED` stream whose RPU follows a slice but is not the last NAL unit of its sample.

The worker accepts only the fragmented MP4 that FFmpeg writes with the copy's settings.
It fails the attempt as malformed output for any other copy, with one exception.
When the copy's video is not HEVC, although the job names an HEVC source, the worker fails the attempt as an output mismatch (`JOB_ATTEMPT_FAILURE_OUTPUT_MISMATCH`).
The source does not hold what the job names, so every retry of the same job would fail the same way.
The server therefore treats that failure as final for the job and does not retry it (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/661[streamarr/streamarr-server#661]).
Auto then falls back to an encode format attempt, as link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc#decision-repeating-failures[ADR 0019] decides.
Two cases instead pass video samples on without moving an RPU, and the attempt goes on:

* The worker holds the previous picture's fragment, and every fragment after it, until the next picture arrives.
When what it holds passes 32 MiB, the worker passes those fragments on.
An RPU that then starts a sample has no previous picture, so the worker drops it.
* A sample that the worker cannot delimit into NAL units, or that holds more than 4096 of them, keeps its bytes.
The next sample's leading RPU then has no previous picture either, and the worker drops it.

A picture whose RPU the worker drops keeps the color cast of an unreshaped picture.

The worker refuses a copy whose video timing it cannot use, and the attempt fails:

* When the timing ends before a video sample's packet arrives, the attempt fails as truncated output.
* When the worker cannot read the timing, a packet's keyframe flag disagrees with its sample's, or a duration does not convert exactly into the video track's timescale, the attempt fails as malformed output.
So does a timing that runs more than 65,536 packets ahead of the copy.

A sample whose packet has no duration keeps the duration that the muxer wrote.

The second FFmpeg reads the source's audio up to the source's end, whatever happens to the copy.
So a stop asks the first FFmpeg to quit and terminates the second FFmpeg.
An unexpected failure of the transfer and the worker's refusal of the copy also terminate the second FFmpeg.
A copy that ends inside a box is such a refusal.
A first FFmpeg that exits with an error after the whole copy reached the second FFmpeg terminates it too, and so does a failed read of the copy.
A failed write to the second FFmpeg means that it closed its input, so its own exit decides.
When the second FFmpeg outlives the grace period after such a termination, the worker ends it forcibly.
A stall, a failure or a forced end ends both processes.
The attempt's outcome follows this order:

. An unexpected failure of the transfer between the two processes, the worker's refusal of the copy, a failed read of the copy, or a first FFmpeg that exited with an error after the whole copy reached the second FFmpeg.
Each of these failed first and terminated the second FFmpeg, so the second FFmpeg's exit does not decide.
When the copy ends inside a box and the first FFmpeg exited with an error, that error decides, because it explains the cut.
. The second FFmpeg's exit, as a single FFmpeg's exit would decide.
. A copy that did not reach the second FFmpeg whole because a write to it failed, or a first FFmpeg that exited with an error, when the second FFmpeg exited cleanly.

Neither process writes media to a temporary file.
The socket for the video timing is the attempt's only file, and the worker removes it.

*Why:* In a local build of the worker image with the pinned FFmpeg 8.1.3, the shifted layout of a profile 5 excerpt encoded through the move gave a mean Cr of 122.3 over the first 3 frames, with the placement `STARTS_SAMPLE` and with `UNSPECIFIED`.
The same excerpt muxed correctly, encoded by one FFmpeg, also gave 122.3, and the output packets were identical.
128 is neutral on that 8-bit scale.
The shifted excerpt through one FFmpeg, without the move, gave 62.7.
A move in the wrong direction shows: on a fixture whose pictures carry RPUs of two different sources, the previous-picture move gave 99 of 100 frames of the own-first layout the wrong reshape.
Either reading of an RPU that starts a sample gives each picture of the other layout a neighbour's RPU, so the repair follows the layout that a known muxer wrote.
On a variable-frame-rate HEVC source with B-frames, the pipe showed all 48 source pictures in the same sequence as one FFmpeg, and every output packet matched.
With the durations that the copy's muxer wrote, it showed 43 of the 48.
Across 108 audio cases, 106 matched the single FFmpeg packet for packet, and the other 2 failed on both paths.
Those cases wrote the audio into the copy.
With the audio read from the source's second input instead, the `framecrc` output of the encode was byte-identical to the single FFmpeg's for MP4, Matroska and MPEG-TS sources with B-frames, after seeks to 0, 9.7, 21.5 and 22 s.
The first media segment arrived about 100 to 120 ms later than from one FFmpeg.
On a raw HEVC stream with B-frames, 100 pictures long, the copy gave every packet its decoding time as its presentation time, and the encode through the pipe held 98 frames, where one FFmpeg held all 100.
Through one FFmpeg, 100 raw packets with B-frames from a profile 5 stream whose RPUs follow their pictures gave 100 of 100 distinct pictures, reshaped as the same stream in MP4.
Evidence: <<evidence-reshape>>.

[[gpu-stages]]
=== A job attempt that encodes on the GPU also decodes, scales and tone-maps on the GPU

An encoder backend is the encoder that a worker runs for a codec family, together with whether that encoder runs on hardware or in software.
When a job attempt encodes with a hardware encoder backend, the worker also decodes, scales and tone-maps that job attempt on the GPU.
Decoding still follows the hardware-decode rule of link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037].
ADR 0037's hardware-decode rule sends AVI sources to software decode.
The same rule sends codecs that the hardware decoder lacks or decodes wrongly to software decode.
The worker does not yet decode and scale on the GPU for every job attempt on a hardware encoder backend.
Only the job attempt that reshapes Dolby Vision into PQ on the GPU, under the five conditions below, decodes and scales on the GPU.
Every other job attempt on a hardware encoder backend decodes and scales on the CPU.
ADR 0037 decides hardware decode for seek attempts, which are the job attempts whose start sequence number is above zero.
Apart from the job attempt above, no seek attempt decodes in hardware yet.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/55[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#55] tracks hardware decode for seek attempts.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/57[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#57] tracks decoding and scaling on the GPU for the VideoToolbox encoders.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/14[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#14] tracks both for the VAAPI, QSV, AMF and RKMPP encoders.
For the NVENC encoders, no issue yet tracks GPU scaling for the other job attempts.
For the same encoders, no issue yet tracks GPU decoding for a job attempt whose start sequence number is zero.
A GPU tone mapper replaces the CPU `tonemapx` filter only after a measurement shows that the GPU tone mapper's output matches the `tonemapx` output with the mobius curve at a 1000-nit peak.
The GPU tone mapper keeps the mobius curve and the 1000-nit peak.
Until that measurement passes, the worker tone-maps the job attempt on the CPU with `tonemapx`.

That match rule governs tone mapping, which produces SDR.
A reshape of Dolby Vision into PQ is not a tone map.
The worker reshapes each picture with its RPU, the output stays PQ, and no tone curve runs.
The worker runs that reshape on the GPU only when all five conditions hold:

* The source is an HEVC Dolby Vision source with no compatible base, such as profile 5.
* The planned output is PQ.
* The job attempt encodes with `hevc_nvenc`.
* Every filter of the job attempt takes its frames on the GPU.
A deinterlacer takes frames from system memory, so an interlaced source keeps the CPU reshape.
So does a job attempt that burns in a subtitle with a filter that takes frames from system memory.
* The worker's own test encode of the GPU reshape passed.

That job attempt also decodes and scales on the GPU, so its frames stay on the GPU up to the encoder.
The server's video decision does not change: the worker chooses where the reshape runs, never the conversion.
Every other reshape of Dolby Vision stays on the CPU with `tonemapx`, into PQ or before a tone map.
An AV1 profile 10.0 source and a job attempt on a software encoder therefore keep the CPU reshape.
The GPU reshape does not have to match the CPU reshape.
Instead, the GPU reshape must stay close to the exact value.
The exact value is the code that BT.2100 and the Dolby Vision profile 5 definition give for a pixel, computed in double precision.
The bound applies to the frames that the GPU chain hands to `hevc_nvenc`, which are 10-bit narrow-range BT.2020 PQ.
On every flat color inside the BT.2020 gamut, each Y, Cb and Cr code of those frames must be within 2 code values of the exact value, on the 10-bit scale of 1,024 code values.
A flat color is an area in which every pixel has the same color, so the result does not depend on how a filter interpolates 4:2:0 chroma.
A difference of at most 2 code values on every sample means a PSNR of at least 54.2 dB against the exact value, with a peak of 1,023 code values, in each of Y, Cb and Cr.
Streamarr accepts a difference within that bound for this conversion, and a larger difference needs a new decision.
The bound does not cover a chroma edge, where the color changes between neighboring pixels.
The bound does not cover a color outside BT.2020 either, because no standard gives the code for such a color.
The worker runs the GPU reshape only after a measurement shows that the GPU reshape of the worker's pinned FFmpeg build stays within the bound.
After the reshape curves, `tonemap_cuda` converts each pixel to BT.2020 PQ.
`tonemap_cuda` can compute that conversion for each pixel or look the conversion up in a 3D table.
The table interpolates between 65 points on each axis.
By default, `tonemap_cuda` uses the table on a GPU that has few multiprocessors and is older than the Ampere generation.
The bound applies to the computation for each pixel and to the table alike.
The worker lets `tonemap_cuda` use the table only after a measurement shows that the table of the pinned build stays within the bound.
The measurement of the computation for each pixel and the measurement of the table must each include flat colors near the boundary of BT.2020.
A color lies near that boundary when its smallest linear RGB component is below 1 % of its largest component.
The color check of Streamarr's FFmpeg fork leaves out those colors.
A pass of that color check alone therefore does not show that the bound holds.
This acceptance does not extend to a tone map to SDR.
There, the match rule above still decides when a GPU tone mapper replaces `tonemapx`.

*Why:* On a host with few or slow cores, the CPU reshape paces this job attempt, and the hardware encoder waits for frames.
With FFmpeg limited to 4 CPU threads, the same encode finished 2.5 to 4.7 times faster in wall time with the decode, the scale and the reshape on the GPU than with them on the CPU.
With all 32 threads of the same host, a 1080p source at 1080p took about the same time both ways, so the gain is for hosts with few or slow cores.
Streamarr trades a difference of up to 2 code values for that speed.
The bound compares the GPU reshape with the exact value, not with the CPU reshape.
A bound against the CPU reshape would hold the GPU reshape to the errors of `tonemapx`.
On the pinned FFmpeg build, `tonemapx` was up to 68 code values away from the exact value on colors inside BT.709.
Evidence: <<evidence-gpu-reshape>>.

=== Every encode forces a codec profile, level and tier that the server chooses

The server computes each variant's codec profile and level.
H.265 (HEVC) and AV1 also signal a tier, Main or High.
The tier sets the highest bitrate that a level allows.
The server chooses the tier for H.265 and AV1.
Every encode forces the codec profile, the level and, for H.265 and AV1, the tier.
The codec profile is High for H.264 and Main for AV1.
For H.265, the codec profile is Main for an 8-bit encode and Main 10 for a 10-bit encode.
The server computes the H.264 level from ITU-T H.264 Table A-1.
The server computes the H.265 level from Annex A of ITU-T H.265.
The server computes the AV1 level from Annex A of the AV1 specification.
All three computations use the output picture size after cropping and rotation, and the exact output frame rate.
The H.264 computation also uses the variant's maximum bitrate and buffer size, because Table A-1 limits both.
For example, 1080p60 needs H.264 level 4.2, H.265 level 4.1 and AV1 level 4.1.
720p60 needs H.264 level 3.2.
The HEVC encode work (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/58[streamarr/streamarr-server#58]) checks the H.265 computation against libx265, as the measurements below checked the H.264 and AV1 computations.

Each encoder handles a forced level that is too low in its own way.
libx264 writes the forced level and only warns.
SVT-AV1 writes the forced level without any message.
libx265 refuses to start, so the job attempt fails.
The server's level computation must therefore be right for every encoder.

*Why:* A level table of about 60 lines predicted the level that each encoder chose on its own in 89 of 89 libx264 encodes and 85 of 85 SVT-AV1 encodes.
When the worker forced the codec profile and level, the encoders wrote exactly the requested values in 174 of 174 encodes.
At 1080p60, libx265 refused to start when forced to level 3.1 or level 4.
Evidence: <<evidence-levels-and-codec-strings>>.

=== An encode's codec string is forced, and a copy's comes from the probe

The probe computes the exact codec string of every video and audio stream from the stream's decoder configuration record.
Probe version 4 stores these codec strings, as link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032] records.
A stream copy's codec string is the codec string that the probe computed for the source stream.
An encode's codec string describes the output that the server plans and the worker delivers.
Forcing the codec profile, level, tier and bit depth does not force every compatibility or constraint flag in that string.
link:0058-hevc-main-encodes-normalize-implied-main-10-compatibility.adoc[ADR 0058] defines the normalization of HEVC Main's implied Main 10 compatibility before the exact output check.
Examples are `avc1.6400LL` and `av01.0.LLM.08`.
In both examples, `LL` stands for the computed level.
In the AV1 example, `M` stands for the Main tier and `08` stands for 8 bits.
The playlist builds each variant's `CODECS` attribute from these codec strings and never from a constant.
The server never delays the playlist to read a codec string from an initialization segment.
The transcode job carries each variant's codec string to the worker.
The worker reads every initialization segment that it produces.
The worker fails the job attempt when an initialization segment's codec string differs from the codec string in the transcode job (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/25[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#25]).
The forced-value check confirms that the delivered output writes exactly the values that the transcode job promises.
Its evidence names the FFmpeg binary, encoder, GPU and driver; a pass on one NVENC device does not qualify every NVENC device.
libx264 and SVT-AV1 passed the forced-value check.
Before the server encodes with libx265 or a hardware encoder, that encoder must pass the same forced-value check.
The NVENC work (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/56[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#56]) runs the forced-value check for NVENC.
The VideoToolbox work (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/57[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#57]) runs the forced-value check for VideoToolbox.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/14[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#14] runs the forced-value check for the remaining hardware encoders.
An H.265 encode's codec string also names the encode's sample-entry tag.
The sample-entry tag is the four-character code that names a track's coding format in an MP4 file, such as `hvc1` or `hev1`.
link:0009-h264-plus-av1-codec-strategy.adoc[ADR 0009] lets the server encode H.265 only after https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18] makes the worker write `hvc1`.
The Dolby Vision configuration record is the `dvcC` or `dvvC` box that declares a stream's Dolby Vision profile.
Every video copy runs with FFmpeg's `-strict unofficial` option.
The `-strict unofficial` option keeps the Dolby Vision configuration record wherever the pinned FFmpeg build allows the record.
The pinned build drops the record from every 10-bit Dolby Vision profile 8.2 copy.
The pinned build also drops the record from every Dolby Vision profile 5 copy whose sample-entry tag is `hev1`.
Streamarr accepts the profile 8.2 loss.
For profile 5, the worker writes the record itself, and the server copies profile 5 only as a genuine `dvh1` output (<<no-compatible-base-copies>>).

*Why:* The constant strings matched the produced media in 6 of 59 video copies.
Strings computed from the decoder configuration record matched 54 of 59 video copies and 42 of 42 audio copies.
The 54 video matches included all 50 H.264 and HEVC copies.
Evidence: <<evidence-levels-and-codec-strings>>.

=== The worker alone chooses a copy's sample-entry tag

The tag rule is the rule that picks the sample-entry tag of a copy.
The tag rule lives only in the worker.
Until the worker chooses the HEVC sample-entry tag of every copy (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18]), a copy of an MP4 or MOV source keeps the source's sample-entry tag.
The Dolby Vision retags in <<dolby-vision-copy-tags>> are the only exceptions.
A copy of HEVC from Matroska, MPEG-TS or a raw stream carries `hev1`.
The copy's codec string names the tag that the copy carries.
Any change to the tag rule, such as the change in https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18], increments the probe version.
A rescan then updates every stored codec string under link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]'s rules for probe versions.
A tag rule that is ready before probe version 4 is released becomes part of probe version 4.

Dolby Vision profiles 8.2 and 9 have an SDR compatible base.
A profile 8.2 stream carries HEVC.
A profile 9 stream carries H.264.
A copy of a source whose sample-entry tag is `dvh1` carries the sample-entry tag `hvc1`.
The server encodes a source whose sample-entry tag is `dvhe` until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18] lets the worker write that copy as `hvc1`.

The server encodes a source whose sample-entry tag is `dvav` until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18] lets the worker copy that source.
If the `avc1` copy fails a check on a real device, the server encodes Dolby Vision profile 9 sources instead.
Over HLS, every HEVC copy of a Dolby Vision source with an SDR compatible base carries `hvc1`, whatever the client declares.

A decoder configuration record is the box that holds a stream's codec parameters, such as `avcC` for H.264 or `hvcC` for HEVC.
A retagged Dolby Vision copy's codec string comes from the decoder configuration record of the Dolby Vision base layer.
The probe computes that codec string under the sample-entry tag that the copy carries.
For example, a copy of a `dva1` source carries `avc1`.
That copy's codec string comes from the source file's own `avcC`.
The codec string never comes from the Dolby Vision sample-entry tag.
The probe runs in the worker, so the probe computes the codec string with the worker's own tag rule.

The playlist entry of a Dolby Vision copy with an SDR compatible base carries a `CODECS` attribute built from the base layer's sample-entry tag.
That playlist entry declares `VIDEO-RANGE=SDR` explicitly.
That playlist entry carries no `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS` attribute.

*Why:* In a headless check, a Dolby Vision profile 8.2 copy under `dvh1` played in 0 of 3 runs in AVPlayer, in WebKit's native player, and in hls.js in Chrome and in Firefox.
The same copy under `hvc1` played in 3 of 3 runs in every headless player that decodes HEVC.
A Dolby Vision profile 9 copy under `dva1` failed in 4 of 6 headless player setups, in 0 of 3 runs each.
The same copy under `avc1` played in 18 of 18 runs across the six headless player setups.
No player took a Dolby Vision path for a profile 8.2 or 9 copy, even with the configuration record and `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`.

=== The server switches to an encode when a copy lacks its parameter sets

Parameter sets are the units that a decoder needs before the first picture: the SPS and PPS, and for HEVC also the VPS.
An `hvc1` or `avc1` sample entry tells a player that the decoder configuration record holds those parameter sets.
The worker checks the record of every `hvc1` or `avc1` copy.
When the record lacks those parameter sets, the worker fails the job attempt as an output mismatch and uploads nothing (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/pull/80[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#80]).
The worker never encodes the stream in place of the copy.
In every playback choice, the server then starts a new format attempt that encodes the video.
The new format attempt has its own stream session, playlist and initialization segment.
The new decision covers the whole stream session's video, because a copy and an encode use different segment lists.
The server starts this format attempt itself, and the attempt counts against the playback session's format-attempt budget.
In Original, the playback choice then changes to Auto, and the client tells the viewer that a converted version is playing (ADR 0052).
In a fixed quality, the encode keeps the same fixed quality, and the client tells the viewer that a converted version is playing (ADR 0052).

*Why:* An `hvc1` or `avc1` copy whose record holds no parameter sets does not play.
Only the server can change a decision, so only the server can switch the copy to an encode.
A new stream session keeps one rule: a playlist describes only its own stream session's output.
The old playlist already advertised the copy's codec string, so it cannot describe the encode.
Original and a fixed quality are manual choices, and the server never silently replaces a manual choice with a different output.
In both, the client tells the viewer about the replacement, so the replacement is visible (ADR 0052).
In Original, the client also shows Auto as the selected choice.

[[copy-planned-range]]
=== A stream copy plans its output range, and a copy of an untagged source plans SDR

Every copy's planned output holds a dynamic range.
A copy plans the range that it produces.
That range is the source's compatible base, with Dolby Vision and HDR10+ as the copy keeps, converts or strips them (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc#decision-bitstream-rewrites[ADR 0036]).
A copy of a source whose compatible base is `UNSPECIFIED` plans `SDR`.
The playlist entry of that copy still announces no `VIDEO-RANGE`, and HLS reads a missing `VIDEO-RANGE` as SDR.
A copy plans HDR10+ that the probe could not check as `ABSENT`, and it plans Dolby Vision that the probe could not check as `ABSENT`.
link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036] forbids `UNSPECIFIED` in a planned output, and the server keeps checking that rule once copies plan a range.
A copy that keeps Dolby Vision without converting it is the one exception.
Its plan keeps the source's Dolby Vision facts, including the RPU observations that the probe could not make.
For example, a probe version 4 result of a profile 8.2 stream holds no first RPU header and an `UNSPECIFIED` RPU placement, and the plan of its copy keeps both.
The copy passes those RPUs through unchanged, so the server plans no RPU of its own.
A copy that converts single-track Dolby Vision profile 7 to profile 8.1 plans the converted Dolby Vision instead (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc#decision-profile-7-conversion[ADR 0036]).
That plan holds a profile 8 record with compatibility identifier 1, a base layer and RPUs at the decision's Dolby Vision level.
That plan also holds an RPU header without an enhancement layer or a residual, and an RPU at the end of each picture's own sample.
The source description keeps the source's profile 7 facts.
A copy's planned output also holds the typed codec profile and level that probe version 5 reports, beside the codec string.
The decision also carries the viewer's HDR choice (<<hdr-choice>>), the output bit depth and the chroma subsampling.

Until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/396[streamarr/streamarr-server#396] ships, only an encode's planned output holds a dynamic range.
Until then, the playlist entry of a Dolby Vision copy with an SDR compatible base takes its `VIDEO-RANGE=SDR` from the source's compatible base.

*Why:* link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc#decision-unknown-values[ADR 0049] already lets an `UNSPECIFIED` compatible base fit a capability entry that accepts `SDR`.
Players show an untagged stream as BT.709.
Untagged files therefore keep playing as copies, as they do under the interim copy rule.
The playlist announces neither HDR10+ nor Dolby Vision for a copy whose probe could not check them, so a plan of `ABSENT` changes nothing that a client or worker sees.
A plan that refused the `UNSPECIFIED` RPU observations of a kept Dolby Vision stream would refuse every profile 8.2 copy of a probe version 4 result, and the server copies those today.

Probe version 5 reads the first frames of the chosen video stream (ADR 0036).

This rule also covers a profile 5 stream in MPEG-TS without the Dolby Vision descriptor.
The probe finds such a stream's RPUs even when FFmpeg's HEVC decoder ignores them, because the probe also parses the NAL units of the packets (ADR 0036).
The interim copy rule therefore encodes each such stream, and the worker tone-maps the encode.
When the stream's RPUs do not follow their pictures, the worker moves the RPUs before the encode under the conditions of <<reshape-rpus-starting-samples>>.
The rule misses a profile 5 stream without a configuration record from which no read decodes a frame, because the probe then finds no RPU header (<<record-less-profile-5-without-frames,Consequences>>).
Dolby Vision profile 10.0, which is AV1 with no compatible base, follows the same rule, so the server encodes a profile 10.0 stream.
Probe version 5 also finds the Dolby Vision profile 9 files from a known faulty muxer, in which an RPU starts a sample.
Such a file ends with a sample that holds no picture.
Its placement is `STARTS_SAMPLE` whether or not its start was cut away, so the server encodes it.
A file whose stored outcome comes from probe version 4 keeps the version 4 decisions until the server stores the file's probe version 5 outcome (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]).

*Why:* No playlist can yet describe an HDR copy truthfully to a client that declares no range.
WebKit's native player and AVPlayer refused PQ and Dolby Vision profile 8.1 copies in a variant that the playlist declared SDR.
When the playlist declared the variant PQ or HLG, AVPlayer without an HDR display refused the variant.
WebKit's native player timed out on that variant.
Evidence: <<evidence-copies>>.

[[hdr-copies]]
=== Once the playlist describes HDR copies, the server copies the best picture that the client declares

Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/396[streamarr/streamarr-server#396] ends the interim copy rule.
From then on, the server plans a PQ, HLG or Dolby Vision copy only for a client with one capability entry that accepts that output (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc#decision-playlist[ADR 0049]).
The playlist builds `VIDEO-RANGE` and `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS` from the decision.
A client that declares no dynamic range never receives `VIDEO-RANGE=PQ`, `VIDEO-RANGE=HLG` or `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`.
A variant's `CODECS` attribute always follows the sample entry that the output actually carries.
No copy rule overrides a `MediaFileProbePropertyIssue` that rules out a copy (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc#decision-property-issues[ADR 0036]).
The server still encodes every HEVC or H.264 stream whose RPU placement is `STARTS_SAMPLE`.

The server always delivers the best picture that the client declares.
No user setting chooses between Dolby Vision and its HDR base, so the decision carries no Dolby Vision choice.
The server picks one output for each client and format attempt.
The playlist never lists the copy with Dolby Vision stripped as a second variant beside the Dolby Vision copy.
A client may declare the base range but not the copy's Dolby Vision profile at the copy's Dolby Vision level.
For that client, the copy with Dolby Vision stripped is the first format attempt.
After a failed Dolby Vision copy, the copy with Dolby Vision stripped is a later format attempt in every playback choice (link:0052-a-failed-hls-copy-falls-back-to-an-encode-and-original-changes-to-auto.adoc#decision-dolby-vision[ADR 0052]).

Each kind of Dolby Vision source plays as follows:

* A profile 8.1 copy has a PQ base.
For a client that declares profile 8.1, it carries `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS="dvh1.08.LL/db1p"`.
* A profile 8.4 copy has an HLG base.
For a client that declares profile 8.4, it carries `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS="dvh1.08.LL/db4h"`.
* Profile 8.1 and 8.4 copies keep `CODECS` from the `hvc1` base sample entry.
`LL` is the copy's two-digit Dolby Vision level (<<dolby-vision-level>>).
* A profile 8 stream with compatibility identifier 6 has a PQ base, as on Ultra HD Blu-ray discs.
The server treats such a stream as profile 8.1.
* Profile 10.1 and 10.4 copies follow the profile 8.1 and 8.4 rules.
They carry `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS="dav1.10.LL/db1p"` and `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS="dav1.10.LL/db4h"`, and they keep `CODECS` from the `av01` base sample entry.
* Profile 8.2 and profile 9 copies keep the rules of <<dolby-vision-copy-tags>> for every client.
Each such copy keeps its RPUs, takes `CODECS` from its base sample entry, declares `VIDEO-RANGE=SDR` and carries no `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`.
* A single-track profile 7 copy reaches clients as profile 8.1 (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/651[streamarr/streamarr-server#651]).
The worker converts the copy during the bitstream rewrites (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc#decision-profile-7-conversion[ADR 0036]).
The server converts a stream whose configuration record names profile 7 and a base layer, and whose own samples carry the RPUs that the probe observed.
The stream's signaled transfer must be PQ, and its Dolby Vision level must be known.
A stream with a MEL and a stream with a FEL convert by the same rule.
The server compares the client's declaration with the converted output, profile 8.1 at the decision's level, and never with the source's profile 7.
A client that declares PQ but not profile 8.1 at that level receives the copy with Dolby Vision stripped.
When the level is unknown, a client that declares PQ also receives the copy with Dolby Vision stripped.
* A dual-track profile 7 source and a profile 6 source play their HDR10 base.
A client that declares PQ receives a copy of the base layer alone, with no RPU.
Every other client receives an encode.
* A Dolby Vision stream whose profile is unknown plays without Dolby Vision (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc#decision-unknown-profile[ADR 0049]).
* Profiles 5 and 10.0 follow <<no-compatible-base-copies>>.

The playlist carries `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS` for no other Dolby Vision copy.
link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc#decision-bitstream-rewrites[ADR 0036] decides how a copy strips Dolby Vision or HDR10+.

[[dolby-vision-level]]
=== The decision sets the Dolby Vision level of every Dolby Vision copy

The decision sets the Dolby Vision level of every copy that the playlist describes as Dolby Vision.
These are copies of profiles 5, 8.1, 8.4, 10.0, 10.1 and 10.4, and the converted profile 7 copy.
The level appears in the configuration record and in the playlist's codec string.
A client declares each Dolby Vision profile with a maximum Dolby Vision level (ADR 0049).
A copy above that maximum counts as undeclared.

* The server takes the level from the probed configuration record when the record names one.
* Otherwise, the server derives the level from the stored width, the stored height and the stored maximum frame rate (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/652[streamarr/streamarr-server#652]), with Dolby's level table.
The maximum frame rate is the highest rate anywhere in the file, so a later section at a higher rate raises the level.
The derivation never uses an encode's output frame rate, an average rate, or the rate in a copy's `FRAME-RATE` attribute.
The same stored facts always give the same level.
* The level never comes from the RPU's `vdr_rpu_level` field.
* When the width, the height or the maximum frame rate is unknown, the Dolby Vision copy is ineligible.
The server then chooses the permitted fallback.
That fallback is the copy with Dolby Vision stripped for a profile with a compatible base, and an encode for profiles 5 and 10.0.
Until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/652[streamarr/streamarr-server#652] stores the maximum frame rate, every Dolby Vision stream without a configuration record that names a level takes that fallback.

*Why:* A level derived from a lower rate than the file's highest rate understates the decoder's work in the faster section.
A player could then accept a copy that it cannot decode in time.

[[no-compatible-base-copies]]
=== A profile 5 copy needs a genuine `dvh1` output, and the server encodes profile 10.0 for now

Dolby Vision profiles 5 and 10.0 have no compatible base.
A capability entry accepts such an output through its Dolby Vision profile and level alone (ADR 0049).
The server never strips Dolby Vision from either profile, because the stripped picture has no base that a player could show.

A client that declares profile 5 at or above the copy's level receives the profile 5 copy.
That copy carries `CODECS="dvh1.05.LL"` and `VIDEO-RANGE=PQ`.
Profile 5 has no compatible base, so the copy carries no `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`.
The worker writes a genuine `dvh1` output.
The parameter sets sit in the decoder configuration record, and the Dolby Vision configuration record is present.
The pinned build drops that record from an `hev1` profile 5 copy, so the worker writes the record itself (ADR 0036).
The pinned build's MP4 muxer writes no `dvhe` sample entry, so a profile 5 copy never carries `dvhe`.
The server never offers a profile 5 copy under a plain HEVC sample entry, because a player that ignores the RPUs shows teal colors.
When the worker cannot write the `dvh1` output, the server encodes the stream.
Every client that does not declare profile 5 receives an encode, in every playback choice.
In Original, that encode plays in Auto (ADR 0052).
The encode is PQ for a client that declares PQ, under the conditions of <<encode-range>>, and SDR otherwise.

A profile 10.0 copy needs a `dav1` sample entry, and the pinned build's MP4 muxer writes none.
For now, the server encodes profile 10.0 for every client, in every playback choice.
The route to a `dav1` output is a worker rewrite of the initialization segment.
The worker will rename the `av01` sample entry to `dav1` and add the Dolby Vision configuration record, in the stage that applies the other bitstream rewrites (ADR 0036).
Once the worker produces a verified `dav1` output, a client that declares profile 10.0 receives the copy.
That copy carries `CODECS="dav1.10.LL"` and `VIDEO-RANGE=PQ`.

*Why:* A profile 5 copy without its Dolby Vision configuration record plays teal, because the record is the only Dolby Vision signal in the copy's initialization segment.
The epic's test material holds no real AV1 Dolby Vision source, and a constructed source tests only the plumbing of a `dav1` rewrite.

[[hdr-frame-rate]]
=== An HDR copy whose frame rate is unknown announces its range without `FRAME-RATE`

Every copy with a known frame rate carries `FRAME-RATE` (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/683[streamarr/streamarr-server#683]), and HDR copies keep that attribute.
When an HDR copy's frame rate is unknown, its playlist entry still announces `VIDEO-RANGE=PQ`, `VIDEO-RANGE=HLG` or `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`, without `FRAME-RATE`.
The server cannot encode such a stream either (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/446[streamarr/streamarr-server#446]).
When a player refuses the entry, the client reports a format error (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/401[streamarr/streamarr-server#401]).
Recovery then tries the copy with Dolby Vision stripped when it is eligible, in every playback choice.
Auto also tries any other eligible format.
When no format remains, playback ends with the typed terminal error (ADR 0052).

*Why:* Players other than AVPlayer may play such an entry.
Whether AVPlayer refuses a PQ or HLG entry without `FRAME-RATE` is unmeasured.
A refused copy would stop the file on every player.

[[encode-range]]
=== An encode keeps 10 bits or a PQ or HLG range when the client, the viewer's choice and a worker's encoder allow it

An encode's planned output has one range and one bit depth.
Every encoded variant of the stream session shares them.
The server never prepares an HDR output and an SDR output side by side.
Beyond 8-bit SDR, an encode has three outputs:

* PQ at 10 bits, for a source whose compatible base is PQ.
A Dolby Vision source with no compatible base, such as profile 5 or profile 10.0, also encodes to PQ at 10 bits.
* HLG at 10 bits, for a source whose compatible base is HLG.
* SDR at 10 bits, for a source of 10 bits or more whose compatible base is SDR or unspecified.

An encode never turns PQ into HLG or HLG into PQ.
An encode never gives an SDR source an HDR range, and it never gives an 8-bit SDR source 10 bits.

The server plans one of the three outputs only when all of these conditions hold:

* For a PQ or HLG output, the viewer has not chosen SDR (<<hdr-choice>>).
* The encode's codec profile holds the bit depth.
H.264 High and H.265 Main hold 8 bits, H.265 Main 10 holds 10 bits, and AV1 Main holds both.
An H.264 encode is therefore always 8-bit SDR.
* One capability entry of the client capability declaration accepts the encode with that range and bit depth (link:0049-capability-declarations-list-entries-and-an-output-fits-one-entry.adoc[ADR 0049]).
* A connected worker has a worker-verified encoder of the encode's codec family that passed the worker's own test encode of that output.
Each worker lists, for each encoder, which of four things passed: 10 bits, PQ, HLG, and the reshape of Dolby Vision into PQ.
A PQ encode of a Dolby Vision source with no compatible base needs both PQ and the reshape.
The server sends the encode only to a worker whose encoder lists everything that the output needs.
Only an encoder on a worker that can take the encode now counts.
While the only worker whose encoder lists PQ is busy, the server plans an 8-bit SDR encode on an idle worker.
A worker can take an encode when it has a free playback slot.
Once encoder headroom replaces playback slots, the headroom rules of link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] decide instead.

When no such output meets every condition, the encode is 8-bit SDR.
A client that declares neither PQ nor the source's Dolby Vision profile therefore receives an SDR encode of a profile 5 or profile 10.0 source.
An SDR encode of a source whose compatible base is PQ, HLG or none is always 8-bit, even in a codec profile that holds 10 bits.

The server ranks the encodes that meet these conditions in one order and plans the first encode in that order.
The order comes ahead of the codec preference of link:0009-h264-plus-av1-codec-strategy.adoc[ADR 0009].
Every comparison between two encodes follows the same order, so the encode that wins never depends on a third encode that loses.

. An encode at a higher frame rate comes before an encode at a lower frame rate, as the frame rate comes before the codec preference (ADR 0049).
A client that plays a 60 fps source's range or bit depth only at 30 fps receives an 8-bit SDR encode at 60 fps.
. At the same frame rate, the encodes come in five groups, in this order:
.. PQ and HLG encodes.
.. SDR encodes of AV1 or HEVC when a connected worker encodes that codec family in hardware.
These codec families form the first group of ADR 0009's codec preference.
.. 10-bit SDR encodes of AV1 and HEVC in software.
.. 8-bit H.264 encodes, on a hardware encoder or a software encoder.
.. 8-bit SDR encodes of AV1 and HEVC in software.
. Within a group, the codec preference decides.
Within one codec family, a 10-bit SDR encode comes before an 8-bit SDR encode.

A PQ or HLG encode therefore comes before an H.264 encode, although the codec preference ranks H.264 before HEVC and AV1 in software.
A PQ or HLG encode also comes before an SDR encode of a codec family that a worker encodes in hardware, even when the PQ or HLG encode runs on a software encoder.
A software encoder that lists PQ therefore comes before a hardware encoder that does not, when that hardware encoder writes another codec family or runs on another worker.
The order reads the one encoder that each worker lists for a codec family.
The order does not reach a software encoder on a worker that lists a hardware encoder for the same codec family, and <<explicitly-deferred>> leaves that case open.
An 8-bit SDR encode of a codec family that a worker encodes in hardware keeps its place before a 10-bit SDR encode in software.
Between two PQ encodes, a codec family that a worker encodes in hardware still comes first.

The order changes what the server on `main` plans in one case.
In that case, the source is 10-bit SDR, and no connected worker encodes HEVC or AV1 in hardware.
At the highest frame rate, no 8-bit H.264 encode meets the conditions, the AV1 encode keeps only 8 bits, and an HEVC Main 10 encode keeps 10 bits.
The server on `main` at commit `6833215c` then plans AV1 Main at 8 bits, and the order plans HEVC Main 10 at 10 bits.
When an 8-bit H.264 encode meets the conditions at that frame rate, the server on `main` already plans HEVC Main 10 at 10 bits.
No pull request builds the order yet.

The order changes no capacity rule.
A PQ or HLG encode on a software encoder runs only on a worker that can take it, and ADR 0044's headroom rules apply to it as to every other encode.
While no worker whose encoder lists the range can take the encode, the 8-bit SDR encode of the hardware codec family plays.

A PQ encode writes the static HDR metadata of HDR10.
The values come from stored probe facts, never from the frames at which a job attempt starts.
Every job attempt of a variant therefore writes the same values.
A PQ encode takes the source's mastering display and the source's content light level, each on its own.
When the source has no content light level, the encode takes the content light level that the probe read from the source's RPUs (link:0032-media-file-probe-results-are-persisted.adoc[ADR 0032]).
When the source has no mastering display, the encode writes a P3 D65 display with a maximum of 1000 cd/m² and a minimum of 0.0001 cd/m².
When no content light level is left, the encode writes 0, which means unknown.
An HLG encode writes no static HDR metadata.
No encode writes HDR10+ or Dolby Vision.

A failed copy does not rule out an HDR encode.
After a format error during an HDR copy, the encode that follows keeps the source's range when the conditions above hold (link:0052-a-failed-hls-copy-falls-back-to-an-encode-and-original-changes-to-auto.adoc[ADR 0052]).
In Auto, a format error during a PQ or HLG encode makes the next format attempt an SDR encode, within the playback session's format-attempt budget.
After a format error during a PQ, HLG or 10-bit SDR encode, that range and bit depth stay out of every later format attempt of the playback session, in every codec family.
The same codec family can still encode 8-bit SDR.
After a format error during an 8-bit SDR encode, the codec family stays out instead.
A format error that the server attributes to an encoded lower variant beside a copy is the exception: the format error changes no format and keeps nothing out, because the server withdraws the lower variants and the copy plays on alone (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
A fixed quality never changes its encode's range after a format error.
A format error during a fixed quality's encode ends playback with the typed terminal error (ADR 0052).
<<hardware-failure-range>> decides what an encode keeps after its hardware encoder fails.

*Why:* A 10-bit source that is reduced to 8 bits shows color bands.
Before this rule, HDR reached a client only as a copy, so a client with an HDR display saw SDR whenever the server encoded.
Almost no device decodes 10-bit H.264, so H.264 stays 8-bit SDR.
Without the order before H.264, the server would encode 8-bit SDR H.264 for every client that plays H.264, whenever no worker encodes HEVC or AV1 in hardware.
One order for every comparison makes the winner independent of the encodes that lose.
Under rules that compare two encodes at a time, whether the client declares H.264 decides between 8-bit AV1 and 10-bit HEVC, although the H.264 encode plays in neither case.
A 10-bit SDR encode in software comes before an 8-bit encode in software, because both run on software encoders and only the 10-bit encode keeps the source's bit depth.
A PQ or HLG encode comes before a hardware codec family's SDR encode to keep a TV in its HDR mode as long as possible.
Without that order, a hardware encoder that writes only 8-bit SDR would give every HDR source an SDR encode, although a software encoder of another codec family or another worker could keep the range.
An older worker, and an encoder that did not pass its test encode, list no such output, so neither receives an encode that it cannot write.
Values from stored facts are the same in every job attempt, and link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] requires a variant's initialization segment to stay identical across job attempts.
In Auto, an SDR picture that plays is better than an error, so Auto gives HDR up within its budget.
A fixed quality with HDR chosen is a manual choice, and the server never silently replaces a manual choice.

[[hardware-failure-range]]
=== A software continuation keeps the range of the encode whose hardware encoder failed

When a hardware encoder backend fails during a stream, the server continues the variant on a software encoder of the same codec family (link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc#decision-hardware-failure[ADR 0044]).
That software continuation keeps the range and the bit depth of the failed encode.
It runs only on a worker that can take the encode and whose software encoder of that codec family lists everything that the output needs (<<encode-range>>).
Every connected worker counts, not only the worker whose hardware encoder failed.
The continuation follows no format error, and it counts no format attempt.
An 8-bit SDR encode continues as 8-bit SDR, even when the software encoder lists PQ and the source is HDR.
An encoded lower variant beside a copy gets no software continuation: its hardware encoder failure withdraws the lower variants, and the copy plays on (<<lower-variant-failure>>).

When no such worker exists for a 10-bit SDR encode, the encode continues as 8-bit SDR on a software encoder of the same codec family, in every playback choice.
The range stays SDR, so this continuation counts no format attempt, and the server names no format change.

When no such worker exists for a PQ or HLG encode, or no worker can take a 10-bit SDR encode even in 8 bits, the playback choice decides:

* In Auto, the stream changes format as it does after a format error during that encode (<<encode-range>>).
The next format attempt counts against the playback session's format-attempt budget, and the failed range and bit depth stay out of the rest of the playback session.
That format attempt can use another codec family, or the same codec family on a software encoder (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
* In a fixed quality, playback ends and the playback session reports the failure.
A fixed quality never changes its range silently.

When no worker can take the software continuation of an 8-bit SDR encode, the stream session fails, as ADR 0044 decides.

Before this decision, the server planned the range and the bit depth again from the software encoders.
A PQ encode then continued as 8-bit SDR when no software encoder of the codec family listed PQ, and an 8-bit SDR encode of an HDR source could continue as PQ.
No format error preceded either change, and both happened in every playback choice, a fixed quality with HDR chosen included.
On 2026-11-11 the maintainer decided that this was a defect.
Draft https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/913[streamarr/streamarr-server#913] builds the rule of this section, and the server on `main` keeps the defect until that pull request merges.

*Why:* A software encoder that writes the failed encode's range keeps a TV in its HDR mode.
A change of range that follows no format error reports no failure and counts no format attempt.
In Auto, an SDR picture that plays is better than an error, and the change counts as any other failed HDR encode does.
In a fixed quality, the range is the viewer's manual choice, and the server never silently replaces a manual choice.

[[failed-hardware-encoder]]
=== After a hardware encoder fails, the playback session encodes that codec family only on software encoders

A hardware encoder can fail or refuse the encode of the variant that the player requests.
After such a failure or refusal, no later format attempt of the same playback session encodes the hardware encoder's codec family on a hardware encoder.
The rule covers that codec family on every worker, not only on the worker whose hardware encoder failed.
The rule lasts for the rest of the playback session, also when the worker offers the hardware encoder again.
A later format attempt can still encode that codec family on a software encoder, or encode another codec family.

When no connected worker has a software encoder of that codec family, no later format attempt can encode that codec family.
A client that also declares another codec family then plays that codec family.
When no other encode can play, the server refuses the stream session for lack of capacity.
The server refuses for lack of capacity only when a hardware encoder of that codec family on a connected worker could encode the request.
The hardware encoder that failed counts as such an encoder.
The client then receives `TranscodeCapacityUnavailableError`, and the viewer reads "The server can't start this video right now. Try again in a few minutes."
When not even a hardware encoder of that codec family could encode the request, the server refuses the stream session because no video format is eligible, as it refuses any other request that no encoder can play.

The software continuation of <<hardware-failure-range>> needs a software encoder of its codec family.
When no connected worker has a software encoder of that codec family, the server always refuses the software continuation for lack of capacity.
What follows that refusal is what follows when no worker can take the software continuation (<<hardware-failure-range>>).

Draft https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/913[streamarr/streamarr-server#913] builds the rules of this section.

*Why:* A worker keeps offering a hardware encoder that refused an encode at its driver's session limit.
Without this rule, Auto's next format attempt could go straight back to the hardware encoder that refused.
The server chooses an encoder by codec family and encoder backend, and a decision cannot name a worker to avoid.
The rule therefore covers the codec family on every worker.
A refusal for lack of capacity names the real cause, which is that no connected worker can encode what the client plays at that moment.
A refusal because no video format is eligible would tell the viewer to try another device, although the client's declaration did not cause the refusal.

[[lower-variant-range]]
=== In Auto, the lower variants beside a copy keep the copy's range and bit depth and use its codec family

In Auto, the multivariant playlist lists encoded lower variants beside a copy (link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc#decision-lower-variants[ADR 0050]).
Auto still prefers a copy, whatever the copy's codec family.
This rule decides only which encodes the playlist lists beside the copy.

Each lower variant keeps the copy's range and bit depth.
The range is the one that the copy plays after its bitstream rewrites, so a copy with Dolby Vision stripped passes on its compatible base's range.
The bit depth is 8 bits beside an 8-bit SDR copy and 10 bits beside a 10-bit copy.
Each lower variant also uses the copy's codec family: H.264 beside an H.264 copy, HEVC beside an HEVC copy and AV1 beside an AV1 copy.
A lower variant meets two conditions of <<encode-range>>: its codec profile holds its bit depth, and one capability entry of the client capability declaration accepts it in the copy's codec family with its range and bit depth.
A worker must also have a worker-verified encoder of the copy's codec family that lists everything that the output needs.

When no encode meets these conditions, the playlist lists the copy alone.
It also lists the copy alone when the copy's range and bit depth, or its codec family, already failed in the playback session (<<encode-range>>), and when no encode writes the copy's range at its bit depth, as for a 12-bit copy or an HDR copy at 8 bits.
An H.264 copy of a 10-bit or HDR source is therefore always listed alone, because no H.264 encode holds 10 bits.
The server never lists a lower variant in another range, bit depth or codec family beside a copy.
The codec preference of link:0009-h264-plus-av1-codec-strategy.adoc[ADR 0009] therefore never chooses the codec family of a lower variant beside a copy.
It still decides the codec family of every stream session without a copy (<<encode-range>>).

A copy that the playlist describes as Dolby Vision (<<dolby-vision-level>>) is listed alone for now.
No encode writes Dolby Vision (<<encode-range>>), so every lower variant beside such a copy would take a TV out of its Dolby Vision mode.
A profile 8.2 or 9 copy, which the playlist describes as SDR (<<hdr-copies>>), and a copy with Dolby Vision stripped list lower variants as every other copy does.
Lower variants beside a Dolby Vision copy wait for a measurement (<<explicitly-deferred>>).

The upload budget can find that a copy does not fit the streams' share (link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc#decision-upload-budget[ADR 0050]).
When the copy lists lower variants, the budget leaves the copy out, and the top lower variant that stays plays.
When a copy of an HDR, 10-bit or Dolby Vision source lists no lower variants and does not fit as the server creates the stream session, the stream session plays as an encode instead.
That encode is Auto's encoded quality ladder, in the range and the bit depth that <<encode-range>> plans: the source's range when the conditions of <<encode-range>> hold, and 8-bit SDR otherwise.
The budget leaves variants out of that ladder as out of any other, so the best encode that fits the budget plays.
A stream session that never had a copy follows the same rule.

These decisions date from 2026-11-11.
Draft https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/759[streamarr/streamarr-server#759] builds the lower variants' range, bit depth and codec family, both conditions, and the Dolby Vision copy listed alone.
As that pull request builds it, a busy worker's encoder counts for a lower variant, because a lower variant starts only when the player requests it.
No pull request builds the upload budget's rule yet.
As https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/796[streamarr/streamarr-server#796] and streamarr/streamarr-server#759 build the budget, a copy that the playlist lists alone is its playlist's lowest variant, so it stays and plays over the budget.
<<explicitly-deferred>> and ADR 0050 list what these decisions leave open.

*Why:* A TV then stays in its HDR mode as long as possible.
A client can play only a variant that one of its capability entries accepts.
Players that move only within one codec family and one range read a lower variant in another codec family or range as a second ladder.
Such a player leaves the copy when the bandwidth falls and does not return to it, so a ladder in another codec family or range cannot be moved along.
Such players also read a Dolby Vision copy and lower variants without Dolby Vision as two ladders, so those lower variants would turn the copy into a one-way step down (<<evidence-encode-range>>).
A copy is still the best picture that the client declares, so Auto keeps it whatever its codec family, and the negotiated codec preference decides only where no copy plays.
A copy that plays over the budget takes upload that every counted stream session shares.
The encode that replaces it is the one that a stream session without a copy already plays, so the budget needs no rule of its own for the range.

[[lower-variant-failure]]
=== A failure that the server attributes to a lower variant replaces the stream session with one that lists the copy alone

When the server attributes a failure to an encoded lower variant beside a copy, the server withdraws the lower variants.
To withdraw them, the server replaces the stream session with a new stream session that lists the copy alone.
Four failures of a lower variant withdraw the lower variants:

* a format error that the player reports and that the server attributes to the lower variant (below);
* a job attempt failure that every later job attempt would repeat: an output mismatch or an invalid specification (link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc#decision-repeating-failures[ADR 0019]), or a segment over the segment cap that every replacement attempt would cut again (link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc#decision-segment-list[ADR 0037]);
* a failure of the lower variant's hardware encoder;
* exhausted recovery, after the lower variant's job attempts failed on every worker that can run them (ADR 0019).

The new stream session lists the same copy in the same range and bit depth, and it lists no lower variants.
The new stream session starts where the viewer is in the video, and each player reloads the copy once.
The upload budget decides the new stream session as it decides every new stream session (link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc#decision-upload-budget[ADR 0050]).
When a copy of an HDR, 10-bit or Dolby Vision source does not fit the budget, <<lower-variant-range>> plays a new stream session as an encode, and what that encode keeps after a withdrawal is open (<<explicitly-deferred>>).
When the copy fits the budget, a withdrawal never changes the copy's range and never gives HDR up.
The replacement keeps the playback choice and counts no format attempt.
The replacement keeps no range, bit depth or codec family out of later format attempts.
No job attempt in another range starts, and no software encoder continues the lower variant (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
The withdrawn lower variants do not come back for the rest of the new stream session.
The URLs of the replaced stream session answer as the URLs of every replaced stream session do.
A later failure report or job failure of the replaced stream session changes nothing.

The player's report of a format error names no variant.
The server therefore attributes the report to the variant that the player plays.
That is the variant whose media segment the player received last, before that the variant of the player's newest media-segment request, and before any request the variant that the stream session started for its playlist.
After a switch up to the copy, the player can still hold lower variant pictures that the player has not shown yet.
When the player's decoder fails on those pictures, the server attributes the format error to the copy.
The copy then recovers as every failed copy does, and that recovery can give HDR up.
The rules of this section, the promise that a withdrawal never gives HDR up included, therefore cover only the failures that the server attributes to a lower variant.

A failure of the copy itself recovers as the failure of every copy does.
After a format error during an HDR copy, the next format attempt keeps the source's range when <<encode-range>> allows it (link:0052-a-failed-hls-copy-falls-back-to-an-encode-and-original-changes-to-auto.adoc[ADR 0052]).
The same holds for the copy alone after a withdrawal, and that next format attempt counts as any other does.
A failure of a variant in a stream session without a copy also recovers as before.

The withdrawal dates from 2026-11-11, and the replacement of the stream session dates from 2026-11-13.
Draft https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/759[streamarr/streamarr-server#759] builds both at its head `10188f6fa`.
As that pull request builds it, the playback session fails when the server cannot create the new stream session, and <<explicitly-deferred>> leaves that case open.

*Why:* The copy is the best picture that the client declares, and a lower variant's failure says nothing about the copy.
Ending the copy, spending a format attempt or giving the range up would cost the viewer the source's picture for a variant that the player may not play again.
The viewer loses only the lower variants, for the rest of the stream session that replaces the first.
Both clients already load a stream session that replaces another, so the replacement needs no change to either client.
The replaced stream session's URLs answer as after any other replacement, so the replacement needs no exception to ADR 0019.
A withdrawal inside the same stream session would have needed both, and on a link slower than the copy the web client's player froze for 143 s (<<evidence-encode-range>>).
The replacement costs each player one reload of the copy, and in measured runs playback continued every time.
A report that names its variant would change the GraphQL contract and both clients, so the server attributes the report, and the rules promise less for a format error than for the other three failures.

[[hdr-choice]]
=== The viewer chooses HDR or SDR in a fixed quality, and Original always plays the source's range

A stream session request carries an optional HDR choice, `HDR` or `SDR`.
In the GraphQL contract, the choice is an optional `dynamicRange` field on `StreamingOptionsInput`.
Without a choice, the playback choice decides: the output keeps the source's HDR range when the client declares that range.
For an encode, <<encode-range>> adds the conditions under which the output keeps that range.

Original always plays the source's dynamic range.
In Original, the client shows the HDR toggle disabled, with an explanation.
SDR is a choice in a fixed quality only.
Auto hides the HDR toggle (ADR 0052).

A copy keeps the source's range, so SDR for an HDR source needs an encode.
When the viewer chooses SDR, the server therefore refuses the copy of an HDR source and encodes 8-bit SDR.
An encode keeps the source's PQ or HLG range only under the conditions of <<encode-range>>.
When a fixed quality's tier needs an encode of an HDR source and those conditions do not hold, the encode is SDR, even when the viewer chose HDR.
The server names SDR as the output range in its answer.
The client then shows SDR in its HDR toggle, with a notice.

Auto gives HDR up for SDR after a format error during a PQ or HLG encode, as <<encode-range>> decides.
Auto also gives HDR up when a hardware encoder fails during such an encode and no software encoder can continue it in that range (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
When the viewer switches from Auto to a fixed quality, the picture that plays keeps its range.
An SDR picture then stays SDR although the source is HDR.
The switch keeps the picture through the HDR choice, with no new field in the contract.
The server's contract asks the client to send the range that plays as the HDR choice of the fixed quality's request.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-web/issues/29[streamarr/streamarr-web#29] and https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-apple/issues/60[streamarr/streamarr-apple#60] own the clients' half.
In Auto, the lower variants beside a copy also keep the copy's range (<<lower-variant-range>>), and a withdrawal of the lower variants never gives HDR up while the copy fits the upload budget (<<lower-variant-failure>>).

*Why:* Original promises the source's own picture, and a copy keeps the source's range.
This rule needs no change to ADR 0052, which plays every encode that Original reaches in Auto.
The SDR encode in a fixed quality mirrors ADR 0052's fixed-quality fallback, which already shows SDR after a failed HDR copy.
The server names the SDR output, so the replacement is not silent.

[[copy-refusals]]
=== The decision names why the server did not copy

The server may encode a stream, or copy a stream that the client could not play directly.
The decision then lists the properties that each capability entry of the source's codec family did not accept.
When no capability entry names the source's codec family, the decision names the codec family.
The decision also names each property issue that rules out a copy.
The server also logs the list and counts it in a metric tagged by property.
The list becomes the decision reasons of the stream session (<<decision-reasons>>).

[[decision-reasons]]
=== Each playback session keeps typed decision reasons, which viewers and ServerAdmins can see

The stream decision records why it plays the file directly, copies a stream or encodes it.
It records each reason as a typed value, not as text.
A reason names the fact that ruled out a copy or direct play, such as a property that <<copy-refusals>> lists.
Examples are a codec that the client does not declare, a source above the viewer's maximum bitrate, a subtitle that needs burn-in, and a copy that lacks its parameter sets.
The playback session keeps the reasons of each of its stream sessions.
The issue that builds the reasons names their values.

Two views show the reasons.
The Apple and web players offer a playback-data panel, which any viewer can open during playback.
The panel shows the viewer's own playback session: whether each stream plays directly, copies or encodes, and why.
A live active-streams view shows a ServerAdmin every active stream session.
Each row shows the Profile that watches, the media title, whether each stream plays directly, copies or encodes, and why.
The view updates through a GraphQL subscription (link:0042-graphql-subscriptions-run-over-websocket-until-token-expiry.adoc[ADR 0042]).
It lists only active stream sessions and keeps no history.
Only a ServerAdmin can open it, and the server checks that authority from live facts through Cedar (link:0025-cedar-decides-authorization.adoc[ADR 0025]).
Like `libraryStatusChanged`, the subscription authorizes when it opens, and that authority lasts until the opening token expires (ADR 0042).
So a ServerAdmin whose authority ends keeps receiving rows for at most the rest of that token's lifetime.

*Why:* A viewer whose file encodes can see why without server logs, and can then change a setting or a device.
A ServerAdmin can see who uses worker capacity, and why each encode runs.
Typed reasons let each client word a reason in its own interface, and let tests assert the reason.

[[shared-playback-reports]]
=== A viewer can share a playback report with the Server Admin

The playback-data panel always offers "Share with Server Admin", because the panel cannot detect every problem.
Pressing it captures the panel's figures at that moment and offers an optional note of up to 280 characters.
A blank note counts as none.
The server then stores a playback report.

The report holds the server's side as it stands when the viewer shares it: the requested and playing playback choice, and each format attempt's delivery, tracks and decision reasons.
It also holds the player's figures, the client's capability declaration, the note and the stream session.
Each figure is a typed field, empty when the player did not know it, never free text or a document that the server cannot check.
A player that shows HDR but cannot name its format reports a plain HDR mode.
A TV reports each display figure on its own, because it knows none of them while it switches modes, and the Dolby Atmos flag is empty when the player does not know it.
The server checks the note's length and each figure's range at the API boundary.
The capability declaration is kept as the document that the server validated when the playback session started, because a ServerAdmin reads it whole and the server never queries it.
The figures belong to the format attempt that the panel showed, so the client names that attempt's stream session, and the server keeps the report only when that stream session is one of the playback session's started format attempts.

The client makes a new submission ID for each press of Share and sends the same ID again when it resends after a lost answer.
A playback session keeps one report for each submission ID, through a unique constraint, so a resend returns the stored report instead of storing a second one.
The server checks the playback session before it answers a resend, so a resend after the playback session closed reads as missing, as any report does.

A viewer shares a report only about a playback session that they own, under the same live facts as playback (link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018]).
Another viewer's playback session reads as missing.
A device-bound session can share a report, so a viewer on a TV shares from the TV's own panel.

Only a live ServerAdmin reads reports, through Cedar (link:0025-cedar-decides-authorization.adoc[ADR 0025]).
The Shared reports list sits next to the active-streams view, newest first, with an unread count.
A ServerAdmin's device-bound session cannot read reports, as it cannot administer anything else.
A report is read once any ServerAdmin marks it read, and it keeps the time of that first mark.

The server deletes a report 30 days after it was shared, and deletes it with its Profile.
Reports are facts in PostgreSQL; no file is stored.

[[explicitly-deferred]]
=== Explicitly deferred

* Dolby Vision and HDR10+ encodes.
<<encode-range>> decides the 10-bit, PQ and HLG encodes, and https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/397[streamarr/streamarr-server#397] adds neither Dolby Vision nor HDR10+ encodes.
* Whether a PQ or HLG encode on a software encoder also comes before an SDR encode on the hardware encoder of the same codec family on the same worker (<<encode-range>>).
A worker lists one encoder for each codec family that it encodes, and the server reads a worker's software encoders only after that worker's hardware encoder fails.
One worker whose hardware encoder writes only 8-bit SDR and whose software encoder of the same codec family lists PQ therefore encodes an HDR source to 8-bit SDR.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/911[streamarr/streamarr-server#911] read this from the code and the worker contract, and no test covers it.
A change would change the worker contract.
* The error type and the viewer's message when a fixed quality ends because no software encoder can continue the range (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
As https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/913[streamarr/streamarr-server#913] builds it, the client receives the errors of a refused stream session, and the viewer's message for one of them advises another device, which does not help.
* Whether a hardware encoder that a worker withdraws without a failure report of the variant's own job attempt counts as a hardware encoder failure of the variants pinned to it (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
Such a variant gets no software continuation today, and its range never changes silently.
streamarr/streamarr-server#913 read this from the code and did not run it.
* Lower variants beside a copy that the playlist describes as Dolby Vision (<<lower-variant-range>>).
A measurement would decide them: whether the players of the web and Apple clients, on a TV in its Dolby Vision mode, move down from such a copy to lower variants in its compatible base's range and back up to the copy, and what the TV shows at each change of mode.
* What the stream session that replaces the first after a withdrawal plays when the copy does not fit the upload budget (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
<<lower-variant-range>> plays such a stream session as an encode, and that encode could list the failed lower variant again.
Draft https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/934[streamarr/streamarr-server#934] proposes an encode that leaves the failed lower variant's encode out, and the copy alone when no other encode is left.
As that pull request builds it, a failed PQ, HLG or 10-bit encode keeps its format out in every codec family, so the encode can be SDR.
The other way plays the copy alone over the budget for the rest of that stream session.
* What plays when the server cannot create the stream session that lists the copy alone after a withdrawal (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
As https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/759[streamarr/streamarr-server#759] builds it, the playback session then fails, and the viewer sees an error although the copy was playing.
The creation fails when the file changed on disk and its new probe has not finished, when the file was removed, when the viewer is at the media's end, or on a database error.
* A report of a format error that names its variant.
Without it, the server can attribute a decode failure of lower variant pictures that the player still holds after a switch up to the copy (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
Such a report changes the GraphQL contract and both clients.
* Client-declared dynamic ranges (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/395[streamarr/streamarr-server#395]).
Client-declared dynamic ranges alone do not bring back HDR copies.
* Profile 10.0 copies, until the worker's `dav1` rewrite is verified (<<no-compatible-base-copies>>).
* A notification when a viewer shares a playback report (<<shared-playback-reports>>).
A ServerAdmin sees the unread count in the Shared reports list.
* How a planned output describes Dolby Vision in the worker contract (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/396[streamarr/streamarr-server#396]).
* Whether the produced-output check accepts HDR10+ or Dolby Vision in a copy whose plan names them `ABSENT`, because the probe could not check them (<<copy-planned-range>>).
* A stop-stream action in the active-streams view, and a history of ended stream sessions (<<decision-reasons>>).
* Choosing the HEVC sample-entry tag of every copy, and copying sources whose sample-entry tag is `dvhe` or `dvav` (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18]).
* A bound for the GPU reshape into PQ at a chroma edge and on a color outside BT.2020 (<<gpu-stages>>).

== Consequences

* Decision records are pure domain objects.
* Mode resolution considers all stream decisions (e.g. subtitle burn-in promotes REMUX -> FULL_TRANSCODE).
* New stream types follow the same pattern.
* Each playback session carries its typed decision reasons (<<decision-reasons>>).
A new rule in the stream decision adds a reason value with it.
* Encodes no longer produce High 10 or PQ-tagged H.264.
Firefox refuses High 10.
Apple's players refuse PQ-tagged H.264.
* With libx264, encoding a 10-bit or HDR source costs less than before.
The 8-bit output saves more than tone mapping costs.
With SVT-AV1, the cost stays about the same.
* A variant's codec profile, tier and level depend only on the variant.
Every job attempt of the variant therefore forces the same values.
The initialization segment then stays identical across job attempts, as link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] requires.
* When an encoder ignores a forced value, the worker fails the job attempt.
The playlist therefore never advertises a false codec string for an encode.
* A libx265 encode whose forced level is too low fails the job attempt, because libx265 refuses to start.
* A full-length Dolby Vision profile 5 encode grows each FFmpeg process's memory until the job attempt ends.
* The GPU reshape of the pinned FFmpeg build exceeds the bound on its difference from the exact value (<<gpu-stages>>, <<evidence-gpu-reshape>>).
No job attempt reshapes Dolby Vision on the GPU until the worker pins a build of Streamarr's FFmpeg fork whose GPU reshape stays within the bound (link:0057-the-worker-runs-ffmpeg-built-from-streamarrs-fork-of-the-pinned-build.adoc[ADR 0057]).
* Even with the color fixes of Streamarr's FFmpeg fork, the output of the 3D table of `tonemap_cuda` is further from the exact value than the bound allows (<<gpu-stages>>, <<evidence-gpu-reshape>>).
No job attempt reshapes Dolby Vision with that table until the worker pins a build whose table stays within the bound.
* A PQ encode that reshapes Dolby Vision on the GPU can differ from the same encode with the CPU reshape, because the bound holds only the GPU reshape to the exact value (<<gpu-stages>>).
When the hardware encoder backend fails during such a stream, the stream continues as link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc#decision-hardware-failure[ADR 0044] decides, on a software backend with the CPU reshape, and the picture can shift at that point.
With the color fixes of Streamarr's FFmpeg fork, the two chains differ most at chroma edges (<<evidence-gpu-reshape>>).
When no worker that can take the encode has a software encoder of the codec family that lists PQ and the reshape, Auto changes format and a fixed quality ends (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
* The worker does not report where a job attempt decodes or reshapes.
The headroom model therefore still charges the decode and the reshape of a job attempt with the GPU reshape to the worker's cores.
link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] decides that a hardware decode counts against the GPU's decode engines instead.
* An encode of an HEVC Dolby Vision source with no compatible base runs two FFmpeg processes only under the conditions of <<reshape-rpus-starting-samples>>.
When the source is read at real time, the first media segment arrives about 100 to 120 ms later than from one FFmpeg process.
* On that path, the job's audio comes out as from one FFmpeg for every audio codec that the job copies or encodes, including in an attempt that starts after the end of the source's audio and with audio that starts late.
The container's rotation and cropping also survive.
* An attempt that copies AC-3 or E-AC-3 audio and starts after the end of that audio fails as malformed output, from one FFmpeg as from two.
The pinned build's MP4 muxer writes no valid description of an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track that holds no packet.
Issue https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/511[streamarr/streamarr-server#511] removes this case: it pads audio that ends early, so that an attempt that starts after the end of the audio encodes silence instead of copying an empty track.
* When the worker moves the RPUs, a `STARTS_SAMPLE` placement, like an `UNSPECIFIED` one, takes the previous-picture move.
A file in the own-first layout therefore gets each picture the next picture's RPU.
* The server never copies a Dolby Vision file whose placement is `STARTS_SAMPLE`, whether it is a shifted file, a shifted file whose start was cut away, or an own-first file.
A copy of a generated profile 9 stream in the shifted layout failed at the end of the stream in Chromium and Firefox (<<evidence-dolby-vision-copies>>).
An own-first profile 8.2 or 9 file is encoded as well, although no player was measured on a copy of that layout.
* An `UNSPECIFIED` placement rules out no copy, so the interim copy rule can copy a profile 8.2 or 9 file whose first packets mix layouts.
* A stream whose RPU layout changes after the packets that the probe read gets the move that those packets call for.
In a stream that mixes the conforming and the shifted layouts, a picture whose sample already ends with its RPU also takes the next sample's leading RPU, and the decoder reads the moved one.
* A variable-frame-rate source keeps its pictures on the path that moves RPUs: the encode repeats and drops the same pictures as from one FFmpeg.
A copy whose video timing the worker cannot use fails the attempt rather than play with other pictures.
* A profile 5 source whose first packets carry no presentation times, such as a raw HEVC file or HEVC in AVI, or whose presentation times the probe could not see, runs one FFmpeg process and keeps every picture.
Its pictures keep their reshape where FFmpeg's HEVC parser ends each packet with its RPU, as in a raw HEVC file whose RPUs follow their pictures.
A picture whose RPU another NAL unit of its packet follows, and a picture of an AVI stream tagged `HEVC` whose RPU starts its sample, keep the color cast of an unreshaped picture.
* The encode of a raw HEVC profile 5 stream whose RPUs lead their samples keeps every picture, but every picture keeps the color cast of an unreshaped picture.
Streamarr keeps every picture rather than the reshape, because the move would reshape the pictures but drop about half of them.
The server cannot play such a stream yet anyway, because the probe reports a raw elementary stream's duration as 0.
* A stream whose first packets carry presentation times and whose later packets carry none still takes the move, and its encode can lose the pictures that B-frames reorder after those packets.
* An H.264 or AV1 Dolby Vision source with no compatible base runs one FFmpeg process.
FFmpeg's H.264 decoder reads no RPU, and its AV1 decoders attach an RPU to the next frame, so moving the RPUs would change nothing.
* An encode of a profile 5 stream whose RPUs FFmpeg's HEVC decoder ignores for another reason, such as an RPU that it cannot parse, still keeps the color cast of an unreshaped picture.
So does a picture whose RPU the worker drops after it passed on 32 MiB that it held, or after a sample that it could not delimit.
Under the interim copy rule, the server never copies a stream that the probe identifies as profile 5.
* [[record-less-profile-5-without-frames]]The server can still copy a Dolby Vision profile 5 stream without a configuration record that is tagged BT.709, when no read decodes a frame of the stream.
Such a copy plays teal.
The probe walks neither the extradata nor the packets of a stream from which no read decodes a frame (link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036]).
Without a configuration record, the probe then finds no RPU header and cannot tell that the stream is profile 5.
The stream's compatible base therefore follows its BT.709 transfer and is SDR, as under probe version 4.
The interim copy rule can then copy the stream to a client that names HEVC.
A profile 5 stream in MPEG-TS without the Dolby Vision descriptor, from which no read decodes a frame, likewise keeps the compatible base of its signaled transfer.
* A Dolby Vision copy with an SDR compatible base plays as SDR in every measured player.
* A 10-bit Dolby Vision profile 8.2 copy loses its configuration record under the pinned FFmpeg build.
In the measured players, the lost record changed nothing.
* A Dolby Vision profile 5 copy under `hev1` also loses its configuration record under the pinned FFmpeg build.
The record is the only Dolby Vision signal in such a copy's initialization segment.
Without the record, such a copy plays teal.
The server therefore copies profile 5 only as a genuine `dvh1` output (<<no-compatible-base-copies>>).
* A source whose compatible base is PQ, HLG or none plays as SDR on every client until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/396[streamarr/streamarr-server#396] ships.
After that, such a source plays in HDR only on a client that declares its range.
* A source whose codec profile is `UNSPECIFIED` always plays through an encode.
A source of a codec family whose codec profiles the worker contract does not name always has an `UNSPECIFIED` codec profile.
* The playlist advertises a copy's codec string before any job attempt runs.
A failed copy therefore costs the player one switch to a new playlist.
* A copy of an untagged source keeps playing as an SDR copy on every client that accepts SDR.
* A profile 10.0 source plays as an encode on every client.
That encode is PQ or SDR, as <<encode-range>> decides.
* A Dolby Vision stream without a configuration record that names a level gets no Dolby Vision copy until https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/652[streamarr/streamarr-server#652] stores each file's maximum frame rate.
Until then, such a stream plays as the copy with Dolby Vision stripped where the client declares its base, and otherwise as an encode.
* An HDR copy whose frame rate is unknown may start and then fail on a player that needs `FRAME-RATE`.
The viewer then waits through a failed start before the next format attempt or the typed terminal error.
* In Original, a viewer who wants SDR must pick a fixed quality.
* A viewer who picks a fixed quality below the size of an HDR source sees SDR and a notice when the encode cannot be PQ or HLG (<<hdr-choice>>, <<encode-range>>).
* An HDR source plays in HDR through an encode only where a connected worker's encoder passed its test encode of that range.
A server whose workers list no such output still encodes every HDR source to 8-bit SDR.
* A server with a hardware encoder that writes only 8-bit SDR encodes an HDR source on a software encoder of another codec family or another worker that lists the range, for a client that declares it (<<encode-range>>).
A software HEVC or AV1 encoder can fall behind the player on a large source, which is why ADR 0009 ranks hardware first.
The headroom rules of ADR 0044 guard against that.
The headroom model is not on `main` yet, and https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/828[streamarr/streamarr-server#828] builds its prediction.
Until the model decides, the server checks only that the worker has a free playback slot.
* When a hardware encoder fails during a fixed quality's PQ or HLG encode and no software encoder can continue that range, playback ends (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
A new playback session in the same fixed quality then plays 8-bit SDR with the notice of <<hdr-choice>> while the hardware encoder stays withdrawn.
A new playback session in Auto plays as well.
* When no software encoder keeps the 10 bits of a 10-bit SDR encode, the picture continues in 8 bits, and color bands can appear (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
* In Auto, a hardware encoder failure that no software encoder can continue in the same PQ or HLG range spends a format attempt.
During Auto's last budgeted format attempt, playback then ends with the typed terminal error (ADR 0052).
The default budget is four format attempts, so that end needs three earlier failures in the playback session.
* Once https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/759[streamarr/streamarr-server#759] merges, a copy plays alone in Auto when no encode in the copy's range, bit depth and codec family meets the conditions of <<lower-variant-range>>.
Every copy that the playlist describes as Dolby Vision plays alone, and so does every H.264 copy of a 10-bit or HDR source.
A server whose workers verify no encoder of a copy's codec family also plays that copy alone, even when another codec family could encode a lower variant.
Such a copy gives the player no lower variant when the viewer's bandwidth drops (link:0050-viewer-limits-bind-copies-and-quality-tiers-are-picture-boxes.adoc#decision-lower-variants[ADR 0050]).
* Beside an HEVC or AV1 copy, the lower variants run on a software encoder of that codec family when no worker encodes the family in hardware, although the codec preference would rank H.264 first for a client that declares it (link:0009-h264-plus-av1-codec-strategy.adoc#decision-hardware-first[ADR 0009]).
* The lower variants beside an HDR10+ copy carry HDR10's static metadata and no HDR10+, because no encode writes HDR10+.
* After a lower variant fails and the copy fits the upload budget, the copy plays alone for the rest of the stream session that replaces the first, and the player has no lower variant when the viewer's bandwidth drops (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
* A withdrawal costs each player one reload of the copy (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
In measured runs of the web client's player, each reload threw away 19 to 25 s of buffered video and took 7.7 to 23.1 s to play again (<<evidence-encode-range>>).
The web client gives its player no start position for the new stream session, so the player first loads the copy's first segment, which took 3.5 to 13 s of each reload.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-web/pull/235[streamarr/streamarr-web#235] loads the new stream session from where the viewer is instead.
No Apple device has played a replacement yet.
* The player's report names no variant, so after a switch up to the copy, a decode failure of lower variant pictures still in the player's buffer counts as the copy's and ends the copy (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
The next format attempt then follows the copy's recovery, which keeps an HDR source's range where <<encode-range>> allows it.
* After a hardware encoder fails, the rest of the playback session encodes that codec family only on software encoders, also on a worker whose hardware encoder of the codec family still works (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
A software HEVC or AV1 encoder can fall behind the player on a large source.
* A client that declares only a codec family whose hardware encoder failed cannot play again in the same playback session when no worker has a software encoder of that codec family (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
A new playback session can use that hardware encoder again once the worker offers it.
* Only whoever runs the server can see why a file was encoded, through the logs and the metric.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Decision reasons only in the server's logs: a viewer cannot read them, and a ServerAdmin must search them.
* Reasons as free text: clients could not word them in their own interface, and tests would match strings.
* An active-streams view open to every viewer: it would show each viewer what other Profiles watch.
* A live authority check before each row of the active-streams view: ADR 0042 bounds every subscription's authority by its token's lifetime, and this view needs no exception to that rule.
* A share button that appears only after the panel detects a problem: the panel cannot detect every problem that a viewer sees.
* A playback report stored whole as one JSON document or as an uploaded file: the server could neither check its figures nor page and count reports in SQL.
* Answering a resend from the stored report before checking the playback session: the answer would skip the playback ownership check.
* Keeping playback reports until a ServerAdmin deletes them: a report describes one moment of playback, and 30 days give a ServerAdmin time to read it.
* A read mark for each ServerAdmin: one mark tells every ServerAdmin that someone has looked at the report.
* 10-bit H.264 output (High 10): Firefox refuses High 10.
In headless Firefox on macOS, `MediaSource.isTypeSupported` returned false for High 10 in 21 of 21 runs.
Before this amendment, the pinned worker image encoded every 10-bit HEVC source to High 10, in 13 of 13 encodes.
* Constant codec strings: the constant strings matched the copy's initialization segment in 6 of 59 video copies from the pinned worker image.
Strings computed from the decoder configuration record matched 54 of the same 59 video copies.
The constants also stated level 4.0 for 1080p60 encodes that need level 4.2.
* Reading every codec string from the produced initialization segments: the playlist would wait for the slowest variant.
One failed first job attempt would block the playlist.
In a four-variant ladder on the pinned worker image, the last initialization segment arrived up to 11.1 s after FFmpeg started.
WebKit's native player and AVPlayer request the playlist once and give up after 20.2 s.
* Predicting approximate codec strings without the source's decoder configuration record: a copy's codec string must match the copy's initialization segment exactly.
The probe can compute the exact codec string.
* No interim copy rule before clients declare dynamic ranges: HDR copies fail in Apple's players when the playlist declares the copies SDR.
* A narrower interim rule that stops copying only Dolby Vision profile 5 and 10-bit H.264: the narrower rule would still copy PQ and HLG sources.
No playlist can describe such copies truthfully to a client that declares nothing.
* Moving the HDR playlist attributes into https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/395[streamarr/streamarr-server#395], so that HDR copies return with client-declared dynamic ranges: https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/396[streamarr/streamarr-server#396] makes the playlist's HDR attributes truthful.
HDR copies wait for https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/396[streamarr/streamarr-server#396].
* Declaring `VIDEO-RANGE=PQ` or `VIDEO-RANGE=HLG` on copies today: on macOS 26.6.2 with no HDR display, AVPlayer refused 4 of 4 such variants.
WebKit's native player timed out on 4 of 4 such variants.
The same variants played when the playlist declared them SDR.
* Copying HLG sources to clients that name the source's codec family: the HLG copies played.
The playlist could not state the copies' HLG range.
AVPlayer without an HDR display refuses a variant declared `VIDEO-RANGE=HLG`.
WebKit's native player times out on that variant.
* Leaving tone mapping to the worker's discretion: link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036] requires the server to choose every change to the source.
The worker would also probe the source a second time.
* Sending an HDR copy to a client on an SDR screen and letting the device tone-map it: the server keeps tone mapping, because no measurement shows how iOS and tvOS tone-map an HDR copy on an SDR TV.
A research issue measures that device tone mapping before Streamarr reconsiders it.
* The bt2390 tone curve at the same 1000-nit peak, or the bt2390 curve as a fallback if the mobius curve showed a color cast: above the peak, the bt2390 curve folds back and maps brighter input to darker output.
A highlight brighter than 1000 nits therefore renders darker than a 1000-nit highlight.
With the pinned build's filter, the bt2390 curve mapped a 983-nit PQ gray to luma 235.
The same curve mapped a 10,000-nit PQ gray to luma 168.
Streamarr chose the mobius curve with no condition.
* The hable tone curve: the hable curve makes pictures darker overall.
Below the peak, the hable curve gave a mean luma of 76.9 on a PQ source, against 92.9 for the mobius curve on the same source.
* Copying a Dolby Vision profile 7 source tagged SDR: the source makes contradictory claims.
The server never copies such a source, as link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036] decides.
* Copying no Dolby Vision source with an SDR compatible base until the worker chooses the HEVC tag of every copy (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/18[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#18]), or encoding every such source with a `dvh1` or `dvhe` tag until then: the retag makes `dvh1` copies play now.
In a headless check on macOS against the pinned worker image, a `dvh1` profile 8.2 copy played in 0 of 3 runs in each of AVPlayer, WebKit's native player, and hls.js in Chrome and in Firefox.
Written as `hvc1`, the same copy played in 3 of 3 runs in every player that decodes HEVC.
* Keeping `dva1` on a Dolby Vision profile 9 copy: in the same kind of headless check, the `dva1` copy failed in 4 of 6 player setups, in 0 of 3 runs each.
The `avc1` copy played in 18 of 18 runs across the six player setups.
* Choosing the HEVC tag of a Dolby Vision copy with an SDR compatible base from the client's declaration, after a tvOS check: the tag is settled now.
Every client receives `hvc1`.
* Encoding every Dolby Vision profile 9 source while the interim copy rule applies, or having the worker drop a trailing sample that holds no picture: probe version 5 finds the malformed files from their first packets, whether or not their start was cut away.
The server encodes only those files.
* Getting the pinned build's validity check for the configuration record relaxed upstream, building FFmpeg ourselves, or re-inserting the configuration record in the worker: for profile 8.2, the record changed nothing measured.
With the record restored, and even with `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS`, AVPlayer, Safari and an iPhone Air still showed SDR.
* Reading the first frame's RPU header in probe version 4, or encoding every Dolby Vision stream that has no configuration record: probe version 5 reads the RPU header instead.
A Dolby Vision stream without a configuration record keeps link:0036-video-contracts-share-vocabulary-and-dynamic-range-facts.adoc[ADR 0036]'s rule for the compatible base.
* Applying the refusal of an `UNSPECIFIED` codec profile to probe version 4 outcomes: a probe version 4 outcome stores no codec profile.
The server would then encode every source until the re-scan stored each file's probe version 5 outcome.
* `SUPPLEMENTAL-CODECS` on Dolby Vision copies with an SDR compatible base: adding the attribute changed nothing measurable in any headless player.
The attribute stays open in case a later need appears.
* Applying the HEVC tag rule in the server as well as in the worker: the tag rule would then live in two places.
* Keeping tone mapping on the CPU while the GPU decodes, scales and encodes, or using the GPU only to encode: when a job attempt encodes on the GPU, the worker runs every stage that the GPU can run on the GPU.
Tone mapping moves to the GPU once the GPU tone mapper matches the CPU output.
The worker does not yet decode and scale every such job attempt on the GPU (<<gpu-stages>>).
* Bounding the GPU reshape into PQ by its difference from the CPU reshape, as the match rule bounds a GPU tone map to SDR: a bound against `tonemapx` would mostly measure the error of `tonemapx`, which was up to 68 code values away from the exact value on the pinned FFmpeg build.
* Accepting the difference that the pinned build's GPU reshape showed against the CPU reshape, a constant offset of about 2 code values and a luma PSNR of 39.1 dB or more: the offset and the PSNR describe two different differences, so neither figure is a limit that a later measurement can pass or fail.
That GPU reshape also fails the bound against the exact value (<<evidence-gpu-reshape>>).
* Extending the accepted difference to a GPU tone map to SDR: the measurements cover only the reshape into PQ.
* Having the worker encode a stream whose copy fails the record check: the job's video decision and codec string would then no longer describe the worker's output.
* Encoding in place in the same stream session and making the player reload its playlist: the old playlist's `CODECS` attribute would no longer match the media until the player reloads.
* Predicting the failure from probe data: the worker cannot tell in advance which sources yield a record without parameter sets.
* Moving a profile 5 stream's RPUs with a bitstream filter of the pinned FFmpeg build: no filter in that build moves a NAL unit.
The `dovi_rpu` filter looks only at the last NAL unit of a packet.
The `filter_units` filter can delete NAL units, but deleting the RPUs removes the reshape.
* Patching FFmpeg's HEVC decoder so that it reads an RPU anywhere in its sample, or so that its MPEG-TS muxer carries more audio codecs: Streamarr runs the pinned Jellyfin build unchanged, and link:0029-pin-jellyfin-ffmpeg-releases.adoc[ADR 0029] defers an FFmpeg build pipeline of Streamarr's own.
* Fixing the colors after decoding, with a filter: the decoder has already discarded the RPUs, and nothing after the decoder can rebuild the per-frame reshape.
The first RPU header that the probe stores tells only the Dolby Vision profile and holds no reshape.
* Moving the RPUs in an MPEG-TS copy, as this amendment first built: MPEG-TS carries the audio's parameters only in the audio's packets.
An attempt that started after the end of the source's audio therefore failed and wrote no bytes, and the second FFmpeg had to read the start of the stream to learn its streams, which cost about 1.4 s before the first segment.
The move relied on the access unit delimiter that the MPEG-TS muxer writes at the start of each sample, and on the second FFmpeg's HEVC parser, which ends each access unit at a delimiter.
An RPU moved ahead of the delimiter became the last NAL unit of the picture before only through that parser rule, so the move could give an RPU only to the previous picture.
The pinned build's MPEG-TS muxer also carries no FLAC or ALAC, the copy lost Opus's pre-skip and the encoder's trim of the last AAC frame, and the copy dropped the container's rotation.
* A placement value for the own-first layout, `BEFORE_OWN_PICTURE`, with a move of each RPU that starts a sample to the end of its own sample, as this amendment first built: the probe would report that value for every file whose first picture's sample starts with an RPU.
A file in the shifted layout whose start was cut away starts the same way, and that move would give each of its pictures the previous picture's RPU.
On the own-first fixture, the move matched the conforming layout in 100 of 100 frames, but no known muxer writes that layout.
* Reading the end of the file to tell the own-first layout from a cut shifted file: the last sample of a shifted file holds an RPU and no picture, and the last sample of an own-first file holds a picture.
A file whose end was cut away as well ends with a picture in both layouts.
The probe would also read the end of every Dolby Vision file on the re-scan, where probe version 5 reads only the first frames.
* Encoding a stream whose placement is `UNSPECIFIED` with one FFmpeg, or failing its attempt, when its packets carry presentation times: one FFmpeg loses every RPU that starts a sample, and a failed attempt plays nothing.
The previous-picture move leaves a conforming stream unchanged.
* Moving the RPUs of a source whose packets carry no presentation times, as this amendment first did: the copy gave each packet its decoding time as its presentation time, and the encode of a raw HEVC stream with B-frames lost 2 of its 100 pictures.
Failing the attempt instead would play nothing, while one FFmpeg keeps every picture and still reads the RPUs that FFmpeg's HEVC parser puts at the end of their packets.
On a raw profile 5 stream with B-frames whose RPUs lead their samples, the move kept the color, but the encode held 50 of its 100 pictures (<<evidence-reshape>>).
* Giving the copy presentation times with `-fflags +genpts`, or with an input frame rate (`-r`), so that such a source could take the move.
`+genpts` gives a packet without a presentation time its decoding time, which loses the order of B-frames: on an H.264 stream with B-frames in AVI, 379 of 480 frames came out in another presentation order than from the same stream in MPEG-PS.
An input `-r` left all 480 packets of that stream without a presentation time.
Neither option makes every raw stream copyable either: with the settings of the server's stream copy, FFmpeg 8.1.3's MP4 muxer aborted on two raw FATE streams at an assertion in `movenc.c`, line 1248, with either option, with both and with neither.
* Requiring Dolby Vision profile 5 by number in the worker's conditions: a compatible base of none already means that no player can show the base without the RPUs, whatever the profile number.
* A raw HEVC stream between the two FFmpeg processes: a raw stream does not carry the source's timestamps.
* A Matroska stream between the two FFmpeg processes: the pinned build's Matroska muxer writes millisecond timestamps, and it changed the durations of the last audio packets.
The last AAC packet declared 1008 samples instead of 272.
* A NUT stream between the two FFmpeg processes: the pinned build's NUT muxer refuses a negative presentation timestamp, and its demuxer read ALAC back as an unknown codec tag and E-AC-3 as AC-3.
After some seeks, the demuxer lost video while FFmpeg exited with status 0, in one attempt delivering 2 of 192 video packets.
The NUT copy also lost the last packets' durations.
* Writing the job's audio into the copy, so that the second FFmpeg copies it unchanged and the source is read once.
FFmpeg's MP4 muxer writes no header until every track has a sample (`libavformat/movenc.c`).
So a copy that carried audio held back every picture that its demuxer read before the first audio sample.
On an MP4 source of 512x288 lossless HEVC, about 330 KB per picture, with B-frames and keyframes 10 s apart, the copy's first fragment held 63.8 MB when the copy carried AAC audio and read from 18 s.
That is over the worker's limit of 32 MiB for what it holds of the copy, so the attempt failed.
With video alone, the first fragment held 323 KB and the largest 332 KB.
`-max_interleave_delta 0` does not help, because 0 means that the muxer buffers without a bound.
The chosen design, which reads the audio from a second input of the source, has the two FFmpeg processes read the source's bytes twice.
On an excerpt, one FFmpeg read 1,699,613 bytes, and the pair read 3,399,226 bytes, although the audio packets held 50,412 bytes.
These are bytes that the processes read, not reads from storage.
The second FFmpeg reads the same part of the source seconds after the first, so the worker host's file cache normally serves the second read, and storage reads the source once.
The path is also narrow: only a Dolby Vision profile 5 source whose RPUs start their samples takes it, and only when the job encodes.
Profile 5 is the profile of streaming services' Dolby Vision, whose 4K streams typically stay below 25 Mb/s.
* Writing the source audio to a second output of the first FFmpeg, on a pipe of its own, so that the source is read once: each muxer writes on its own, so the header problem above does not arise.
It adds a second pipe between the two processes, and when one pipe fills while an FFmpeg waits on the other, both stall.
Ruling that out needs its own tests, and the file cache already spares storage the second read on this rare path.
* Carrying the source audio's sample rate and channel layout in the transcode job, so that the second FFmpeg could declare an MPEG-TS audio track before its first packet: the pinned build refused the sample rate as an MPEG-TS input option, and an audio copy still failed with `sample rate not set`.
The job also cannot restore a codec's configuration, such as FLAC's or ALAC's, its packet durations or its padding.
* Clearing the copy's frame rate with `setpts=PTS:strip_fps=1`, so that a variable-frame-rate source keeps its pictures: on the pinned build, each constant-frame-rate source then came out one frame short, with every frame different from a single FFmpeg's.
* Leaving the copy's video durations as the MP4 muxer writes them: on a variable-frame-rate source with B-frames, the encode showed 43 of 48 source pictures, where one FFmpeg showed all 48.
* Reading the video timing from the first FFmpeg's standard error: FFmpeg's log writes one line in up to five separate writes, and the `framecrc` output does not take the log's lock, so a timing record could land inside a log line.
A socket of its own carries nothing else.
* Changing the durations in place inside a fragment's single track run: a fragment can hold more than one video sample, as the first fragment of a copy with audio held two, so a changed duration would move the next sample's decoding time.
* Pairing each timing with its sample by timestamp: FFmpeg repairs bad timestamps separately for each output, and the `framecrc` output allows timestamps that MP4 does not.
After a Matroska seek with B-frames, the leading packets carried different timestamps in the two outputs, so a timestamp check refused every such seek.
* Seeking in the second FFmpeg with an output `-ss` option: FFmpeg subtracts that time from every output timestamp, so the attempt's timeline would no longer match a single FFmpeg's timeline.
The second FFmpeg trims instead.
An input seek cannot run on a pipe.
* A planned value of its own for a copy of an untagged source, such as "SDR, assumed": it adds a value to ADR 0036's vocabulary, and the playlist would still describe the copy as SDR.
* Encoding untagged sources for clients that declare HDR: it spends encoder capacity on the most common untagged files, old SDR files, which play as copies today.
* Refusing an HDR copy whose frame rate is unknown: the file would then play on no player, although players other than AVPlayer may play the entry.
AVPlayer's refusal of such an entry is unmeasured.
* Copying profile 10.0 once an FFmpeg pin update writes `dav1`: no prebuilt FFmpeg release is known to write `dav1`, and ADR 0029 pins prebuilt releases.
* A boolean `preferSdr` on the stream session request: it names only one of the two values that the HDR toggle shows.
* Playing an SDR encode at the source's size in Original when the viewer picks SDR, with Original kept: Original would then play an encode under the Original label, which ADR 0052 rules out.
* Changing Original to Auto, with ADR 0052's notice, when the viewer picks SDR in Original: a tap on SDR would also change the quality mode, which may surprise the viewer.
* Refusing a fixed quality's HDR request with a typed error when the tier needs an encode that cannot keep the range (<<encode-range>>): the viewer would get an error instead of a picture that can play.
* Offering only the fixed qualities that a copy covers while HDR is on: the viewer could not pick a smaller fixed quality of an HDR source where no encode keeps the range.
* 8-bit SDR lower variants beside a copy of an HDR, 10-bit or Dolby Vision source in Auto (<<lower-variant-range>>): the playlist would mix ranges.
A TV would drop to SDR whenever the player switched down, and Auto would give HDR up without a format error.
* No lower variants beside any copy of an HDR or 10-bit source: the copy would give the player no lower variant when the viewer's bandwidth drops (ADR 0050).
A worker whose encoder writes the copy's range and bit depth can encode one.
* Lower variants in the codec family that the codec preference ranks first, beside a copy of another codec family (<<lower-variant-range>>): players that move only within one codec family would leave the copy when the bandwidth falls and never return to it.
* A copy only in Original, with Auto encoding in the negotiated codec: Auto would encode a source that the client plays as it is, and spend worker capacity and picture quality on it.
* Lower variants in the compatible base's range beside a Dolby Vision copy, now: players that move only within one codec family read them as a second ladder, and no TV was measured (<<lower-variant-range>>).
* Ending the copy, or changing format, when a lower variant fails (<<lower-variant-failure>>): the copy still plays, and Auto could give the source's range up for a variant that the player may not play again.
* Continuing a lower variant on a software encoder after its hardware encoder fails, as ADR 0044 continues other variants: that continuation would also replace the stream session, so the player would reload the copy as after a withdrawal, and the four failures of a lower variant would no longer follow one rule (<<lower-variant-failure>>).
* Keeping the stream session after a lower variant fails and withdrawing the lower variants inside it (<<lower-variant-failure>>): both clients would need changes, and the `404 Not Found` answers for the withdrawn variants would need an exception to link:0019-recoverable-jit-hls-segment-delivery.adoc[ADR 0019]'s rule that `503 Service Unavailable` is the only way that the server refuses an advertised segment.
Without the client changes, the web client's player froze for 143 s on a link slower than the copy, and the Apple client waits 30 s after its own report and then fails (<<evidence-encode-range>>).
* Writing down the order that the server on `main` follows, in which a 10-bit SDR encode comes before an 8-bit H.264 encode and before no other 8-bit encode (<<encode-range>>): whether the client declares H.264 would then decide between 8-bit AV1 and 10-bit HEVC, although the H.264 encode plays in neither case.
* Keeping a hardware codec family's SDR encode before a PQ or HLG encode on a software encoder, as ADR 0009's order alone would: a TV would leave HDR on a server whose hardware encoder writes only 8-bit SDR, although a software encoder of another codec family or another worker could keep the range (<<encode-range>>).
* Planning the range and the bit depth again from the software encoders after a hardware encoder fails: a PQ encode would continue as 8-bit SDR with no format error and no counted format attempt, also in a fixed quality with HDR chosen (<<hardware-failure-range>>).
* Keeping a copy that the playlist lists alone when the copy does not fit the upload budget: the copy would take upload that every counted stream session shares, although an encode that fits can play (<<lower-variant-range>>).
* Keeping only the failed worker's hardware encoder out of the later format attempts: the server chooses an encoder by codec family and encoder backend, so a decision cannot name a worker to avoid (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
* Letting the failed hardware encoder back into the playback session once the worker offers it again: a worker keeps offering a hardware encoder that refused at its driver's session limit, so Auto could go straight back to that encoder (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
* Letting Auto go back to the failed hardware encoder when no worker has a software encoder of its codec family: that encoder can refuse again at its driver's session limit (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
* Refusing a client whose only codec family has no software encoder on any worker because no video format is eligible: the viewer would read advice to try another device, although the client's declaration did not cause the refusal (<<failed-hardware-encoder>>).
* Preparing an SDR output beside an HDR encode, to keep another format ready: the variants of the selected format start on demand, and another format starts only after a format error (<<encode-range>>).
* A PQ or HLG H.264 encode: <<encode-range>> plans PQ and HLG at 10 bits only, and almost no device decodes 10-bit H.264 (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/397[streamarr/streamarr-server#397]).
Firefox refused High 10 in 21 of 21 runs (<<evidence-sdr-output>>).
* Refusing a planned copy whose kept Dolby Vision holds an `UNSPECIFIED` RPU observation: every profile 8.2 copy of a probe version 4 result would then fail, although the server copies those today.
* Showing the list of refused properties on the stream session in GraphQL now: no client has a media information screen yet, and a pre-production contract can add the field later without a new version.

== Evidence

Unless a measurement names another date and version, every measurement used the pinned worker image with FFmpeg 8.1.2 on a 10-core host.
The encoders were libx264 and SVT-AV1.
One level check also used libx265.
The players were hls.js 1.7.0 in Chromium, Chrome, Firefox and WebKit, plus WebKit's native player and AVPlayer on macOS 26.6.2.
These six player setups ran headless.
No headless player ran with an HDR display.
The sources covered HDR10, HDR10+, HLG from an iPhone, Dolby Vision profiles 5, 7 (base layer), 8.1, 8.2, 8.4 and 9, 10-bit SDR with an untagged transfer characteristic, and 8-bit SDR.

[[evidence-sdr-output]]
=== 8-bit SDR output

* Before this amendment, the worker encoded every 10-bit HEVC source to H.264 High 10 (13 of 13).
* Firefox's `MediaSource.isTypeSupported` returned false for `avc1.6E00xx` in 21 of 21 runs.
hls.js therefore dropped the variant.
* WebKit's native player and AVPlayer failed PQ-tagged High 10 in 16 of 16 runs with `CoreMediaErrorDomain -12927`.
The PQ transfer characteristic in a variant that the playlist declared SDR triggered the failure.
Converting to 8 bits alone therefore does not fix the failure.
* All 14 tone-mapped outputs (7 sources, 2 encoders) were 8-bit 4:2:0.
All 14 used BT.709 color in limited range.
None carried the static HDR boxes `mdcv` and `clli` or a Dolby Vision configuration record.
* The mean chroma offsets (Cb and Cr) of the HDR10 and HLG encodes were measured against Apple's own SDR rendering of the same source.
The offsets of an HDR10 encode moved from −32 and −12 before tone mapping to −7.3 and −3.1 after tone mapping.
The offsets of tone-mapped HLG stayed within 1.5 code values.
The Dolby Vision profile 5 offsets were measured against the rendering of the Dolby Vision profile 8.1 version of the same master.
These offsets moved from −35 and −44 to −9.5 and −11.3.
The Dolby Vision profile 5 teal cast disappeared.
* The tone-mapped libx264 output played from every source in Chromium, Chrome, WebKit with hls.js, WebKit's native player and AVPlayer.
Firefox played 6 of 7.
Firefox's one failure matched a separate, intermittent Firefox failure on H.264 with muxed AAC.
That separate failure also occurs without tone mapping.
* The following sentences give libx264's cost in CPU seconds per media second at the 1080p variant, before and after this amendment's change.
From a 1080p HDR10 source, the cost fell from 9.11 to 6.07.
From a 2160p Dolby Vision profile 5 source, the cost fell from 12.60 to 10.19.
From an iPhone HLG source, the cost fell from 15.26 to 7.52.
SVT-AV1 cost about the same as before.

[[evidence-tone-mapping]]
=== Tone mapping

* On 1080p PQ and HLG sources, tone mapping cost at most 0.1 CPU seconds per media second more than a plain 8-bit conversion.
Reshaping Dolby Vision profile 5 added about 1.1 to 1.2 CPU seconds per media second at 1080p60.
* Against the Dolby Vision profile 8.1 version of the same master, the reshaped Dolby Vision profile 5 encode scored 32.7 dB peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR).
Without the reshape, the Dolby Vision profile 5 encode scored 14.8 dB.
* The tone-mapping filter treats an untagged input as PQ.
On a 10-bit SDR source with an untagged transfer characteristic, the filter gave a mean luma of 41 instead of 106.
* With tone mapping, one 1080p variant that libx264 encoded at its default preset, `medium`, ran at 1.20–1.43× real time from 1080p60 HDR sources.
The same variant ran at 0.90–1.01× real time from 2160p60 sources.
A four-variant ladder from 60 fps HDR sources ran at 0.35–0.56× real time, with or without tone mapping.
* The bt2390 curve at 1000 nits mapped a 983-nit PQ gray to luma 235.
The bt2390 curve mapped a 10,000-nit gray to luma 168.
The mobius curve clips at 235 instead of folding.
* Below the peak, mobius and bt2390 gave the same brightness (mean luma 92.9 and 92.1).
The hable curve gave a mean luma of 76.9.
* The color, player and seek checks ran with the bt2390 curve.
Mobius was compared only for brightness.
The tone-mapping work (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/26[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#26]) therefore repeats the color and player checks with the mobius curve.

<<lower-variant-range>> and <<lower-variant-failure>> record draft https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/pull/759[streamarr/streamarr-server#759] at its head `d0985e626`, read on 2026-11-11.
The replacement of the stream session records the same pull request at its head `10188f6fa`, read on 2026-11-13.
The figures below come from that pull request's description; the test names and counts at `d0985e626` were checked against its source.

* Its `LowerVariantsInTheCopysRangeTest` holds 24 test methods, 7 of them parameterized.
They cover the lower variants' range, bit depth and codec family, the codec profile and the capability entry, the Dolby Vision copy listed alone, an AV1 copy, and the codec preference of a stream session without a copy.
They also cover the withdrawal after each of the four failures, the copy's own failure, and a report after a withdrawal.
* Against the production code before the codec family and failure rules, 15 of that class's 32 cases failed: 9 for the codec family and 6 for a lower variant's failure.
* The pull request fed playlists shaped like the server's to the web client's player, at the version that streamarr-web pins, in a script with no media and no browser.
A copy and lower variants of one codec family and one range moved both ways.
Lower variants in another codec family, and a Dolby Vision copy above lower variants without Dolby Vision, were a one-way ladder: the player left the copy when the measured bandwidth fell and did not return to it.
In such a ladder, when the copy's `BANDWIDTH` was above 5 Mb/s, the player never played the copy.
* A review ran the web client's player in a headless browser against a local server that answered as head `d0985e626` does, once per case.
At that head the stream session stayed after a withdrawal, and every request for a withdrawn variant answered `404 Not Found`.
The synthetic H.264 source had a copy at 8 Mb/s and lower variants at 1.5, 0.8 and 0.4 Mb/s, in 6 s segments.
On a link faster than the copy, a withdrawal cost at most three immediate `404 Not Found` answers and no stall.
On a 3 Mb/s link, playback froze for 143 s, until the run ended, while the copy alone on the same link stalled about 10.5 s per segment but advanced.
After nine `404 Not Found` answers for withdrawn variants, one `503 Service Unavailable` on the copy ended playback with a fatal error.
When the upload budget left the copy out, the player stopped with a fatal error 13 s after the withdrawal.
* A second review ran hls.js 1.7.4 with the web client's configuration in a headless browser against a stand-in server that replaces the stream session as head `10188f6fa` does.
The synthetic source had 6 s segments, a copy at 5 Mb/s and lower variants at 3, 1.5 and 0.8 Mb/s, and each case ran once for 170 s.
Playback continued in every run, with no fatal error.
Each replacement threw away 19 to 25 s of buffered video and took 7.7 to 23.1 s to play again.
On a 3 Mb/s link, playback reached 91.0 s into the video after 170 s.
The copy alone on the same link reached 93.4 s, and a run without a failure reached 162.0 s.
The player spent 3.5 to 13 s of each replacement on the copy's first segment, because the web client gives the player no start position.
* No Apple device and no TV was measured.
The Apple client, read in source, waits 30 s for another stream session after its report and then fails when the answer names the same stream session.
After a replacement, the answer names the new stream session, and the Apple client, read in source, loads the new stream session at the failed position.
How the Apple client's player answers a `404 Not Found` for a media segment is unmeasured.
* No test covers a copy that the playlist lists alone and that does not fit the upload budget.
The tests of the HDR encodes run with the upload budget off.
