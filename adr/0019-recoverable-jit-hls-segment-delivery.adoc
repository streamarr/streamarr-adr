= ADR 0019: Recoverable Just-In-Time HLS Segment Delivery

== Status

Accepted

This ADR narrows the "RFC 8216 compliance" consequence of link:0008-server-generated-hls-playlists.adoc[ADR 0008] and refines link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018] by un-deferring its request-driven recovery slice while leaving 0018's multi-control-plane failover and durable job recovery deferred.

This ADR replaces the eager start of every variant that the playback capabilities epic (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/399[streamarr/streamarr-server#399]) describes.
Only the requested variant starts or restarts until the headroom policy of link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] allows extra variants.
Its two-segment relocation gap depends on the decode-only early-start seek that link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] will record.

=== Implementation status

Implemented.
`SegmentDeliveryCoordinator` owns the liveness-and-progress-bounded wait and the bounded replacement budget; `ProducerLifecycleService` owns every producer mutation under the per-session mutex, with the attempt fence at the replacement write (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/252[issue #252]).
Worker-side upload back-pressure retry and the worker's stall-bounded segment wait are tracked in https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/1[worker issue #1, transferred from streamarr-server issue #253].
The requested-variant start and the two-segment relocation gap are not implemented.
The server still starts every variant together, and it relocates only when the segment 24 s before the requested one has not been produced.

== Context

Streamarr transcodes on-demand library files and serves them as HLS, generating the media playlist from the source's probed duration rather than from produced output.
The playlist advertises every segment up front and closes with `EXT-X-ENDLIST`, while FFmpeg produces those segments lazily behind it.
A request for an advertised-but-unproduced segment currently waits on a fixed wall-clock budget and then returns `404`, and a failed transcode is not recovered: the outbound-worker path abandons the variant with no re-dispatch, and the player cannot distinguish a permanently dead segment from one that is merely slow.

RFC 8216 §6.2.1 requires that any segment listed in a playlist be immediately downloadable, and forbids removing or altering a segment once it has been advertised.
Advertising the whole timeline up front is the price of instant start and arbitrary seek; it is an intentional deviation from §6.2.1's immediate-availability requirement, and it converts every advertised segment into a standing obligation to attempt recovery and deliver.
A listed segment cannot later be withdrawn, gapped, or substituted; exhausting a finite recovery budget is a failure to fulfill the obligation, not another valid representation of it.
Robust playback under that obligation is therefore not optional polish; having promised every segment, the server must be able to produce any of them.

== Decision

=== The advertised playlist is a delivery obligation

On-demand media playlists continue to advertise every segment computed from source duration, close with `EXT-X-ENDLIST`, and embed each segment's own seek offset in its URI so any segment is independently re-producible.
A listed segment is an obligation, not a hint: the server never un-advertises, gaps, or substitutes an advertised segment and normally fulfills the obligation by recovering a producer and delivering the segment bytes.
When every eligible execution target has been attempted once in the current recovery cycle and none can produce the segment, the controller returns `503 Service Unavailable`.
That terminal response reports failure to fulfill the advertised representation and gives the player a clear boundary at which to switch variants or stop; it is not treated as successful HLS delivery.
The advertise-ahead deviation from §6.2.1 is intentional and is recorded here rather than hidden.

=== Segment waits are bounded by producer liveness, not a wall clock

A request for an advertised-but-unproduced segment waits while a producer for that segment is alive and making progress; a producer that publishes nothing within the configured stall threshold is classified stalled and replaced.
A fresh producer's cold start (seek plus first-segment encode) receives the configured stall threshold plus one target-segment duration; after the first publication, the steady-state stall threshold applies by itself.
The local store writes every segment completely to a temporary file before moving it into its final name. It requests an atomic replacement, but falls back to a same-filesystem replacement when the filesystem does not support atomic moves. Under the segment-store contract, only the final name is observable as ready; supported deployments must therefore provide rename visibility consistent with that contract. No successor-segment inference is needed or permitted.
The wait ends when the segment is ready, when no producer can be made to produce it, or on request cancellation — never on a fixed wall-clock timeout.
A slow or resource-starved transcode must not surface to the client as a missing segment.

=== Producers are recovered on demand, driven by the request

When an advertised segment is requested and no live producer covers it, the server starts a fresh producer seeking to that segment; recovery is pull-based, triggered by the segment request, not a supervised push loop.
A recovered producer emits a contiguous run of segments from the requested index and never a single isolated segment, so audio and video boundaries stay continuous.
Presentation timestamps continue the absolute timeline across a replacement producer (`-copyts`, input seek, `-start_number`); MPEG-TS continuity counters reset at the replacement boundary — an accepted, documented deviation from RFC 8216 §3's uninterrupted-counter requirement, shared with comparable JIT servers, because a static playlist cannot signal a discontinuity after the fact.
Seeking uses this same mechanism: a relocation restarts a producer at the requested offset, and because segment numbering derives from the offset the playlist never changes.
A relocation is a planned restart, not recovery: it revives even a variant whose recovery has been exhausted.
<<decision-relocation-gap>> defines when a request relocates.

[[decision-requested-variant]]
=== Only the requested variant starts or restarts

A variant is one quality level of the stream session's ladder, RFC 8216's Variant Stream (ADR 0018).
The requested variant is the variant whose media segment the player has just requested.
A stream session starts a variant's producer only when the player requests one of that variant's media segments.
That producer starts at the requested segment.
A relocation stops every producer of the stream session and restarts only the requested variant.
Each other variant starts again when the player next requests one of its media segments.
No variant starts before the player requests it, unless the headroom policy of link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] allows it.
That policy starts extra variants only while every running variant stays at about 1.35× real time or faster.
It also leaves room for other users' encodes, so one session's extra variants never slow another session's requested variant.

*Why:* Starting a ladder's four variants together made every variant about 3× slower than starting it alone.
The 1080p variant then produced 0.94–1.08 media seconds per wall second, against 2.95–2.99 alone.
A player that adapts to measured throughput keeps a variant only while the server produces it at about 1.35× real time or faster.
Below that speed, hls.js abandoned 1080p 12–16 times a session and showed mostly 360p on a 25 Mb/s link.
Restarting only the requested variant kept 1080p for 76–83 % of playback in Firefox with hls.js and in WebKit's native player, and 100 % in AVPlayer.
Evidence: <<evidence-requested-variant>>.

[[decision-relocation-gap]]
=== The relocation gap is two media segments

A relocation is a planned restart of the requested variant's producer at the requested media segment.
The relocation gap is how far a request may lie ahead of a running producer's output before the server restarts rather than waits.
A request relocates when the requested segment lies before the running attempt's first segment.
A request also relocates when the segment one relocation gap before it has not been produced.
Once the decode-only early-start seek ships, the relocation gap is 2 media segments: 12 s at 6 s segments.
ADR 0037 will record that seek, which also removes the junction gap that https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/44[streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker#44] describes.
Until then the gap stays at today's 24 s, 4 segments at 6 s segments.
The 2-segment gap was measured with the new seek's restart times, not with today's seek.

*Why:* Waiting for a running producer and restarting a new one cost about the same when the request lies a few media seconds ahead.
With the decode-only early-start seek, a fresh 1080p attempt completed its first segment in 2.9–3.0 s, against 5.1–5.2 s with today's seek.
The difference is the media encoded before the first segment: about 7 s instead of 13 s at 6 s segments.
A running 1080p producer delivered about 3 media seconds per wall second.
The extra wait is how much longer a viewer waits than the better of waiting and restarting.
With that seek, a gap of 2 segments had the smallest worst-case extra wait over four variants: 0.9 s for 1080p alone.
The 24 s gap made the same viewer wait 4.9–5.0 s longer than a restart would have taken.
A gap of 1 segment measured within 0.9 s of 2 segments, but it also restarts whenever a player requests the next segment while the current one is still in production.
Evidence: <<evidence-relocation-gap>>.

=== Distributed producers are the recovery redundancy

When the transcode executor is distributed, a variant whose producer fails is re-dispatched, on the next request that needs it, to another available worker for the same source namespace; the pool of workers is the recovery redundancy.
Recovery is attempt-bounded, never wall-clock-bounded: each variant carries a replacement budget sized by the currently eligible execution targets — locally the single local runner, remotely each live worker connection (its `worker_session_id`, per ADR 0018's live-connection fence).
Every replacement consults the live target set at dispatch time; no snapshot of fleet membership is retained across the recovery window.
Publication progress clears the attempted-target log (the frontier advancing is the one success signal). Fleet membership is re-derived on every pass; changing it does not clear an active window, but a currently eligible target not yet attempted in a failed window can revive recovery.
Only when the budget is exhausted does the failure surface, as a terminal `503 Service Unavailable` with no body and no `Retry-After`; the client's own retry budget and its adaptive-bitrate switch to a healthy variant are the last-resort recovery.
The terminal state is sticky for same-window retries and cleared by exactly two events: a currently eligible execution target that was never attempted in the failed window (a reconnecting worker is a new target by construction), or a genuine seek's planned restart.
Within one recovery window, each live target is tried at most once; departed targets stop mattering and newly appearing targets can be tried without resetting the attempted-target log.
Attempt identity is a write-point fence, not per-request state: `ProducerLifecycleService`'s mutex-guarded predicate compares the expected attempt exactly once, at the replacement write, so a late or superseded replacement can never clobber a newer producer's state.
Worst-case pre-terminal latency is `budget × stall threshold` plus dispatch overhead; in a large worker pool that can exceed one viewer's patience, which is accepted — every attempt is a genuine recovery chance and the player's variant switch proceeds in parallel.
Transient upload back-pressure is a self-classifying condition: the producer retries the affected segment with bounded back-off rather than failing the whole variant (worker-side retry tracked in https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/1[worker issue #1, transferred from streamarr-server issue #253]; until it lands, a back-pressure failure consumes one replacement attempt).

=== A playlist structure suite gates the advertised representation

An HLS conformance suite maintained in this repository runs inside the standard build and the continuous-integration gate, asserting playlist structure and the delivery obligations above against generated playlists and a controllable segment-producing fake.
Intentional deviations from the standard — advertise-ahead first among them — are recorded in this ADR and exercised by the relevant playlist and delivery tests, so the deviation remains reviewable rather than tribal knowledge.
A deeper platform-specific stream validator remains an optional local check outside the portable gate.

* Explicitly deferred:
** cross-worker session affinity, durable job recovery, and multiple control-plane replicas — deferred by ADR 0018 and unchanged here;
** low-latency HLS (partial segments, blocking playlist reload, preload hints, delta playlist updates, rendition reports) — it targets live-edge latency, presumes a growing playlist, and is incompatible with the on-demand static-playlist contract; it would be revisited only if true live streaming is introduced, under its own ADR;
** a growing or event-type media playlist for on-demand content;
** object-store delivery and any worker-to-client segment path;
** starting extra variants before the player requests them, until ADR 0044's headroom model is validated;
** how the server answers a request for the initialization segment of a variant that has no producer yet, since the measurements served every variant's initialization segment at once; and
** the relocation gap at segment lengths other than 6 s, which were not measured.

== Consequences

The static-playlist obligation makes recovery mandatory rather than optional, and the decision states that obligation as a first-class contract.
Liveness-bounded waits remove the spurious-timeout failure mode under load and slow start for both local and distributed execution.
Pull-based recovery makes a failed producer invisible to the viewer in the common case: a brief stall while a fresh producer catches up; `503 Service Unavailable` appears only after the finite recovery cycle is exhausted.
The active playlist structure suite converts structural protocol assumptions into always-on checks; the delivery suite lands with the recovery implementation tracked by issue 252.
ADR 0008's blanket "RFC 8216 compliance" consequence is narrowed: server-generated playlists remain fully controlled, but on-demand delivery intentionally deviates from §6.2.1 immediate-availability as recorded here.
The costs are added producer-lifecycle and liveness logic in the streaming layer, per-request liveness evaluation on the segment path, continuity validation across producer boundaries, and additional build and continuous-integration time for the protocol suites.
Starting only the requested variant has its own costs.
A switch to a variant that is not running waits for that variant's first segment: 0.8–3.0 s when it runs alone.
WebKit's native player started in 3.5 s instead of 2.0 s, and its first seek took 10.7 s instead of 6.6 s, because no lower variant was running to fall back on.
These costs are accepted, because every other measured player held a higher variant and started sooner.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Growing or event-type media playlist advertising only produced segments — satisfies §6.2.1 but forfeits instant whole-timeline seek, makes segment production stateful, and complicates recovery for no viewer-visible gain.
* Low-latency HLS blocking playlist reload as the segment-wait mechanism — presumes a growing live playlist, does not apply to a static on-demand playlist, and specifies no error path for a segment that cannot be produced.
* A fixed wall-clock segment-wait timeout returning `404` before liveness-driven recovery — converts slow production into spurious failures and papers over the missing recovery instead of providing it.
* Signaling a dead variant immediately through a distinct HTTP status or an advertised playlist gap — the standard forbids withdrawing a listed segment and the client cannot reconstruct or skip an advertised segment URL. A terminal `503 Service Unavailable` is reserved for finite recovery exhaustion; the playlist remains immutable.
* Per-segment isolated transcoding on demand — independent single-segment encodes glitch at audio and video boundaries; a contiguous run per producer is required.
* A per-window snapshot of eligible execution targets — the snapshot is a retained copy of live fleet state that must be defended against every concurrent mutation (supersession fences, exhausted-window retention, new-target set-difference); review found two high-severity staleness-of-the-copy defects in exactly that machinery, while live consultation admits newly eligible targets without retaining a second copy of fleet membership.
* Producer lifetime driven by subscription-style demand handles (a producer runs only while a delivery holds a handle) — stateless HLS clients hold no standing connection to hang a handle on; last-request-time reaping is the honest demand signal for HTTP delivery.
* Starting every variant of the ladder together at session start and on every relocation — each variant ran about 3× slower, hls.js cycled between 1080p and 360p, and AVPlayer took about 19 s to become ready to play.
* Starting the requested variant's ladder neighbours once its first segment exists — the neighbours took the requested variant's CPU, so 720p fell from 5.45× to 2.04× real time and hls.js rarely switched up to 1080p.
* Starting extra variants whenever a worker has a free slot — a free slot says nothing about the CPU left, and four software encodes on four slots each ran too slowly to hold.
* Keeping the 24 s relocation gap after the decode-only early-start seek ships — at its edge, a viewer waits 4.9–5.0 s longer than a restart takes.
* A push-based media transport (Media over QUIC) in place of HLS — dissolves the wait-for-segment inference class entirely, but clients speak HLS; per the settled transport-experiment rule this belongs on a spike branch, revisited when WebTransport reaches consumer playback devices.

== Evidence

The measurements behind the requested-variant start and the relocation gap ran from 2026-09-25 to 2026-09-28.
They used the pinned worker image, libx264 at its then-default `medium` preset, 6 s segments and a 1080p, 720p, 480p and 360p ladder.
The host had 10 cores.
The sources were two openly licensed test films.

[[evidence-requested-variant]]
=== Starting every variant together

* Four variants started together took about 3× as long to complete their first segment as each started alone; the paired ratios were 2.78–3.22.
A 1080p seek completed its first segment in 8.4–9.0 s instead of 2.9–3.0 s.
An independent re-run measured paired ratios of 2.95–3.22.
* One variant alone already used 6.9–8.7 of the 10 cores, and four together used 9.8–9.9.
* While all four ran, 1080p produced 0.94–1.08 media seconds per wall second, against 2.95–2.99 alone.
* hls.js 1.7.0 keeps a variant only while 0.95 × production speed × bitrate is at least the variant's `BANDWIDTH` (derived from its source).
For the measured ladder that means a production speed of about 1.33–1.37× real time.
* Four players ran 180 s sessions against a simulated server with the measured speeds: hls.js in Chromium and Firefox, WebKit's native player, and AVPlayer on macOS.
Each session sought twice, and its link dropped from 25 Mb/s to 2.5 Mb/s after 100 s.
* With every variant running, hls.js made 12–16 emergency aborts a session and showed 1080p for 0–10 % of playback before the drop.
AVPlayer became ready to play after 18.9–19.2 s.
* With only the requested variant running, 1080p held for 38–82 % of playback in Chromium with hls.js, 76–82 % in Firefox with hls.js, 82–83 % in WebKit and 100 % in AVPlayer.
AVPlayer became ready after 3.4 s, and after 5.2 s in the independent re-run.
* Starting the neighbours of the requested variant gave hls.js 1080p for 6–13 % of playback and made WebKit's first seek 14.2 s.

[[evidence-relocation-gap]]
=== The relocation gap

* The decode-only early-start seek completed a replacement attempt's first 1080p segment in 2.92–2.99 s, against 5.14–5.20 s for today's one-period-early seek.
It encoded 7.05 s of media before that segment was complete, against 13.01 s.
* A running 1080p producer delivered 2.96–2.99 media seconds per wall second.
Waiting for it cost about (6.5 s + d) ÷ 3, where d is how far the request lies ahead of its last delivered fragment.
* With the decode-only seek, the worst extra wait at the gap's edge was measured for gaps of 1 to 4 segments.
For 1080p alone it was 1.1–1.2 s at 1 segment, 0.9 s at 2, 2.9–3.0 s at 3 and 4.9–5.0 s at 4 (24 s).
With all four variants running it was 3.3 s, 2.4–3.1 s, 8.1–9.5 s and 13.7–15.9 s.
* An independent re-analysis found 1 and 2 segments within 0.9 s of each other in both directions.
It confirmed that both stay far below the 24 s gap's 13–17 s with all four variants running.
* A comparable just-in-time server uses the same 24 s rule: it restarts only when a request lies more than 8 of its 3 s segments past its last written segment.
