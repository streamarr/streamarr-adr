= ADR 0019: Recoverable Just-In-Time HLS Segment Delivery

== Status

Accepted

This ADR narrows the "RFC 8216 compliance" consequence of link:0008-server-generated-hls-playlists.adoc[ADR 0008] and refines link:0018-live-playback-authority-and-outbound-workers.adoc[ADR 0018] by un-deferring its request-driven recovery slice while leaving 0018's multi-control-plane failover and durable job recovery deferred.

The playback capabilities epic, https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/399[streamarr/streamarr-server#399], describes starting every variant eagerly.
This ADR replaces that eager start.
For now, variants start on demand.
<<decision-requested-variant>> gives the rules for when each variant starts.
The headroom model of link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] decides when extra variants may start early.
Extra variants are variants that the player has not requested.
This ADR sets the relocation gap to two media segments.
The relocation gap is how far a requested media segment may lie ahead of a running job attempt's output before the server starts a new job attempt at the requested segment.
The two-segment relocation gap depends on the decode-only early-start seek that link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] specifies.
With the decode-only early-start seek, a job attempt reads from the source keyframe that the server sends.
The job attempt then decodes the media before its first segment but does not encode that media.

=== Implementation status

Implemented.
`SegmentDeliveryCoordinator` owns the liveness-and-progress-bounded wait and the bounded replacement budget; `ProducerLifecycleService` owns every producer mutation under the per-session mutex, with the attempt fence at the replacement write (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/252[issue #252]).
Worker-side upload back-pressure retry and the worker's stall-bounded segment wait are tracked in https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/1[worker issue #1, transferred from streamarr-server issue #253].
The recovery rules are implemented.
The server does not yet start variants on demand.
The server does not yet use the two-segment relocation gap (https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/502[streamarr/streamarr-server#502]).
The server still starts every variant together.
The server still relocates forward when the segment 24 s before the requested segment has not been produced.

== Context

Streamarr transcodes on-demand library files and serves them as HLS, generating the media playlist from the source's probed duration rather than from produced output.
The playlist advertises every segment up front and closes with `EXT-X-ENDLIST`, while FFmpeg produces those segments lazily behind it.
A request for an advertised-but-unproduced segment currently waits on a fixed wall-clock budget and then returns `404`, and a failed transcode is not recovered: the outbound-worker path abandons the variant with no re-dispatch, and the player cannot distinguish a permanently dead segment from one that is merely slow.

RFC 8216 §6.2.1 requires that any segment listed in a playlist be immediately downloadable, and forbids removing or altering a segment once it has been advertised.
Advertising the whole timeline up front is the price of instant start and arbitrary seek; it is an intentional deviation from §6.2.1's immediate-availability requirement, and it converts every advertised segment into a standing obligation to attempt recovery and deliver.
A listed segment cannot later be withdrawn, gapped, or substituted; exhausting a finite recovery budget is a failure to fulfill the obligation, not another valid representation of it.
Robust playback under that obligation is therefore not optional polish; having promised every segment, the server must be able to produce any of them.

== Decision

=== The advertised playlist is a delivery obligation

On-demand media playlists continue to advertise every segment computed from source duration, close with `EXT-X-ENDLIST`, and embed each segment's own seek offset in its URI so any segment is independently re-producible.
A listed segment is an obligation, not a hint: the server never un-advertises, gaps, or substitutes an advertised segment and normally fulfills the obligation by recovering a producer and delivering the segment bytes.
When every eligible execution target has been attempted once in the current recovery cycle and none can produce the segment, the controller returns `503 Service Unavailable`.
That terminal response reports failure to fulfill the advertised representation and gives the player a clear boundary at which to switch variants or stop; it is not treated as successful HLS delivery.
The advertise-ahead deviation from §6.2.1 is intentional and is recorded here rather than hidden.

=== Segment waits are bounded by producer liveness, not a wall clock

A request for an advertised-but-unproduced segment waits while a producer for that segment is alive and making progress; a producer that publishes nothing within the configured stall threshold is classified stalled and replaced.
A fresh producer's cold start (seek plus first-segment encode) receives the configured stall threshold plus one target-segment duration; after the first publication, the steady-state stall threshold applies by itself.
The local store writes every segment completely to a temporary file before moving it into its final name. It requests an atomic replacement, but falls back to a same-filesystem replacement when the filesystem does not support atomic moves. Under the segment-store contract, only the final name is observable as ready; supported deployments must therefore provide rename visibility consistent with that contract. No successor-segment inference is needed or permitted.
The wait ends when the segment is ready, when no producer can be made to produce it, or on request cancellation — never on a fixed wall-clock timeout.
A slow or resource-starved transcode must not surface to the client as a missing segment.

=== Producers are recovered on demand, driven by the request

When an advertised segment is requested and no live producer covers it, the server starts a fresh producer seeking to that segment; recovery is pull-based, triggered by the segment request, not a supervised push loop.
A recovered producer emits a contiguous run of segments from the requested index and never a single isolated segment, so audio and video boundaries stay continuous.
Presentation timestamps continue the absolute timeline across a replacement producer (`-copyts`, input seek, `-start_number`); MPEG-TS continuity counters reset at the replacement boundary — an accepted, documented deviation from RFC 8216 §3's uninterrupted-counter requirement, shared with comparable JIT servers, because a static playlist cannot signal a discontinuity after the fact.
When a player seeks, the server uses the same request-driven mechanism.
A relocation is a planned restart of the requested variant's job at the requested media segment.
A relocation starts a new job attempt at the requested segment's offset.
Each segment's number derives from that segment's offset, so the playlist never changes.
A relocation is a planned restart, not recovery.
A relocation revives even a variant whose recovery has been exhausted.
<<decision-relocation-gap>> defines when the server relocates.

[[decision-requested-variant]]
=== Variants start on demand

A variant is one quality level that the stream session offers.
A variant is what RFC 8216 calls a Variant Stream, as ADR 0018 records.
The ladder is the stream session's set of variants.
The requested variant is the variant whose media segment the player has just requested.
The first-listed variant is the variant that the multivariant playlist lists first.
The current segment is the first media segment of the stream session's most recently started job attempt.
Before any job attempt starts, the current segment is the media segment at which the stream session starts.

The server starts and restarts variants by these rules:

* Creating a stream session starts the first-listed variant.
* A request for the initialization segment of a variant that has no producer starts that variant at the current segment.
* A request for a media segment of a variant that has no producer starts that variant at the requested segment.
* A relocation restarts only the requested variant at the requested segment.
* After a relocation, a variant that is not running starts when the player next requests one of that variant's segments.
* Apart from the first-listed variant, no variant starts before the player requests one of that variant's segments, unless the headroom model of link:0044-encoder-headroom-and-the-preset-scale.adoc[ADR 0044] allows that variant to start early.
* Until the headroom model is validated, the headroom model allows no variant to start early.

These rules hold for now.
The long-term goal is a rule based on capacity.
With enough encoder headroom, every variant may start eagerly.
Enough encoder headroom leaves room for several users' encodes at once.
Several workers or a strong hardware encoder can provide that headroom.
ADR 0044 owns the headroom model that decides when enough headroom exists.

*Why the first-listed variant starts at creation:* Low latency is a product priority.
Viewers want to see video fast.
A variant that starts at creation begins encoding before the player has fetched the playlists.

*Why an initialization request starts a variant:* A player requests a variant's initialization segment before any of that variant's media segments.
Starting the variant at the initialization request saves the time until the player's first media-segment request.

*Why other variants wait for a request:* Starting the four variants of the measured ladder together made every variant about 3× slower than starting that variant alone.
With all four variants running, the 1080p variant produced 0.94–1.08 media seconds per wall second.
Alone, the 1080p variant produced 2.95–2.99 media seconds per wall second.
For this ladder, hls.js keeps a variant that the player waits on only when the worker produces that variant at about 1.35× real time or faster.
ADR 0044 calls that speed the hold threshold.
The shared rate of 0.94–1.08 is below the hold threshold.
Below the hold threshold, hls.js abandoned 1080p 12–16 times a session.
hls.js showed mostly 360p on a 25 Mb/s link.
In each measured player session, the link dropped from 25 Mb/s to 2.5 Mb/s after 100 s.
When the server ran only the requested variant, Firefox with hls.js kept 1080p for 76–82 % of playback before the link dropped.
WebKit's native player kept 1080p for 82–83 % of playback before the link dropped.
AVPlayer kept 1080p for 100 % of playback before the link dropped.
Chromium with hls.js kept 1080p for 38–82 % of playback before the link dropped.
Evidence: <<evidence-requested-variant>>.

[[decision-relocation-gap]]
=== The relocation gap is two media segments

A relocation starts a new job attempt for the requested variant at the requested media segment.
The relocation gap is how far a request may lie ahead of a running job attempt's output before the server starts a new job attempt instead of waiting for the running one.
The server relocates when the requested segment lies before the first segment of the running job attempt.
The server also relocates when the segment one relocation gap before the requested segment has not been produced.
The relocation gap is counted in media segments.
Once the decode-only early-start seek ships, the relocation gap is 2 media segments at every segment length.
At 6 s segments, 2 media segments are 12 s.
At 3 s segments, 2 media segments are 6 s.
link:0037-worker-delivers-fmp4-segments-from-ffmpeg-stdout.adoc[ADR 0037] specifies the decode-only early-start seek for an encoded job attempt that starts after the first segment.
With that seek, the job attempt reads from the source keyframe that the server sends.
The job attempt then decodes the media before its first segment but does not encode that media.
The decode-only early-start seek also fixes a defect of the worker's current seek.
The worker's current seek uses FFmpeg's accurate seek.
FFmpeg's accurate seek drops the frame that a variable-frame-rate gap repeats.
The decode-only early-start seek reads from a keyframe without FFmpeg's accurate seek, so the job attempt keeps that frame.
https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-server/issues/502[streamarr/streamarr-server#502] implements the decode-only early-start seek and the two-segment relocation gap.
Until the decode-only early-start seek ships, the relocation gap stays at 24 s.
At 6 s segments, 24 s is 4 segments.
The relocation gap changes from 24 s to 2 segments in one step, when the decode-only early-start seek ships.
The 2-segment gap was measured with the restart times of the decode-only early-start seek.
The one-period-early seek is the seek that the production worker used while these measurements ran.
With the one-period-early seek, a job attempt starts encoding one segment duration before its first segment.
The 2-segment gap was not measured with the restart times of the one-period-early seek.

*Why:* A request can lie a few media seconds ahead of a running producer's output.
For such a request, waiting for that producer and restarting a new one cost about the same.
The measurements timed both seeks.
With the decode-only early-start seek, a fresh 1080p job attempt completed its first segment in 2.9–3.0 s.
With the one-period-early seek, a fresh 1080p job attempt took 5.1–5.2 s.
The time difference comes from the media that each job attempt encodes before its first segment.
At 6 s segments, a job attempt with the decode-only early-start seek encodes about 7 s of media.
A job attempt with the one-period-early seek encodes about 13 s.
A running 1080p job attempt alone delivered about 3 media seconds per wall second.
The extra wait is how much longer a viewer waits than under the faster of two choices.
The two choices are waiting for the running producer and restarting a new producer.
With the decode-only early-start seek, a gap of 2 segments had the smallest worst-case extra wait.
That result held for each of the four variants, both when that variant ran alone and when all four variants ran.
For 1080p alone, the worst-case extra wait at a gap of 2 segments was 0.9 s.
For 1080p alone, the 24 s gap made a viewer wait 4.9–5.0 s longer than a restart would have taken.
The worst-case extra wait at a gap of 1 segment was within 0.9 s of the worst-case extra wait at 2 segments.
A gap of 1 segment also makes the server relocate whenever a player requests the next segment while the running job attempt is still producing the current segment.
Evidence: <<evidence-relocation-gap>>.

=== Distributed producers are the recovery redundancy

When the transcode executor is distributed, a variant whose producer fails is re-dispatched, on the next request that needs it, to another available worker for the same source namespace; the pool of workers is the recovery redundancy.
Recovery is attempt-bounded, never wall-clock-bounded: each variant carries a replacement budget sized by the currently eligible execution targets — locally the single local runner, remotely each live worker connection (its `worker_session_id`, per ADR 0018's live-connection fence).
Every replacement consults the live target set at dispatch time; no snapshot of fleet membership is retained across the recovery window.
Publication progress clears the attempted-target log (the frontier advancing is the one success signal). Fleet membership is re-derived on every pass; changing it does not clear an active window, but a currently eligible target not yet attempted in a failed window can revive recovery.
Only when the budget is exhausted does the failure surface, as a terminal `503 Service Unavailable` with no body and no `Retry-After`; the client's own retry budget and its adaptive-bitrate switch to a healthy variant are the last-resort recovery.
The terminal state is sticky for same-window retries and cleared by exactly two events: a currently eligible execution target that was never attempted in the failed window (a reconnecting worker is a new target by construction), or a genuine seek's planned restart.
Within one recovery window, each live target is tried at most once; departed targets stop mattering and newly appearing targets can be tried without resetting the attempted-target log.
Attempt identity is a write-point fence, not per-request state: `ProducerLifecycleService`'s mutex-guarded predicate compares the expected attempt exactly once, at the replacement write, so a late or superseded replacement can never clobber a newer producer's state.
Worst-case pre-terminal latency is `budget × stall threshold` plus dispatch overhead; in a large worker pool that can exceed one viewer's patience, which is accepted — every attempt is a genuine recovery chance and the player's variant switch proceeds in parallel.
Transient upload back-pressure is a self-classifying condition: the producer retries the affected segment with bounded back-off rather than failing the whole variant (worker-side retry tracked in https://github.com/streamarr/streamarr-transcode-worker/issues/1[worker issue #1, transferred from streamarr-server issue #253]; until it lands, a back-pressure failure consumes one replacement attempt).

=== A playlist structure suite gates the advertised representation

An HLS conformance suite maintained in this repository runs inside the standard build and the continuous-integration gate, asserting playlist structure and the delivery obligations above against generated playlists and a controllable segment-producing fake.
Intentional deviations from the standard — advertise-ahead first among them — are recorded in this ADR and exercised by the relevant playlist and delivery tests, so the deviation remains reviewable rather than tribal knowledge.
A deeper platform-specific stream validator remains an optional local check outside the portable gate.

* Explicitly deferred:
** cross-worker session affinity, durable job recovery, and multiple control-plane replicas — deferred by ADR 0018 and unchanged here;
** low-latency HLS (partial segments, blocking playlist reload, preload hints, delta playlist updates, rendition reports) — it targets live-edge latency, presumes a growing playlist, and is incompatible with the on-demand static-playlist contract; it would be revisited only if true live streaming is introduced, under its own ADR;
** a growing or event-type media playlist for on-demand content;
** object-store delivery and any worker-to-client segment path; and
** starting extra variants before the player requests them, until ADR 0044's headroom model is validated.

== Consequences

The static-playlist obligation makes recovery mandatory rather than optional, and the decision states that obligation as a first-class contract.
Liveness-bounded waits remove the spurious-timeout failure mode under load and slow start for both local and distributed execution.
Pull-based recovery makes a failed producer invisible to the viewer in the common case: a brief stall while a fresh producer catches up; `503 Service Unavailable` appears only after the finite recovery cycle is exhausted.
The active playlist structure suite converts structural protocol assumptions into always-on checks; the delivery suite lands with the recovery implementation tracked by issue 252.
ADR 0008's blanket "RFC 8216 compliance" consequence is narrowed: server-generated playlists remain fully controlled, but on-demand delivery intentionally deviates from §6.2.1 immediate-availability as recorded here.
The costs are added producer-lifecycle and liveness logic in the streaming layer, per-request liveness evaluation on the segment path, continuity validation across producer boundaries, and additional build and continuous-integration time for the protocol suites.
Starting variants on demand has its own costs.
When a player switches to a variant that is not running, the player waits for that variant's first segment.
That wait was 0.8–3.0 s when the new variant ran alone.

Open question: WebKit's native player started and sought more slowly with only the requested variant running than with every variant running.
WebKit's native player started in 3.5 s instead of 2.0 s.
The first seek in WebKit's native player took 10.7 s instead of 6.6 s.
Both were slower because no lower variant was running for the player to fall back on.
Streamarr does not accept this cost yet.
Low latency is the goal, so Streamarr accepts the cost only if no alternative is left.
Streamarr is measuring the alternatives.
The alternatives include:

* the order of the variants in the multivariant playlist;
* starting the lowest variant beside the first-listed variant;
* shorter segments;
* the rule by which a player gives up on a slow segment and switches to a lower variant.

The measured sessions started no variant when the stream session was created.
Starting the first-listed variant at creation was not measured.
Starting a variant at its initialization request was not measured either.
The server currently lists the highest-quality variant first.
Creating a stream session therefore starts the most expensive encode.

== Rejected Alternatives

* Growing or event-type media playlist advertising only produced segments — satisfies §6.2.1 but forfeits instant whole-timeline seek, makes segment production stateful, and complicates recovery for no viewer-visible gain.
* Low-latency HLS blocking playlist reload as the segment-wait mechanism — presumes a growing live playlist, does not apply to a static on-demand playlist, and specifies no error path for a segment that cannot be produced.
* A fixed wall-clock segment-wait timeout returning `404` before liveness-driven recovery — converts slow production into spurious failures and papers over the missing recovery instead of providing it.
* Signaling a dead variant immediately through a distinct HTTP status or an advertised playlist gap — the standard forbids withdrawing a listed segment and the client cannot reconstruct or skip an advertised segment URL. A terminal `503 Service Unavailable` is reserved for finite recovery exhaustion; the playlist remains immutable.
* Per-segment isolated transcoding on demand — independent single-segment encodes glitch at audio and video boundaries; a contiguous run per producer is required.
* A per-window snapshot of eligible execution targets — the snapshot is a retained copy of live fleet state that must be defended against every concurrent mutation (supersession fences, exhausted-window retention, new-target set-difference); review found two high-severity staleness-of-the-copy defects in exactly that machinery, while live consultation admits newly eligible targets without retaining a second copy of fleet membership.
* Producer lifetime driven by subscription-style demand handles (a producer runs only while a delivery holds a handle) — stateless HLS clients hold no standing connection to hang a handle on; last-request-time reaping is the honest demand signal for HTTP delivery.
* Starting every variant of the ladder together at session start and on every relocation.
Each variant ran about 3× slower.
hls.js cycled between 1080p and 360p.
AVPlayer took about 19 s to become ready to play.
* Starting the requested variant's ladder neighbours once the requested variant's first segment exists.
The ladder neighbours are the variants next to the requested variant in the ladder.
The neighbours took CPU from the requested variant.
720p fell from 5.45× to 2.04× real time.
hls.js rarely switched up to 1080p.
* Starting extra variants whenever a worker has a free slot.
A free slot says nothing about how much CPU is left.
With four software encodes on four slots, the 1080p variant ran below the hold threshold.
* Keeping the 24 s relocation gap after the decode-only early-start seek ships.
For 1080p alone, when a request lies at the edge of the 24 s gap, a viewer waits 4.9–5.0 s longer than a restart takes.
* A push-based media transport (Media over QUIC) in place of HLS — dissolves the wait-for-segment inference class entirely, but clients speak HLS; per the settled transport-experiment rule this belongs on a spike branch, revisited when WebTransport reaches consumer playback devices.

== Evidence

The measurements behind starting only the requested variant and behind the relocation gap ran from 2026-09-25 to 2026-09-28.
The measurements ran FFmpeg inside the worker image that streamarr-server pinned at the time.
The encoder was libx264 at the `medium` preset, which was the worker's default preset then.
Segments were 6 s long.
No other segment length was measured.
The ladder had four variants: 1080p, 720p, 480p and 360p.
The host had 10 cores.
The sources were two openly licensed test films.

[[evidence-relocation-gap]]
=== The relocation gap

* With the decode-only early-start seek, a replacement attempt completed its first 1080p segment in 2.92–2.99 s.
With the one-period-early seek, a replacement attempt took 5.14–5.20 s.
* With the decode-only early-start seek, the replacement attempt encoded 7.05 s of media before its first segment was complete.
With the one-period-early seek, the replacement attempt encoded 13.01 s.
* A running 1080p job attempt alone delivered 2.96–2.99 media seconds per wall second.
Waiting for the running producer cost about (6.5 s + d) ÷ 3.
Here d is how far the request lies ahead of the producer's last delivered fragment.
* The extra wait is how much longer a viewer waits than under the faster of two choices.
The two choices are waiting for the running producer and restarting a new producer.
The edge of a gap is the farthest a request can lie ahead of the producer's output without a relocation.
With the decode-only early-start seek, the measurements found the worst extra wait at the edge of gaps of 1 to 4 segments.
The following table lists the worst extra wait for each gap.

[cols="1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
| Gap | 1080p alone | All four variants running
| 1 segment | 1.1–1.2 s | 3.3 s
| 2 segments | 0.9 s | 2.4–3.1 s
| 3 segments | 2.9–3.0 s | 8.1–9.5 s
| 4 segments, which is 24 s | 4.9–5.0 s | 13.7–15.9 s
|===

* An independent re-analysis compared the worst extra waits for gaps of 1 and 2 segments.
The re-analysis found that the two worst extra waits differ by at most 0.9 s, in either direction.
With all four variants running, the re-analysis found 13–17 s for the 24 s gap.
The re-analysis confirmed that the worst extra waits for gaps of 1 and 2 segments stay far below 13–17 s.
* A comparable just-in-time server uses the same 24 s rule as the current Streamarr server.
That server restarts only when a request lies more than 8 of its 3 s segments past the last segment it wrote.
